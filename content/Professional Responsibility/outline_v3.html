I. The Regulatory Frame

Chs. 1–3 · Week 1 · Where the rules come from, who can practice where, and all the guns pointed at you.

A. Sources of law [W1:1–2, 19–21]

  • States regulate lawyers (Leis v. Flynt). The ABA Model Rules bind no one on their own. Every state adopted a version, none verbatim. A state's adopted rule, as its high court reads it, controls discipline in that state. The MPRE tests the ABA text.
  • Persuasive only: ABA and state ethics opinions and the Restatement (Third) of the Law Governing Lawyers.
  • NEW Binding vs. persuasive [S1:27–28, 84–85]: state and county or city bar ethics committees (e.g., NYC, LA) issue opinions that are persuasive only. For "binding authority in New Jersey," only a New Jersey court opinion counts. An ABA Formal Opinion (e.g., Op. 492) and the Restatement don't. A Model Rule violation as such can't be the basis for discipline; the state's version of the Rule is (Atkinson MC).
  • NEW Other regimes have their own ethics rules: federal and state judges, law clerks and court staff, bankruptcy lawyers, and securities lawyers [S1:25].
  • History: 1908 ABA Canons (32 aspirational precepts); 1969 Model Code (Canons + Ethical Considerations ("should") + Disciplinary Rules, with only the DRs enforceable); 1983 Model Rules (Rules + Comments, every Rule enforceable), which followed Watergate. The first state code was Alabama's in 1887.
  • The Rules govern discipline only. Civil remedies, sanctions, and disqualification come from other law.

NEW Tensions built into the Rules [S1:26]: they're written mainly for litigators, not deal lawyers. Getting informed consent is hard when you also have to protect confidences or when the client isn't sophisticated. The advocate role pulls against the officer of the court role.

Preamble ¶ 20. A violation "should not itself give rise to a cause of action against a lawyer nor should it create any presumption in such a case that a legal duty has been breached," and doesn't necessarily warrant disqualification. The Rules are relevant evidence of the standard of care, but there is no negligence per se. [W1:31–32]

B. Admission, character & fitness [W1:21–26, 46]

  • Two requirements everywhere: (1) minimum competence (bar exam + MPRE) and (2) character and fitness. The applicant bears the burden.
  • Inquiries must rationally relate to fitness (Schware). Lawful political membership, or responsibly seeking counseling, isn't a ground for denial. Rehabilitation counts: bank robbers have been admitted, but Stephen Glass was not.
  • Candor matters most. Bars compare your law school application to your bar application, and nondisclosure usually hurts more than the underlying fact. Fix omissions now.
  • NEW Bradwell v. State (1872) [S1:29]: the Supreme Court upheld Illinois's refusal to admit Myra Bradwell because she was a woman. It's the historical counterpoint to the modern rule that admission criteria must rationally relate to fitness.
  • NEW Problem 3A-2 quick takes [S1:32–33]. The test is honesty, trustworthiness, and fitness, and candor about the item usually matters more than the item.
    • Default on student loans; 14 bounced checks: these aren't automatic bars, but they can show financial irresponsibility or dishonesty. Bars ask whether the applicant dealt with them responsibly.
    • Recovering alcoholic; therapy and antidepressants: responsibly seeking treatment isn't a ground for denial. The question is current fitness. Disclose honestly when asked.
    • Eight speeding tickets: minor, though a pattern can suggest disregard for the law.
    • Plagiarism: this is dishonesty, so it weighs heavily. The more recent and mature the applicant (middle school, then high school, then college, then law school), the worse it gets.
    • Robbery conviction: serious, but rehabilitation can overcome it (bank robbers have been admitted).
  • NEW Lexa MC [S1:88–90]: an old lie to the press, then answering "No" to "Have you ever made a public false statement?" The most likely ground for denial is knowingly making a false statement of material fact on the application (8.1(a)), not the original lie.
MR 8.1 (bar applicants and lawyers in disciplinary matters) must not: (a) knowingly make a false statement of material fact; (b) fail to disclose a fact necessary to correct a misapprehension the person knows has arisen, or knowingly fail to respond to a lawful demand for information. But 8.1 does not require disclosing information protected by MR 1.6. You may decline to reveal a confidence. You may not lie.
Trap. Ignoring a bar grievance letter because the complaint is frivolous is itself a violation (8.1(b)), even if the grievance is later dismissed. The Fifth Amendment must be asserted properly. It doesn't license silence.

C. Where you may practice: UPL, federal courts, choice of law

  • No national license. Practicing without admission is UPL (a misdemeanor in most states) [W1:21]. A firm office in State B doesn't license an A-only lawyer, and colleagues' licenses don't count. NEW Problem 3A-1 [S1:30]: a PA-only lawyer can't practice in the firm's Camden office five minutes away. Borris MC [S1:86–87]: an out-of-jurisdiction lawyer who advises clients from a Hawaii office, files papers in Hawaii courts, and shares the firm name is engaged in UPL, even if he "only" handles his home jurisdiction's law.
  • MPRE MR 5.5(a)–(b): no practicing where not admitted, and no systematic and continuous presence or holding out as admitted there.
  • MPRE MR 5.5(c) temporary practice is allowed for a lawyer in good standing elsewhere if the work is temporary and: (1) done in association with an actively participating local lawyer; (2) related to a pending or expected proceeding where the lawyer is (or expects to be) authorized, e.g., pro hac vice; (3) related to an ADR proceeding arising from the lawyer's home practice; or (4) otherwise reasonably related to the home practice.
  • Federal courts admit separately. Under the Supremacy Clause, each federal court sets its own bar. After removal, a state-admitted lawyer needs admission to that district or pro hac vice ("for this occasion only") [W1:26, 46]. NEW Most district courts admit anyone admitted in the state where the court sits. Some districts, and all federal courts of appeals, admit lawyers admitted in any state [S1:38]. Problem 3A-3: Frank and his boss kept serving discovery after removal, so they need federal admission or pro hac vice (5.5(a); see 5.5(c)(1)–(2)). Adam MC [S1:91–93]: a lawyer not admitted to the district court can't make even a routine "coverage" appearance. His partner's admission doesn't help, so the appearance would be UPL.
  • McDade Amendment (28 U.S.C. § 530B): federal government lawyers follow the ethics rules of the states where they practice [W1:47]. Federal employment doesn't exempt anyone from a court's contempt power.
MR 8.5Rule
(a) JurisdictionYou're subject to discipline where you're admitted (wherever the conduct occurs) and where you provide services. Both states can discipline you. One state's discipline has no automatic effect elsewhere, but most states impose reciprocal discipline. [W1:21, 29]
MPRE (b)(1) Tribunal mattersApply the rules of the jurisdiction where the tribunal sits, unless the tribunal's rules say otherwise.
MPRE (b)(2) Everything elseApply the rules where the conduct occurred, or where its predominant effect is. Safe harbor: no discipline if you conformed to the rules of a jurisdiction where you reasonably believed the predominant effect would be, even if that prediction turns out wrong.

D. Discipline [W1:27–29]

MR 8.4 misconduct. (a) Violate or attempt to violate the Rules, knowingly assist or induce another to, or do so through the acts of another (e.g., sending an investigator to contact a represented party that you couldn't contact yourself, MR 4.2). (b) A criminal act reflecting adversely on honesty, trustworthiness, or fitness; no charge or conviction needed. (c) Dishonesty, fraud, deceit, misrepresentation. (d) Conduct prejudicial to the administration of justice. (e) Stating or implying an ability to improperly influence an official; the statement alone is enough. (f) Knowingly assisting a judge's misconduct. (g) Harassment or discrimination that the lawyer knows or reasonably should know is harassment or discrimination, based on race, sex, religion, national origin, ethnicity, disability, age, sexual orientation, gender identity, marital status, or socioeconomic status, in conduct related to the practice of law, including managing a firm. NEW Two carve-outs: it doesn't limit accepting, declining, or withdrawing under 1.16, and it doesn't preclude legitimate advice or advocacy [S1:37]. Delaware's 8.4 has no paragraph (g) [S1:34].
  • Off-duty conduct counts (tax evasion, academic cheating). But 8.4(b) doesn't reach every crime: a petty civil-disobedience trespass usually doesn't reflect on fitness (cmt [2]).
  • Process: the complaint is privileged (no defamation suit against the complainant), then screening, then an investigation (cooperation is required, subject to the Fifth Amendment), then probable cause, then a hearing with due process (Ruffalo; Spevack).
  • Sanctions protect the public; they aren't punishment. They range from private or public reprimand to suspension to disbarment, and a reprimand still follows you. Compensation comes from civil remedies.

E. Civil liability, disqualification, fees [W1:29–34]

"Malpractice" is just a collective term. Always name the specific duty breached.

ClaimElements / key points
Professional negligenceDuty, breach, actual + proximate cause, economic damages (emotional distress almost never). Blown deadline means a case within a case: prove what you would have recovered and collected. A few states use "substantial factor" causation.
Breach of fiduciary dutyLoyalty, confidentiality, or candor. No negligence or loss required (e.g., a secret finder's fee from the other side). Remedies: disgorgement; punitive damages if intentional. NEW ADA Anna MC [S1:96–98]: secretly feeding the defense her research to "throw" a case her office ordered her to defend breached her duty of loyalty to her client, the DA's office, however just her motive. The right move was to withdraw or quit. Mr. Kent MC [S1:99–101]: disclosing an invention despite a confidentiality clause supports breach of contract (as well as fiduciary breach).

NEW Max-and-Chip MC: a frivolous suit filed out of spite and summarily dismissed supports malicious prosecution, not fiduciary or contract claims (Chip was never the lawyer's client).

FRCP 11(b)–(c)Certification after reasonable inquiry; monetary or nonmonetary sanctions. The firm is jointly responsible absent exceptional circumstances. NEW Sanctions can be sought by a party or imposed by the court on its own, and can be compensation to the injured party, a penalty paid into court, or nonmonetary directives [S1:46]. Informal consequences (lost clients, partnership, reputation) can be worst of all. Many referrals come from former opposing counsel, so reputational damage can rival formal sanctions [S1:47].

II. Personal Responsibility, Supervision & Human Factors

Ch. 4 · Week 1 · Good people do bad things, and orders don't excuse them. [W1:48–66]

A. The responsibility rules

RuleDuty
MR 5.2(a)A lawyer is bound by the Rules even when acting at another's direction. "I was only following orders" is almost never a defense. Kodak-Berkey [W1:49–51; S1:57]: partner Mahlon Perkins withheld an expert's documents and then lied under oath that they didn't exist. He resigned, served a month in prison, and lost his license. Associate Joseph Fortenberry knew, whispered a warning, and then did nothing further. 5.2(a) means his silence wasn't excused by the hierarchy.
MR 5.2(b)The narrow safe harbor: no violation if the subordinate acts on a supervisor's reasonable resolution of an arguable question of professional duty. It holds even if a tribunal later disagrees. It never covers a clear violation such as destroying evidence or lying. NEW Problem 4A-1 [S1:67–68]: Fortenberry gets no 5.2(b) protection. Perkins never "resolved an arguable question," and withholding documents plus a perjured affidavit is not arguable. A note left on a chair isn't enough. Raise it directly, then go up the chain (another partner, the firm's ethics partner or general counsel) until the documents are produced and the false statements corrected (3.3, 3.4(a)).
MR 5.1(a)–(b)Partners and managers must make reasonable efforts to have firm-wide measures ensuring compliance (e.g., a conflict-check system). No harm is needed for a violation. Direct supervisors must make reasonable efforts to ensure the supervised lawyer complies.

Problem 4D-1 (Ted Toughtimes) [S1:80–81] Partner Ted is distracted, irritable, and returning from lunch unsteady and smelling of liquor. Partner Sarah gives him a new matter "to help his hours" and assigns associate Maya to help.
Ted: must decline if his condition materially impairs him (1.16(a)(2)). Risks 1.1 and 1.3 violations.
Sarah: as a partner and the assigning lawyer, she risks 5.1(a)–(b) (no reasonable measures or supervision), and 5.1(c)(2) if she knows of a violation in time to fix it and doesn't. She also risks civil liability and fee consequences for the firm. She should reassign or closely supervise the matter and steer Ted to help (a LAP or EAP).
Maya: bound by 5.2(a). She can't let the client's matter suffer because Ted is the lead. She should raise concerns with Sarah or the firm's ethics partner. 8.3 applies only if she knows of a violation raising a substantial question about fitness. LAP information is exempt (8.3(c)).

C. When you mess up, 'fess up [W1:59–61]

  • Error festers in darkness, and concealment almost always turns out worse. Confess to the right people in the right order: your supervisor, then the firm's ethics partner or committee, then the malpractice insurer (many policies require notice before you admit error to the client), then outside counsel retained for advice so the conversation is privileged. Then the client must be told of any material error (Part IV-D).
  • NEW Places to go for help [S1:74–75]: your supervising lawyer (unless she's the one leading you astray), your firm's "ethics czar," the bar's or your insurer's ethics hotline, or your own lawyer. 1.6(b)(4) lets you reveal what's reasonably necessary to get advice about your own compliance.
  • NEW Why confess early [S1:121]: you may find the mistake has no consequences, wasn't a mistake at all, or can be fixed. Clients trust a lawyer who comes clean, explains the fix, and doesn't charge for fixing it [S4:30].

NEW D. Stress, impairment & getting help [W1:62–66; S1:58, 76–77, 122–124]

  • Practice stress mirrors law school stress: more stress means less sleep, and less sleep means fuzzy thinking and worse ethical judgment. The habits you build now carry into practice.
  • Warning signs of depression and anxiety: changes in sleep or eating, trouble making decisions, withdrawal, and similar. (Flashbacks of a traumatic event point to PTSD, not depression or anxiety, so it's the "not a common sign" answer.)
  • Get help: student services, referrals from friends and family, EAPs, and Lawyer Assistance Programs. LAP help is confidential and generally isn't reported to the disciplinary authority while the lawyer is complying with treatment (Linda MC). 8.3(c) exempts LAP information from the reporting duty.
  • Bar applications and mental health: responsibly seeking treatment isn't a ground for denial (Part I-B), and impairment that materially affects the work triggers 1.16(a)(2).

III. Forming & Defining the Relationship

Ch. 9 A–E (Week 2) + Ch. 8 A (Week 3) · Who is your client, what did you agree to do, and what do you owe people who aren't clients?

The Bobby Boilermaker problem [W2:28–39]. Charlie Comp's letter said "worker compensation matter." Bobby reasonably understood "all my compensation," and his products-liability claim expired. Scope is judged from the client's point of view. Forming a relationship instantly imposes all four duties, and you bear the risk of uncertainty.

A. Declining [W2:30–34, 63–64]

  • May decline for almost any reason (e.g., disliking an abrasive client), absent a court appointment or an unlawful reason such as invidious discrimination.
  • Intake red flags (intake mistakes are among the most common): unfamiliar law; revenge or "making a point"; unmanageable expectations; serial lawyer-changers; rudeness to staff; "I don't care what it costs"; Internet experts; shifting stories.
  • MPRE MR 6.2 (appointments): don't seek to avoid an appointment except for good cause: (a) it would violate the Rules or law; (b) it would impose an unreasonable financial burden; or (c) the client or cause is so repugnant it would impair the relationship or your ability to represent. Distaste alone isn't enough.
MR 1.16(a) must decline or withdraw if: (1) the representation will violate the Rules or other law (e.g., a nonconsentable conflict); (2) the lawyer's condition materially impairs the representation; (3) the lawyer is discharged; (4) (2023) the client or prospective client seeks to use or persists in using the lawyer's services for crime or fraud despite the MR 1.2(d)/1.4(a)(5) discussion.
2023 duty to inquire: "A lawyer shall inquire into and assess the facts and circumstances of each representation." ABA Op. 513 calls for a risk-based, proportional inquiry at intake, repeated when circumstances change. The "must withdraw" threshold sits somewhere between naked suspicion and actual knowledge. No state had adopted this as of the book, but the MPRE tests it.

B. Forming the relationship [W2:34–37]

Restatement § 14. A relationship arises when (1) a person manifests intent that the lawyer provide legal services, and either (a) the lawyer manifests consent, or (b) the lawyer fails to manifest lack of consent and knows or reasonably should know the person reasonably relies on the lawyer; or (2) a tribunal appoints the lawyer (no fee or contract needed).
  • The test is objective with a subjective element: the client must actually and reasonably believe a relationship exists. Silence can be consent (e.g., "please handle my eviction" and no reply while the hearing approaches).
  • Not required: fees, a conflict check, or an engagement letter, unless you told the person there's no relationship until those are done. "Use your words."
  • Status is matter by matter. One person can be a current, former, and prospective client at once (a PI client from last year who now asks about estate planning is a former client and a prospective client).
  • NEW Appointment creates the relationship without any signed agreement. Abed, appointed to defend Troy, can't skip the arraignment because Troy hasn't signed a retainer [S2:69–70]. Logan MC [S2:71–73]: Logan took the facts, never sent the retainer or ran conflicts, and Connor did nothing further. There's a relationship (and malpractice exposure) if an objective observer would find Connor reasonably relied on Logan.
  • NEW Accidental client at a party (Problems 9A-1, 8A-5) [S2:47–48; S3:59]. Risk rises at each step. Small talk: none. He describes his divorce: a possible prospective client (confidentiality). He asks whether his lawyer is doing a good job: a request for advice. Maya asks follow-ups and comments on each piece of advice: she's giving legal advice he may rely on (Togstad care; maybe a relationship under § 14). Handing over her card when he says he wants to switch lawyers suggests future representation. Fix: "I can't advise you, and I'm not a family lawyer. Please raise this with your lawyer or get a formal second opinion."
  • Open-ended engagements (occasional consulting) are never "completed." Recent contact, bills, and no disengagement letter mean a current client, so a new adverse matter needs consent (1.7).

C. Prospective clients: MR 1.18 [W3:3–6; W2:36–37]

  • Who qualifies (1.18(a), cmt [2]): someone who consults about the possibility of forming a relationship, with an actual and reasonable belief the talk was about possible representation.
    • Likely yes: the lawyer specifically invites information without clear warnings.
    • Likely no: unilateral communications prompted by general advertising, and "cookie-licking," i.e., consulting every lawyer in town to conflict them out, which fails the subjective test (ABA Op. 492). Don't infer bad faith just because someone interviewed several firms.
  • 1.18(b): even when no relationship follows, you may not use or reveal information learned from the prospective client (only that information, not what you find independently).
  • Avoid accidental prospective clients: "I'm not a family lawyer." Invite a real consultation only after a conflict check.
  • NEW Cmt [1]: prospective clients get "some but not all" of a client's protection, because consultations are usually limited in time and depth [S3:11].
  • NEW Shola MC [S2:78–79]: a prospective client who decides not to hire you is still owed confidentiality for what she told you (1.18(b)), even with no relationship and no privilege argument. Threatening to publicize it violates 1.18(b).
MPRE MR 1.18(c)–(d) conflicts. A lawyer who received information that could be significantly harmful may not represent a client with materially adverse interests in the same or a substantially related matter, and that bar is imputed to the firm. The firm is cured by (1) informed consent, confirmed in writing, from both the affected client and the prospective client; or (2) the lawyer took reasonable measures to limit intake to what was needed, and is timely screened, gets no part of the fee, and written notice goes promptly to the prospective client.

IV. The Lawyer as Fiduciary: Loyalty, Conflicts & Candor

Ch. 5 · Week 4 · "Not honesty alone, but the punctilio of an honor the most sensitive" (Meinhard v. Salmon). [W4:1–14]

A. Becoming a fiduciary

  • By status: lawyers (always, to clients, everywhere), agents, trustees, guardians, directors, general partners, doctors, clergy. By circumstance: someone reposes special trust and the fiduciary accepts it (a neighbor given signature authority over an elderly friend's accounts; some states call this a "confidential relationship").
  • NEW What makes someone a fiduciary [S4:5–7]: a position of trust and responsibility over another's property or affairs, plus special skills the principal relies on. Fiduciary status arises by circumstance when the beneficiary offers, and the fiduciary accepts, trust because of (1) the special nature of the relationship, (2) the fiduciary's special skills, or (3) the beneficiary's entrusting secret or confidential information. Some states treat a "confidential relationship" as less than a full fiduciary relationship.
  • Scope is limited by beneficiary (identified persons) and subject matter (the engagement).
  • Self-dealing claims: in many states the burden shifts to the fiduciary to prove fairness, and enhanced remedies (disgorgement, punitives) are available. Expect a higher standard even when you "weren't acting as a lawyer."
Four-question fiduciary analysis [S4:4, 9] (1) What is the obligation, and to whom? (2) Was it breached? (3) What harm did, or could, the breach cause, and to whom? (4) What are the consequences (the "Very Bad Thing")? Applied to Nora Neighbor (5B-1): a situational fiduciary who invested Dale's savings in her spouse's start-up is self-dealing, so expect the burden to shift to her, with disgorgement and punitive damages possible, and discipline under 8.4(c) even though she was never Dale's lawyer.

V. Competence, Diligence & Care

Frame: competence and diligence (MR 1.1, 1.3) are disciplinary duties, and no harm is required. Care is the civil duty, and causation of monetary harm is required. Criminal defense adds the Sixth Amendment.

A. Competence: MR 1.1

MR 1.1: "competent representation... requires the legal knowledge, skill, thoroughness and preparation reasonably necessary for the representation."
  • You can become competent (cmts [2], [4]): "A newly admitted lawyer can be as competent as a practitioner with long experience." You may accept if competence "can be achieved by reasonable preparation," or by associating with "a lawyer of established competence" (cmt [1]). Neither client consent nor pro bono status waives the duty.
  • Cmt [1] factors (analyze each, then weigh): complexity and specialization; general experience; training in the field; preparation possible; feasibility of referring, associating, or consulting. NEW "In many instances, the required proficiency is that of a general practitioner," though some matters require expertise.
  • NEW Cmt [2]: analysis of precedent, evaluation of evidence, and drafting are required in every matter, and "perhaps the most fundamental legal skill" is identifying what kind of legal problems a situation involves. Cmt [5]: competent handling requires inquiry into and analysis of the facts and law, methods meeting the standards of competent practitioners, and preparation proportional to what's at stake. A 1.2(c) scope limit can narrow what you're responsible for [S2:3–5].
  • NEW Problem 7A-1 (Maya and sales tax) [S2:7–8]: she may expand the engagement if she becomes competent through reasonable study (without billing all of it) or by associating with or consulting a tax lawyer.   • Emergency (cmt [3]): in a true emergency, when referral or consultation is impractical, you may give advice beyond your experience, limited to what's reasonably necessary [W2:24].
  • The "duty to be supervised": ask, insist, or find help elsewhere. "I asked and asked" is no defense (5.2(a)). A looming deadline requires protective action, not letting it lapse.
  • Learning time vs. fees: becoming competent doesn't justify billing an unreasonable amount of learning time at full rates. Competence (1.1) and fee reasonableness (1.5(a)) are separate questions.

  • Technology (cmt [8]): keep up with "changes in the law and its practice, including the benefits and risks associated with relevant technology," and do your CLE. Mata v. B. Diligence: MR 1.3

  • "Reasonable diligence and promptness." Neglect and abandonment are among the most common sources of discipline. Cmt [3]: "Perhaps no professional shortcoming is more widely resented than procrastination." Ignoring calls for months violates 1.3 and 1.4 even if the client isn't harmed.
  • The cats won't herd themselves: use ticklers, calendars, and staff. Cmt [4]: when it's unclear whether the relationship continues, clarify, "preferably in writing." NEW Cmt [2]: "A lawyer's work load must be controlled so that each matter can be handled competently" [S2:10].
  • NEW Leanne MC [S2:31–32]: the judge orally moved deadlines, she never told her assistant, and she missed the reply. That violates 1.3 even though the court allowed a late filing and she won. Delegating calendaring doesn't delegate responsibility.
  • NEW Problem 7B-3 (Willie's unfunded living trust) [S2:16–17]: he signed the trust documents but never retitled the assets, and the husband died eight months later. That breaches diligence (1.3, so discipline) and care (civil damages for probate costs, delay, and taxes). The partner is personally liable and the firm vicariously so. "Busy practice" is exactly what cmt [2] forbids.
  • NEW Problem 7B-2 (Stanley Sloppy) [S2:15]: he let the limitations period run on a whiplash claim and ignored the client's calls. That breaches care, 1.3, and 1.4. To recover, Wanda must prove the case within a case: the claim would have won, for how much, and been collectible.

C. The civil duty of care [W2:9–11; W4:18]

  • Standard: the skill, diligence, care, and knowledge of a reasonably careful and prudent lawyer in similar circumstances. It's the standard of a reasonable specialist if you hold yourself out as one or were hired as one.
  • Losing isn't negligence. The standard guarantees no result. It's the minimum, so aim above it. NEW Louise MC [S2:36–37]: a reasoned strategic choice not to impeach, discussed with the client beforehand, doesn't establish malpractice. The client must prove both breach and causation.
  • Never promise results. Hawkins v. McGee (the "hairy hand" case) imposed contract liability on a guarantee without negligence. Silence in the face of unrealistic expectations may be read as a guarantee, so correct them promptly and put a no-guarantee clause in the engagement letter.
  • Causation: a case within a case. A worthless underlying claim means no damages, but discipline remains possible.

D. Exceptions: framing principles [W3:25–29]

  • Break information into its smallest units. Check whether it's confidential at all before hunting for an exception. "Not confidential" means only "may," not "should."
  • Every 1.6(b) exception is permissive, covers disclosure only (not use), and applies only "to the extent the lawyer reasonably believes necessary": the minimum information, to the minimum people, for the specific purpose. Exceeding any of the three is a violation. "Reasonably believes" means a subjective belief that is also objectively reasonable.
  • Consent: express informed consent needs no particular form (oral is fine; confirm significant consents in writing). Implied authorization covers need-to-know sharing with the firm team and facts in a filed complaint (cmt [5]). It doesn't override an express client restriction. NEW Problem 8B-1 [S3:76–78]: briefing a firm colleague who is helping review documents is implied. Revealing where the bodies are, after explaining the goodwill and the incriminating effect and getting the client's agreement, is informed consent. Previewing the client's anticipated testimony in an opening statement is implied (it carries out the representation), though it's wise to walk the client through it.
  • Exceptions also live outside 1.6: 1.13(c) and 3.3. States vary on 1.6(b) more than on any other provision.

F. When disclosure becomes mandatory, or goes beyond 1.6

  • MR 4.1(b): you must disclose a material fact when necessary to avoid assisting a client's crime or fraud, unless 1.6 prohibits it. This turns a 1.6(b)(2)–(3) permission into a duty. See noisy withdrawal, Part IX.
  • MR 1.13(b) report up: if an organization's lawyer knows a constituent is violating a legal obligation to the organization, or law that may be imputed to it, likely causing substantial injury, the lawyer must act in its best interest, ordinarily reporting to higher authority, up to the board. A rogue officer's demand for silence doesn't control.
  • MR 1.13(c) report out: if the highest authority fails to address a clear violation of law reasonably certain to cause substantial injury to the organization, the lawyer may reveal information, even if 1.6 wouldn't permit it, to the extent necessary. Unlike 1.6(b)(2), this doesn't require use of the lawyer's services [W2:85]. MPRE 1.13(d): it doesn't apply to a lawyer retained to investigate or defend the alleged violation.
  • MPRE MR 3.3 candor to the tribunal: (a)(1) no false statements of fact or law to a tribunal, and correct your own; (a)(2) disclose directly adverse controlling authority the opponent hasn't disclosed; (a)(3) no offering evidence you know is false; if material false evidence was offered, take reasonable remedial measures, including disclosure to the tribunal. (c): these duties apply even if compliance requires disclosing 1.6 information [W3:40; W2:85].
  • 8.1 and 8.3 don't require revealing confidences.
Honesty and confidentiality together. When asked about a confidential plan ("Will you move to dismiss?"), decline to answer, and don't lie (MR 4.1(a), 8.4(c); In re Pressly). "I can't discuss that" satisfies both duties.

G. Hard cases [W3:36]

  • 8C-1, miscarriage of justice: another man is imprisoned for the client's crime. Imprisonment isn't death or bodily harm, and there's no wrongful-conviction exception. Urge the client to come forward. Things that can change the answer: the client's death (in some states) or an approaching execution (arguably "reasonably certain death").
  • 8C-2, kidnapped children: if they may be alive, (b)(1) permits disclosing the location, limited to what's necessary (McClure v. Thompson).
  • NEW The Buried Bodies Case (Lake Pleasant, N.Y., 1970s) [W3:42 n.26]: defense lawyers who knew where two missing girls' bodies were stayed silent even when the families begged. They were ostracized locally and lionized by the defense bar. Contrast Problem 8B-1(b), where the client consented to disclosure.

VII. The Lawyer as Agent: Authority

Ch. 6 · Week 4 · Fiduciary law governs lawyer ↔ client. Agency adds the lawyer's power to change the client's legal relations with third parties. [W4:20–51]

  • Formation: the principal manifests intent that the agent act, and the agent consents (Restatement (Third) Agency § 1.01). If you're someone's lawyer, you're their agent. Authorized contracts bind the client, and for the agent's torts both principal and agent can be liable.
  • The Homeowners: "Honest Abe" did nothing, and they lost the foreclosure despite winning defenses. Careless means decisions bind the client, so there's no automatic do-over. The remedy is against the lawyer. Otherwise third parties couldn't rely on agents, and debtors would hire lawyers to fail on purpose.
The book's three questions: (1) What kind of authority (actual, implied, apparent, ratification), from what facts? (2) What is its scope? (3) If exceeded, what are the consequences, and who bears them?

B. Exceeding authority: consequences [W4:30–32]

  • Discipline under MR 1.2(a) (failing to abide by the client's decision). A false claim of authority also violates 4.1(a) and 8.4(c).
  • Undoing the act: rescission, quiet title, reinstatement (FRCP 60), usually after litigating authority.
  • Liability to the client: an unreasonable belief in authority breaches care, and the client recovers the cost of cleanup.
  • Liability to the opponent: signing implies a warranty of authority. Claiming authority you know you lack is fraud, and the firm is liable by respondeat superior.
  • A conflict with the client once she disputes your authority (disclose and get consent, or withdraw).
  • NEW What the opponent does next [S4:56–59]: walk away (which doesn't excuse your ethics violation), or try to enforce the deal, often by motion in the pending case (Kokkonen). Either way, expect your client to sue you, fire you, and dispute your fees. The relationship can be saved only with informed consent, preferably in writing, and that's unlikely. The opponent can also sue you and your firm for fraud, and opposing counsel may have to report you (8.3). Misrepresentation (tort) and breach of warranty of authority (contract) carry different damages measures [W4:31 n.11].
  • NEW Ratification outcomes (Problem 6A-4) [S4:53–55]: (a) she grumbles "have it your way," signs, then fires Frank: ratified and bound. (b) She refuses, the defense withdraws, then she wants the deal: too late (§ 4.05). (c) She X's out the agreement but deposits the check as a "down payment": conduct ratifies, and she can't take the benefit while rejecting the rest (§ 4.07), so the settlement is likely enforceable.
  • Negotiating safely: the "non-offer offer." "My own view is X. My client wants more, but I'd recommend X if your side would." Report it to the client, who decides.

MR 1.2(a): a lawyer "shall abide by a client's decisions concerning the objectives... and, as required by Rule 1.4, shall consult with the client as to the means." The client decides (1) whether to settle, and in a criminal case (2) the plea, (3) jury waiver, (4) whether to testify.

  • Fee terms that penalize the client's decision are void or unconscionable: hourly fees kick in if the client rejects the recommended settlement; retroactive conversion to hourly. Ordinary deposits for real next steps are fine, but timing that looks coercive is suspect.

VIII. Fees & Client Property

Chs. 20–21 · Week 5 · Fee agreements are contracts with special overlays. Trust money is sacred. [W5:1–39]

A. Communicating the fee: MR 1.5(b)–(c) [W5:2–8]

  • Fees vs. expenses: fees pay for professional services. Expenses are out-of-pocket costs specific to this matter, never rent or general salaries. In-house charges (copying, research) must reflect actual cost or a reasonable amount agreed in advance. A markup disclosed only on the invoice doesn't count (cmt [1]).
MR 1.5(b): the scope and the basis or rate of the fee and expenses "shall be communicated to the client, preferably in writing, before or within a reasonable time after commencing the representation, except when the lawyer will charge a regularly represented client on the same basis or rate." Any change in basis or rate must be communicated (a long-time client's rate increase too). Best practice: a writing signed by the client (cmt [2]).
MR 1.5(c) contingent fees: "in a writing signed by the client" stating: the method, "including the percentage or percentages... in the event of settlement, trial or appeal"; the expenses to be deducted; whether expenses come off before or after the percentage; and any expenses the client owes "whether or not the client is the prevailing party." At the end: a written closing statement of the outcome, the remittance, and how it was calculated. A signature doesn't cure missing terms, and the lawyer's own email or letter plus the client's "sounds good" isn't a signed writing. Any contingent component of a hybrid fee (a success bonus, reverse contingency, or shares payable on sale) needs the client's signed writing.

B. Substantive limits: MR 1.5(a), (d) [W5:9–12, 26]

MR 1.5(a): no agreement for, charging, or collecting an unreasonable fee or unreasonable expenses. The violation is complete on agreeing or charging, even without collecting. Eight factors (none alone decides): (1) time, labor, novelty, difficulty, skill; (2) preclusion of other work, if apparent to the client; (3) customary local fee; (4) amount involved and results; (5) time limits; (6) nature and length of the relationship; (7) the lawyer's experience, reputation, ability; (8) fixed or contingent. The client's ability to pay is not a factor.
  • Judge fees as of the agreement, accounting for the risk assumed. A quick $150K settlement a week after a 33% agreement doesn't make the fee unreasonable. Rush premiums agreed in advance are OK (factors 2 and 5), and different rates for different work are OK.
  • Statutory caps bind (workers' comp, med-mal, Social Security, probate, the FTCA's 25%). Goldfarb v. Virginia State Bar: bar minimum-fee schedules violated antitrust law. That's different from lawful statutory caps.
MR 1.5(d) no contingent fee: (1) in a domestic relations matter contingent on securing a divorce or on the amount of alimony, support, or a property settlement in lieu thereof; (2) for representing a criminal defendant. Signatures and reasonableness don't cure either. The book calls both anachronisms, but they're still the law. Civil plaintiff contingencies are fine. NEW Cmt [6]: a contingent fee to collect post-judgment arrears of support, alimony, or other financial orders is allowed; it doesn't raise the same concerns [S5:11]. Rationales: the domestic ban is meant to avoid discouraging reconciliation (McCarthy v. Santangelo: "The state does not favor divorces"); the criminal ban is about the danger of corrupting justice (Peyton v. Margiotti) [W5:10–11].

B. Leave of the tribunal: MR 1.16(c) [W2:57–61]

MR 1.16(c): "comply with applicable law requiring notice to or permission of a tribunal when terminating a representation. When ordered to do so by a tribunal, a lawyer shall continue representation notwithstanding good cause for terminating."
  • Why courts keep you in: to protect the calendar and prevent opportunism (firing lawyer after lawyer on the eve of trial). Until relieved, every duty continues, even if the client is the one at fault. Leave is most often denied close to trial or when the motion looks opportunistic.
  • Two routes out: substitution of counsel (almost always approved; look before you leap if you're the one coming in), or a motion to withdraw. Your reasons are confidential, so say only that "professional considerations require termination" (cmt [3]). If ordered to say more, 1.6(b)(6) permits it; ask for sealing or in camera review.
  • NEW Nonpayment disclosures: ABA Op. 476 (2016) suggests you may disclose the fact of nonpayment under 1.6(b)(5) when withdrawing, but the case law is mixed, so avoid it unless the court presses [W2:84 n.23]. "Your lack of planning is not my emergency": courts deny last-minute motions [W2:84 n.22].
  • Never threaten to skip trial to get paid. That's abandonment and violates 1.16(c), 1.1, and 1.3, with sanctions and liability to follow. Warn the client and file a motion instead.

C. Noisy withdrawal [W2:66–68]

  • When a client used your work product (an opinion letter or offering documents) for fraud: must withdraw (1.16(a)(4)); may give notice and disaffirm the opinion or document (1.2 cmt [10]); disclosure needs a 1.6 exception, usually (b)(2)–(3); and MR 4.1(b) makes disclosure mandatory if needed to avoid assisting the fraud.
  • In-house counsel: mandatory withdrawal can mean quitting your job. Employment never excuses assisting fraud, and the company is the client.

D. Exit duties: MR 1.16(d) [W2:69–74]

MR 1.16(d): "take steps to the extent reasonably practicable to protect a client's interests, such as giving reasonable notice, allowing time for employment of other counsel, surrendering papers and property to which the client is entitled and refunding any advance payment... that has not been earned or incurred." You may retain papers only to the extent other law permits. Cmt [9]: these duties apply even if you were unfairly discharged.
  • Files are the client's. In all but a few states (Illinois and Georgia are the exceptions), you can't hold the file for unpaid fees. "Papers to which the client is entitled" means at least everything reasonably necessary to protect the client's rights, paid for or not (an unpaid expert report). Turn over embarrassing items too: they're discoverable, and withholding them looks like a cover-up. You may withhold your own privileged consultations with properly designated firm counsel.
  • NEW Never destroy or "lose" file material to hide an error. That violates 3.4(a) (and (d)), risks obstruction charges, and turns negligence into an intentional tort. Handing over the whole file is usually the least bad option [W2:73].
  • Refunds: return unearned funds, hold disputed amounts in trust, no self-help.
  • Also: give the client all dates and deadlines in writing, flag limitations periods, forward papers that arrive later, and help successor counsel within reason, "especially when the client doesn't deserve it." A detailed file index isn't required.
