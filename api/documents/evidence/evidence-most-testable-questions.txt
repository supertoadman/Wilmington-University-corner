Evidence Most Testable Questions
================================

Subject: Evidence
Type: Practice Questions
Source: https://supertoadman.github.io/Wilmington-University-corner/subjects/evidence/evidence-most-testable-questions/

60 five-choice questions on the most testable material from Weeks 1–15, in two sets of 30. About a third is hearsay, followed by character evidence, impeachment, confrontation, expert testimony, and privileges. Every question uses new facts, and every choice is explained.

---

## Set 1 · Q1 · Specialized Relevance Rules — Rule 407: feasibility put in dispute
A customer sues a ladder manufacturer, claiming the hinge on its Model 6 ladder was defectively designed. Two months after the customer's fall, the manufacturer changed the hinge. At trial, the manufacturer's engineer testifies on direct that the old hinge was the safest design that could practically have been built. The customer now offers the design change. What is the most likely ruling?

A. Excluded, because Rule 407 bars later design changes in any claim of a product defect.
B. Admitted to prove the defect, because Rule 407 does not reach strict-liability product claims.
C. Admitted, because the manufacturer disputed feasibility, with a limiting instruction on request.
D. Excluded, because a defense witness may be impeached only with measures taken before the injury.
E. Admitted for any purpose, because calling an engineer to testify waives the protection of Rule 407.

Answer: C
- A: Rule 407 bars the use only for negligence, culpable conduct, a defect, or a need for a warning. It allows another purpose, including feasibility if disputed.
- B: This is the view the 1997 amendment rejected. Rule 407 now expressly covers a defect in a product or its design.
- C: Correct. Rule 407 permits proof of feasibility if disputed. Saying nothing safer could practically be built disputes it, and Rule 105 limits the jury's use on request.
- D: A subsequent measure is exactly what Rule 407 lets a party use for impeachment or feasibility. Timing defines the rule; it doesn't limit impeachment this way.
- E: Opening the door permits only the other purpose. The evidence still can't be used to prove the defect.
Rule: Rule 407 bars subsequent measures to prove negligence, culpable conduct, a defect, or a need for a warning. They may come in for another purpose, such as impeachment or, if disputed, ownership, control, or feasibility.

## Set 1 · Q2 · Specialized Relevance Rules — Rules 408 and 409: apology vs. negotiation
After a parking-lot collision, Dana jumps out and tells Lee, "This was my fault. I'll pay your hospital bill." Three weeks later, Lee's lawyer demands $50,000 and Dana's lawyer disputes the amount. At a settlement meeting, Dana says, "I know I ran the stop sign." Lee sues Dana. Which statement is admissible to prove Dana's liability?

A. Only "I know I ran the stop sign."
B. Only "This was my fault."
C. "This was my fault" and "I'll pay your hospital bill."
D. All three, because each is an opposing party's statement.
E. None of the three, because Rules 408 and 409 cover them all.

Answer: B
- A: This one was made during compromise negotiations over a claim disputed in amount, so Rule 408(a)(2) bars it.
- B: Correct. Rule 409 bars only the offer to pay expenses, not statements that go with it. The fault statement is Dana's own under Rule 801(d)(2)(A), and no claim was yet in dispute for Rule 408.
- C: The fault statement comes in, but Rule 409 bars the offer to pay the hospital bill when offered to prove liability.
- D: Rule 801(d)(2) answers only a hearsay objection. Rules 408 and 409 are separate bars that still apply.
- E: Rule 409 is narrower than Rule 408. It doesn't protect an accident-scene admission of fault.
Rule: Rule 409 protects only the offer to pay medical expenses. Rule 408 protects conduct and statements in negotiations over a disputed claim as well. An apology at the scene isn't covered; the same words at a settlement meeting are.

## Set 1 · Q3 · Character Evidence — Doctrine of chances; acquittal; Huddleston
Ruth is charged with burning her warehouse to collect insurance. She says old wiring started the fire by accident. The government offers evidence that two other buildings she owned burned in the past six years and that she collected insurance on both. Ruth was tried and acquitted for the second fire. No one was charged for the first. How should the court analyze this evidence?

A. It is barred as to the second fire, because an acquittal establishes that the act did not occur.
B. It may come in only if the judge herself finds by a preponderance that Ruth set both earlier fires.
C. It is barred, because Rule 404(b) requires a conviction or at least a charge for each other act.
D. It may show lack of accident if a jury could reasonably find the acts occurred, subject to Rule 403.
E. It may come in to show Ruth is the kind of person who burns buildings for money, subject to Rule 403.

Answer: D
- A: An acquittal shows only that guilt wasn't proved beyond a reasonable doubt. Under Huddleston, a jury need only be able to find the act occurred, so acquittal doesn't bar it.
- B: This is a Rule 104(a) standard. Huddleston treats other acts as a Rule 104(b) question: could a jury reasonably find the act occurred?
- C: Rule 404(b) speaks of any other crime, wrong, or act. No conviction or charge is required.
- D: Correct. Lack of accident is a listed purpose under Rule 404(b)(2). Repeated accidents are improbable (Rex v. Smith), and Huddleston sets the Rule 104(b) standard, followed by Rule 403 and a limiting instruction on request.
- E: That is the propensity use barred by Rule 404(b)(1). Rule 403 doesn't make a barred purpose acceptable.
Rule: Rule 404(b)(2) allows other acts for a non-character purpose. Under Huddleston: a proper purpose, Rule 104(b) sufficiency, Rule 403, and a Rule 105 instruction on request. An acquittal or the lack of a charge doesn't bar the evidence.

## Set 1 · Q4 · Character Evidence — Identity: prosaic commonalities
Marek is charged with robbing a credit union. The robber wore a ski mask, showed a handgun, and passed the teller a note demanding cash. The government offers evidence that Marek robbed a bank in another city two years earlier, also wearing a ski mask, showing a handgun, and passing a note. It says the evidence proves identity. What is the most likely ruling?

A. Admitted, because identity is listed in Rule 404(b)(2) and listing it ends the inquiry.
B. Admitted, because two crimes of the same type make it more likely the same person did both.
C. Excluded, because other crimes may never prove identity when the defendant denies being there.
D. Excluded, because two years is too remote to allow any use of a prior crime.
E. Excluded, because these common features leave only a propensity inference.

Answer: E
- A: Listing a purpose doesn't compel admission. The evidence must actually prove identity by a non-propensity chain, and Rule 403 still applies.
- B: That reasons from the defendant's type of crime, which is the propensity inference. United States v. Trenkler asks whether the crime could likely be anyone else's.
- C: Identity is a recognized purpose. United States v. Trenkler admitted another crime for this reason because the similarities were distinctive.
- D: Rule 404(b) has no fixed time limit. Remoteness is at most a factor in the balance.
- E: Correct. Identity requires characteristics sufficiently idiosyncratic to permit an inference of pattern. Under United States v. Trenkler, prosaic commonalities aren't enough, and masks, guns, and notes are common to many robberies.
Rule: To prove identity under Rule 404(b)(2), the other crime must share idiosyncratic features that work like a signature. Prosaic commonalities leave only propensity (United States v. Trenkler).

## Set 1 · Q5 · Character Evidence — Identity: a signature method
(Varies question 1.4.)
Same facts, except that in both robberies the robber wore a beekeeper's veil, handed the teller a rhyming demand typed in green ink, and left one chess piece on the counter. What is the most likely ruling?

A. Excluded, because similarity between crimes shows only propensity, however unusual the method.
B. Admitted for identity, subject to Rule 403, because the features work like a signature.
C. Admitted without Rule 403 review, because signature evidence falls outside the rule entirely.
D. Excluded, because the government must first prove the earlier robbery beyond a reasonable doubt.
E. Admitted as proof that Marek has a character trait for planning elaborate robberies.

Answer: B
- A: A distinctive method supports a non-character inference: the same person did both. That is the route United States v. Trenkler allowed.
- B: Correct. These details are idiosyncratic enough to suggest one author, so identity is a proper purpose under Rule 404(b)(2). Rule 403 and a limiting instruction still apply.
- C: Rule 404(b)(2) never compels admission. Rule 403 applies to every non-character use.
- D: Huddleston requires only evidence from which a jury could reasonably find the earlier act occurred.
- E: A trait for elaborate robberies is character. The permitted inference runs from the method to identity, not through character.
Rule: One changed fact decides the result: distinctive shared features turn a propensity argument into proof of identity, subject to Rule 403 (United States v. Trenkler).

## Set 1 · Q6 · Character Evidence — Rule 404(a)(2)(B): victim's trait opens the door
Owen is charged with assaulting Reese and claims self-defense. Owen calls a witness who testifies, "In my opinion, Reese is a violent person." Which response by the prosecution is proper?

A. Calling witnesses to give opinions that Reese is peaceful and that Owen is violent.
B. Calling an officer to testify that Owen broke a man's jaw in a bar fight in 2021.
C. Offering nothing on Owen's character, because Owen never offered his own character.
D. Offering Reese's peacefulness only, because rebuttal is limited to the alleged victim.
E. Offering reputation evidence only, because Rule 405(a) excludes opinion on rebuttal.

Answer: A
- A: Correct. Once a defendant offers the alleged victim's pertinent trait, Rule 404(a)(2)(B) lets the prosecutor rebut it and offer the defendant's same trait. Rule 405(a) allows reputation or opinion.
- B: Rule 405(a) limits this proof to reputation or opinion. Violence isn't an essential element here, so Rule 405(b) doesn't allow specific acts.
- C: Attacking the victim's trait exposes the defendant's same trait, even if he never offered his own character.
- D: Rule 404(a)(2)(B) reaches further: the prosecutor may also offer the defendant's same trait.
- E: Rule 405(a) treats reputation and opinion alike, including on rebuttal.
Rule: Under Rule 404(a)(2)(B), a defendant who offers the victim's pertinent trait lets the prosecutor rebut it and offer the defendant's same trait. Under Rule 405(a), the form is reputation or opinion.

## Set 1 · Q7 · Character Evidence — Rule 413 and ordinary Rule 403
Tobin is charged in federal court with sexually assaulting a coworker. Thirty days before trial, the government discloses that it will call a woman who says Tobin sexually assaulted her four years ago. Tobin was never charged. Which statement is correct?

A. The testimony must fit a non-character purpose listed in Rule 404(b)(2).
B. The testimony is inadmissible, because Rule 413 requires a prior conviction.
C. The testimony must be admitted, because Rule 413 displaces Rule 403.
D. The testimony may be considered for any relevant matter, if it survives Rule 403.
E. The testimony is barred by Rule 412, because it concerns another person's sexual conduct.

Answer: D
- A: Rule 413 is a true exception that allows propensity reasoning, so no separate purpose is needed.
- B: No conviction is required. Rule 413 speaks of evidence that the defendant committed another sexual assault.
- C: United States v. Guardia holds that Rule 413 doesn't displace Rule 403. The ordinary balance still applies.
- D: Correct. Under Rule 413(a), the evidence may be considered on any matter to which it is relevant, including propensity. United States v. Guardia keeps the normal Rule 403 balance, and the 15-day disclosure was met.
- E: Rule 412 protects a victim's sexual behavior and predisposition. It doesn't shield the defendant's other assaults.
Rule: Rules 413–415 let propensity reasoning in for sexual-assault and child-molestation cases, with no conviction required, on 15 days' disclosure, and subject to ordinary Rule 403 balancing (United States v. Guardia).

## Set 1 · Q8 · Impeachment and the Rape Shield — Rule 609(a)(1)(B): testifying defendant
Cole is on trial for armed robbery and plans to testify; his testimony is the core of his defense. Six years ago he was convicted of armed robbery and served two years. The government moves to impeach him with that conviction. Which analysis is correct?

A. It must be admitted unless its value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice.
B. It must be admitted with no balancing, because robbery is a crime of dishonesty.
C. It comes in only if its value outweighs the prejudice to Cole, and similarity cuts against it.
D. It comes in only if its value substantially outweighs prejudice, because it is over five years old.
E. It is excluded automatically, because the conviction is for the same crime as the charge.

Answer: C
- A: That is the Rule 403 test of Rule 609(a)(1)(A), used for witnesses other than a criminal defendant.
- B: Robbery generally isn't a crime of dishonesty, so Rule 609(a)(2) doesn't apply.
- C: Correct. Rule 609(a)(1)(B) requires that probative value outweigh prejudicial effect to the defendant. Under the Gordon factors in United States v. Brewer, similarity to the charge and the importance of his testimony weigh against admission.
- D: The reverse balance belongs to Rule 609(b), which starts after 10 years, not five.
- E: Similarity weighs against admission but doesn't exclude automatically. The rule calls for a balance.
Rule: A testifying criminal defendant's felony comes in only if probative value outweighs prejudicial effect, under Rule 609(a)(1)(B). The Gordon factors treat similarity to the charge and the importance of the defendant's testimony as weighing against admission.

## Set 1 · Q9 · Impeachment and the Rape Shield — Rule 608(b): take the answer
In a contract trial, the plaintiff's sales manager testifies. Defense counsel has an insurance claim the manager filed swearing a laptop was stolen, and a repair invoice showing she still had it a month later. Counsel asks, "You lied on that insurance claim, didn't you?" She says no. Counsel then offers the claim and invoice, both authenticated. She was never charged. What is the correct ruling?

A. The question is improper, because counsel must have admissible proof of the lie before asking.
B. The question is proper, and the documents come in because they have been authenticated.
C. The question is improper, because Rule 609 is the only way to prove a witness's lies.
D. The question is proper, and the documents come in as contradiction of her denial.
E. The question is proper, but the documents are excluded and counsel must take her answer.

Answer: E
- A: The questioner needs only a good-faith basis, not admissible proof (United States v. Whitmore).
- B: Authentication clears only one hurdle. Rule 608(b) still bars extrinsic evidence offered to prove the lie.
- C: Rule 609 covers convictions. Rule 608(b) covers untruthful acts that didn't result in one.
- D: Contradicting a denial on a matter relevant only to truthfulness would swallow Rule 608(b). The sole purpose here is to attack character for truthfulness.
- E: Correct. Rule 608(b) allows inquiry on cross into acts probative of truthfulness but bars extrinsic evidence to prove them. If the witness denies the act, counsel is stuck with the answer.
Rule: Rule 608(b) permits cross-examination about specific untruthful acts, with a good-faith basis, but bars extrinsic proof when the only purpose is to attack character for truthfulness (United States v. Whitmore).

## Set 1 · Q10 · Impeachment and the Rape Shield — Bias: extrinsic proof allowed
A defense witness in a drug trial denies on cross that the defendant has been paying his rent. The prosecutor then calls the witness's landlord, who testifies that the defendant paid that rent for 14 months. The defense objects. What is the correct ruling?

A. Sustained, because Rule 608(b) bars extrinsic evidence of a witness's specific conduct.
B. Overruled, because bias is not a collateral matter and may be proved extrinsically.
C. Sustained, because the prosecutor is stuck with the witness's answer on cross.
D. Sustained, because bias may be shown only through the biased witness himself.
E. Overruled, because the payments are substantive evidence of the defendant's guilt.

Answer: B
- A: That bar applies only when the sole purpose is to attack character for truthfulness. Proof of bias isn't offered for that purpose (United States v. Abel).
- B: Correct. Under United States v. Abel, bias isn't character evidence and is never collateral, so a witness's denial can be answered with extrinsic proof.
- C: Being stuck with the answer is the rule for Rule 608(b) acts and collateral matters, not bias.
- D: No rule confines bias to cross-examination. United States v. Abel allowed another witness to prove it.
- E: The evidence is admitted because it impeaches the witness. Its relevance here is to credibility.
Rule: Bias may be shown by extrinsic evidence because it isn't collateral. Rule 608(b) bars extrinsic proof only when its sole purpose is to prove character for truthfulness (United States v. Abel).

## Set 1 · Q11 · Impeachment and the Rape Shield — Rule 613(b): 2024 timing of the foundation
A prosecution eyewitness testifies the getaway car was blue. Defense counsel finishes cross without mentioning that the witness told an officer the car was green. The defense then calls the officer to prove that statement. The prosecutor objects. Under the current rules, what is the most likely ruling?

A. Overruled, because Rule 613(b) lets the foundation be laid at any time, even after the officer testifies.
B. Sustained, because a prior inconsistent statement may never be proved by extrinsic evidence.
C. Overruled, because Rule 613(b) applies only to a witness who is also a party.
D. Sustained, because the statement to the officer was not made under penalty of perjury.
E. Sustained, because unless the court orders otherwise the witness must first get to explain or deny.

Answer: E
- A: That was the rule in United States v. Barrett. Since the 2024 amendment, the opportunity must come before extrinsic proof unless the court orders otherwise.
- B: Rule 613(b) allows extrinsic proof on a non-collateral point once the foundation is laid.
- C: This gets the rule backward. Rule 613(b) doesn't apply to an opposing party's statement under Rule 801(d)(2).
- D: An oath matters to substantive use under Rule 801(d)(1)(A). It isn't required to impeach.
- E: Correct. As amended in 2024, Rule 613(b) bars extrinsic evidence of the statement until after the witness has had a chance to explain or deny it, unless the court orders otherwise.
Rule: Since Dec. 1, 2024, Rule 613(b) requires the witness's chance to explain or deny before extrinsic proof of the inconsistent statement, unless the court orders otherwise. Fisher (2022) and United States v. Barrett state the older rule.

## Set 1 · Q12 · Definition of Hearsay — Notice: effect on the listener
A shopper sues a grocery store after slipping on cooking oil. Other witnesses prove the oil was on the floor. To show the store knew about it, the shopper calls Ana, who testifies that ten minutes before the fall she told the store manager, "There is oil all over aisle six." The store objects on hearsay grounds. What is the best ruling?

A. Overruled, because Ana is on the stand and can be cross-examined about her statement.
B. Sustained, because a witness's own earlier words are hearsay whenever she repeats them.
C. Overruled, because the statement is offered to show notice, not that oil was on the floor.
D. Overruled, because a statement made to a party's manager is an opposing party's statement.
E. Sustained, because notice can be proved only by the manager's own admission.

Answer: C
- A: A witness repeating her own out-of-court words is still within Rule 801(c)(1). Being on the stand doesn't answer the objection.
- B: Her own words are hearsay only if offered for their truth. The purpose decides this, under Rule 801(c)(2).
- C: Correct. Under Rule 801(c)(2), a statement isn't hearsay unless it is offered to prove the truth of what it asserts. Here it matters only that the manager heard it.
- D: Rule 801(d)(2) covers statements made by a party or its agents, not statements made to them.
- E: Any evidence that the manager was told supports notice. No rule requires an admission.
Rule: You can't decide whether something is hearsay without knowing the proponent's purpose. A statement offered for its effect on the listener, such as notice, isn't offered for its truth under Rule 801(c)(2).

## Set 1 · Q13 · Definition of Hearsay — Nonassertive conduct
Residents sue a town, claiming its well water was contaminated. To prove the water was safe, the town offers testimony that its chief engineer, alone in the pump house and unaware anyone could see, read the test results, filled a cup from the well, and drank it. The residents object on hearsay grounds. Is the evidence hearsay?

A. Yes, because conduct revealing a person's belief is treated as a statement of that belief.
B. No, because the hearsay rule applies only to spoken and written words.
C. No, because the engineer did not intend the act as an assertion.
D. Yes, because a party can't offer its own employee's conduct for its truth.
E. No, because drinking the water is a verbal act with independent legal significance.

Answer: C
- A: That is the common-law view of Wright v. Doe d. Tatham. Rule 801(a) replaced it by asking whether the person intended an assertion.
- B: Rule 801(a) includes nonverbal conduct if the person intended it as an assertion.
- C: Correct. Under Rule 801(a), conduct is a statement only if intended as an assertion. Alone and unobserved, the engineer wasn't telling anyone anything.
- D: A party can't use Rule 801(d)(2) for its own statements, but there is no statement here to begin with.
- E: A verbal act is words with legal effect, like a contract offer. This conduct has no legal effect of that kind.
Rule: Under Rule 801(a), nonverbal conduct is a statement only if the person intended it as an assertion. Nonassertive conduct isn't hearsay even when offered to show the actor's belief.

## Set 1 · Q14 · Definition of Hearsay — Conduct intended as an assertion
(Varies question 1.13.)
Same facts, except the engineer announced to reporters that the town would prove the water was safe, then drank a cup in front of their cameras. The engineer does not testify, and the town offers a video of the event to prove the water was safe. Is the evidence hearsay?

A. Yes, because the act was meant to assert the water was safe and is offered for that truth.
B. No, because the act was recorded on video and the video speaks for itself.
C. No, because the act is an opposing party's statement offered by the town.
D. Yes, because any act performed in front of a witness counts as a statement.
E. No, because a public demonstration of this kind is a present sense impression rather than an assertion.

Answer: A
- A: Correct. The engineer meant the act to tell the public the water was safe, so it is a statement under Rule 801(a). Offered for that truth, it is hearsay under Rule 801(c).
- B: A recording preserves the out-of-court statement. It doesn't change what the statement is.
- C: Rule 801(d)(2) requires offering the statement against the opposing party. The town is offering its own employee's act.
- D: Rule 801(a) turns on intent to assert, not on whether anyone watched.
- E: A present sense impression is an exception that applies to statements. It can't show that the act wasn't an assertion.
Rule: Conduct meant to communicate is a statement under Rule 801(a). Compare a private act done for one's own assurance with a public act done to assure others.

## Set 1 · Q15 · Opposing Party and Prior Statements — Rule 801(d)(2)(D): no personal knowledge needed
A forklift strikes a visitor at a warehouse. The next day supervisor Vance, who was off site when it happened and heard about it secondhand, emails the company's owner: "Our operator hit a visitor because he was going too fast." Reporting incidents to the owner is part of Vance's job. In the visitor's suit against the company, the visitor offers the email. What is the most likely ruling?

A. Excluded, because Rule 602 requires every declarant to have personal knowledge.
B. Excluded, because a statement to the employee's own employer isn't made to outsiders.
C. Admitted, because it is an employee's statement within the scope of her employment.
D. Admitted only if the company authorized Vance to speak for it to the public.
E. Admitted, because any email kept by a company is a record of regularly conducted activity.

Answer: C
- A: Under Mahlandt, Rule 801(d)(2) has no personal-knowledge requirement.
- B: Mahlandt admitted statements made inside an organization. An opposing party's statement doesn't have to reach outsiders.
- C: Correct. Rule 801(d)(2)(D) covers a statement by a party's employee on a matter within the scope of the relationship while it existed. Mahlandt requires no personal knowledge.
- D: Authority to speak is Rule 801(d)(2)(C). Rule 801(d)(2)(D) needs only the employment relationship and scope.
- E: Rule 803(6) requires a regular practice and a qualified foundation. An email isn't a business record just because a company kept it.
Rule: Rule 801(d)(2)(D) admits an employee's statement against the employer if it concerns a matter within the scope of employment and was made while employed. Personal knowledge isn't required (Mahlandt).

## Set 1 · Q16 · Opposing Party and Prior Statements — Rule 801(d)(2)(D): relationship must exist
(Varies question 1.15.)
Same facts, except the company fired Vance before she sent the email, and she wrote it from home after her employment ended. What is the most likely ruling?

A. Admitted, because the subject of the email falls within her former duties.
B. Excluded, because she was no longer an employee when she made the statement.
C. Admitted, because Rule 801(d)(2) has no timing requirement for agents.
D. Excluded, because a fired employee is presumed to be biased against the company.
E. Admitted as a statement against interest, because it exposed the company to liability.

Answer: B
- A: The subject matter is only half the test. The statement must also be made while the relationship existed.
- B: Correct. Rule 801(d)(2)(D) requires that the statement be made while the employment existed. After she was fired, it isn't the company's statement.
- C: Rule 801(d)(2)(D) expressly says "while it existed."
- D: There is no such presumption. Bias goes to weight, and the failure here is the timing.
- E: Rule 804(b)(3) asks whether the statement was against the declarant's interest and requires unavailability. Neither is shown.
Rule: An employee's statement counts against the employer only if made while the employment existed, under Rule 801(d)(2)(D). Scope alone isn't enough.

## Set 1 · Q17 · Opposing Party and Prior Statements — Rule 801(d)(1)(B): pre-motive statement
A government witness testifies against his former partner. On cross, the defense suggests he invented his story to get the plea deal he signed on March 1. The government offers two consistent statements for their truth: a January letter to his sister and an April interview with an agent. Which is admissible?

A. Both, because each is consistent and the witness is subject to cross-examination.
B. Only the April interview, because it was made to a law enforcement officer.
C. Neither, because Rule 801(d)(1) requires a prior statement given under oath.
D. Only the January letter, because it was made before the alleged motive arose.
E. Both, but only to support credibility and not for their truth.

Answer: D
- A: Being consistent isn't enough. Under Tome, the statement must come before the alleged motive arose.
- B: The audience doesn't matter, and the April statement came after the motive, so it doesn't rebut the charge.
- C: An oath requirement appears only in the pre-amendment Rule 801(d)(1)(A). Rule 801(d)(1)(B) has never required one.
- D: Correct. Rule 801(d)(1)(B)(i) requires rebuttal of a charge of recent fabrication or improper motive. Under Tome, only a statement made before the motive arose does that.
- E: Rule 801(d)(1)(B) makes a qualifying statement substantive evidence, and the April statement doesn't qualify anyway.
Rule: A consistent statement offered to rebut a charge of fabrication or improper motive must predate that motive (Tome, Rule 801(d)(1)(B)(i)). If it qualifies, it is substantive evidence.

## Set 1 · Q18 · Hearsay Exceptions — Rule 805: outsider in a business record
Facts: Wren sues Harbor Ferry Co. after falling on a gangway. Deckhand Ito completed the company's incident log an hour after the fall. Harbor Ferry requires a log entry after every incident, and its claims manager tells deckhands to fill them out "so we have our side on paper if there is a lawsuit." One entry reads: "Passenger Alvarez told me the plaintiff was running when she fell." Another reads: "I checked the gangway chain ten minutes before the fall and it was hooked." Alvarez, a passenger with no tie to the company, cannot be found. Ito has died.
Harbor Ferry offers the first log entry to prove Wren was running. Assume Rule 803(6) covers Ito's entry. What is the most likely ruling?

A. Admitted, because the entire entry is a record of a regularly conducted activity.
B. Admitted, because Alvarez is unavailable and so the exception for his statement is met.
C. Excluded, because a business record may never contain anyone else's statement.
D. Admitted, because Ito had a business duty to record what he was told.
E. Excluded unless Alvarez's statement has its own exception, because he had no business duty.

Answer: E
- A: Rule 803(6) covers the layer supplied by people in the business. It doesn't carry an outsider's statement with it (Johnson v. Lutz).
- B: Unavailability under Rule 804(a) is only a precondition. It isn't itself an exception.
- C: A record can hold an insider's report or an outsider's statement that has its own exception. Rule 805 asks only that each layer qualify.
- D: Ito's duty covers his own layer. The informant must also have a business duty or another exception must apply.
- E: Correct. Under Rule 805, each layer needs its own route. As in Johnson v. Lutz, an outsider has no business duty, so Alvarez's statement needs its own exception.
Rule: Under Rule 805, each layer of hearsay needs its own exception. An outsider's statement in a business record is a separate layer because the outsider has no business duty to report (Johnson v. Lutz).

## Set 1 · Q19 · Hearsay Exceptions — Rule 803(6)(E): prepared with litigation in view
Facts: Wren sues Harbor Ferry Co. after falling on a gangway. Deckhand Ito completed the company's incident log an hour after the fall. Harbor Ferry requires a log entry after every incident, and its claims manager tells deckhands to fill them out "so we have our side on paper if there is a lawsuit." One entry reads: "Passenger Alvarez told me the plaintiff was running when she fell." Another reads: "I checked the gangway chain ten minutes before the fall and it was hooked." Alvarez, a passenger with no tie to the company, cannot be found. Ito has died.
Harbor Ferry offers the second log entry through its records custodian to prove the chain was hooked. What is Wren's strongest objection?

A. The company failed to prove the record is trustworthy, as the proponent must.
B. The custodian did not personally make the entry and can't lay the foundation.
C. The circumstances of its preparation show it is untrustworthy, as in Palmer.
D. A party may never offer its own employee's statement in its favor.
E. Ito's death means the entry can come in only under Rule 804.

Answer: C
- A: Since 2014, the opponent bears the burden of showing untrustworthiness under Rule 803(6)(E).
- B: Rule 803(6)(D) lets a custodian or another qualified witness lay the foundation. The custodian need not have written the entry.
- C: Correct. The stated purpose, to have the company's side on paper for a lawsuit, lets Wren show under Rule 803(6)(E) that the method or circumstances indicate a lack of trustworthiness. Palmer is the ancestor of that clause.
- D: That limit applies to Rule 801(d)(2), which runs only against a party. Rule 803(6) can be used by anyone.
