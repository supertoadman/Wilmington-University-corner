Evidence Comprehensive Practice Questions
=========================================

Subject: Evidence
Type: Practice Questions
Source: https://supertoadman.github.io/Wilmington-University-corner/subjects/evidence/evidence-comprehensive-practice-questions/

150 bar-style questions on all of Weeks 1–15, in five sets of 30, each covering every topic. It keeps the 100 hardest questions from the earlier Weeks 1–5 and hearsay sets, now with five choices each, and adds 50 new questions on the material they never reached: competency, Chambers and Bruton, lay and expert opinion through Daubert, Joiner, and Kumho Tire, authentication and the best evidence rule, and the lawyer-client, psychotherapist, and spousal privileges. It covers jury secrecy, relevance and 403, the policy rules (407–411), character evidence, 404(b), and 413–415, impeachment and the rape shield, hearsay and its exceptions, and the Confrontation Clause, and applies the 2023–2026 amendments, including the December 1, 2026 change to Rule 801(d)(1)(A). Five choices each, and every choice is explained.

## Set 1 · Q1 · Jury Secrecy and Competency — Rule 606(b): extraneous information vs. a juror’s experience
After a defense verdict in a federal products-liability suit over a collapsed extension ladder, the plaintiff moves for a new trial and submits a juror’s affidavit stating: (1) during deliberations Juror 6, a retired carpenter, told the others that in his 30 years on job sites, ladders like this one failed only when overloaded; (2) Juror 2 brought a printout of the ladder’s online customer reviews, which praised its sturdiness, into the jury room and read it aloud; and (3) after hearing the reviews, two jurors changed their votes to find for the defendant. Which parts of the affidavit may the court consider?

A. Items 1 and 2, because both brought information from outside the trial record into deliberations.
B. Items 2 and 3, because once extraneous information is shown, the court needs to know whether it actually changed the outcome.
C. Item 2 only.
D. None of them, because each describes something that happened during deliberations and the Peña-Rodriguez exception is limited to racial bias.
E. All three items, because a new-trial motion requires proof of what the jury actually relied on.

Answer: C
- A: Warger draws the line by the nature of the information, not by whether it was in the record. A juror’s own experience is something jurors bring with them, so it is internal even when it is specific and influential.
- B: The exception opens the door only to whether extraneous information reached the jury. 606(b)(1) still forbids testimony about “the effect of anything on that juror’s or another juror’s vote.” Courts judge the likely effect on a hypothetical average juror instead.
- C: Correct. Online reviews are information from outside the trial that reached the jury improperly, so 606(b)(2)(A) lets a juror testify that it happened. Item 1 is a juror drawing on his own life experience, which is internal (Warger). Item 3 describes the effect of something on jurors’ votes, which 606(b)(1) bars even when an exception applies; the court decides prejudice objectively, without asking jurors how they voted.
- D: 606(b)(2) has its own textual exceptions apart from Peña-Rodriguez. Extraneous prejudicial information, an outside influence, and a mistake on the verdict form may all be proved by juror testimony.
- E: Rule 606(b)(1) bars testimony about any effect on jurors’ votes, so item 3 is out, and Juror 6’s own experience is internal, not extraneous (Warger). The court judges prejudice objectively.
Rule: Rule 606(b) bars juror testimony about deliberations, effects on votes, and mental processes. Exceptions: extraneous prejudicial information, outside influence, a verdict-form mistake (plus Peña-Rodriguez racial bias). A juror’s own experiences are internal (Warger).

## Set 1 · Q2 · Jury Secrecy and Competency — Rule 606(b): misconduct reported before the verdict
On the second day of deliberations in a federal fraud trial, before any verdict, the foreperson sends the judge a note: “Juror 9 smells of alcohol and keeps falling asleep. We can’t deliberate with him.” Defense counsel argues that Rule 606(b) prevents the judge from questioning any juror about what happens in the jury room. What should the judge do?

A. Declare a mistrial at once, because an intoxicated juror denies the defendant a competent jury under the Sixth Amendment.
B. Tell the jury to keep deliberating, because Tanner held that juror intoxication is an internal matter.
C. Question only the foreperson, because Rule 606(b) lets a juror report another juror’s misconduct but not her own.
D. Wait until after the verdict, then decide whether the note shows extraneous prejudicial information.
E. Look into it now, questioning jurors as needed, because 606(b) limits only inquiries into a verdict’s validity.

Answer: E
- A: A mistrial may be unnecessary. The judge can investigate first and replace an impaired juror with an alternate. Nothing requires a mistrial on an unverified note.
- B: Tanner held that juror testimony about intoxication can’t impeach a verdict after it is returned. It pointed to pre-verdict reports like this one as a reason that rule is tolerable.
- C: Rule 606(b) draws no line between testifying about oneself and about others, and it doesn’t apply before a verdict at all. The judge may question whichever jurors she needs to resolve the report.
- D: Waiting would make things worse: after the verdict, 606(b) and Tanner would bar juror testimony about the drinking. The time to act is now.
- E: Correct. 606(b) applies “during an inquiry into the validity of a verdict or indictment.” Before the verdict, the judge may investigate and, if necessary, replace the juror with an alternate. Tanner listed exactly this safeguard: jurors can report misconduct before they return a verdict.
Rule: Rule 606(b) restricts juror testimony only in an inquiry into the validity of a verdict or indictment. Before the verdict, the court can investigate reports of misconduct, one of the safeguards Tanner relied on.

## Set 1 · Q3 · Relevance and Rule 403 — Rule 403: gruesome photos after a concession (State v. Bocharski)
At a murder trial, the defense concedes the victim’s death, its cause, and the number of stab wounds; the only issue is identity. The prosecution offers (1) a crime-scene photograph of the wounds, which the medical examiner uses to explain that their angle shows a left-handed attacker (the defendant is left-handed), and (2) three color autopsy photographs of the victim’s internal organs, which no witness refers to. The defense objects to both under Rule 403. Which ruling is most defensible?

A. Admit photograph 1 and exclude the autopsy photographs.
B. Exclude photograph 1 as cumulative of the medical examiner’s testimony and admit the autopsy photographs.
C. Exclude all of them, because the defense conceded the death, its cause, and the wounds.
D. Admit all of them, because photographs of the victim are always relevant to the fact and cause of death in a murder case.
E. Admit all of them, but instruct the jury not to let the photographs inflame its passions.

Answer: A
- A: Correct. This tracks Bocharski. Concessions lower a photo’s probative value on the conceded points but don’t eliminate its value on contested ones. Photo 1 bears directly on identity, the disputed issue. The autopsy photos, tied to no testimony, serve mainly to inflame, so their slight value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice.
- B: Photo 1 illustrates and supports the examiner’s key inference. If anything is needlessly cumulative or inflammatory here, it’s the autopsy photos.
- C: A concession doesn’t make evidence irrelevant (ACN to 401). Photo 1 is probative of the one disputed issue, the attacker’s identity, through the angle of the wounds.
- D: Relevance isn’t the end of the analysis. Where those facts are conceded and the photos are unexplained, 403 exclusion is appropriate (Bocharski Exhibits 46–47).
- E: An instruction can’t supply probative value the autopsy photographs lack. With the death and wounds conceded and no witness using them, their only effect is emotional, so Rule 403 supports excluding them (State v. Bocharski).
Rule: Rule 403: weigh probative value on contested issues against unfair prejudice. Concessions lower probative value (Bocharski: the angle-and-depth photos came in; the unexplained autopsy photos didn’t).

## Set 1 · Q4 · Relevance and Rule 403 — Rule 104(a) vs. Rule 104(b)
Four preliminary questions come up in a federal drug-conspiracy trial. Which one does the judge decide for herself, by a preponderance of the evidence, without being bound by the rules of evidence other than privilege?

A. Whether the defendant actually signed a letter the government offers against him.
B. Whether the government’s chemist is qualified to testify as an expert.
C. Whether the defendant knew about a threat that supplies his supposed motive.
D. Whether the defendant made an uncharged drug sale that the government offers under Rule 404(b).
E. Whether the defendant wrote a drug ledger found in a co-conspirator’s house.

Answer: B
- A: Authorship goes to authenticity, a conditional-relevance question under Rules 104(b) and 901(a). The judge asks only whether a jury could reasonably find that the defendant signed it.
- B: Correct. 104(a) gives the judge questions about whether “a witness is qualified, a privilege exists, or evidence is admissible.” She decides them herself by a preponderance (Bourjaily) and may consider inadmissible evidence. The other questions ask whether a fact exists on which relevance depends, which 104(b) leaves to the jury, subject only to a sufficiency screen.
- C: Knowledge is a fact on which the relevance of the motive evidence depends. Under 104(b) and Cox, the judge asks only whether a reasonable jury could find it.
- D: Huddleston held that whether the other act happened is a 104(b) question. The judge asks whether a jury could reasonably find it by a preponderance, without deciding it herself.
- E: Authorship is a fact on which the ledger’s relevance depends. The judge decides only whether there is enough evidence for a jury to find that he wrote it.
Rule: Rule 104(a): the judge decides qualification, privilege, and admissibility by a preponderance, unbound by the evidence rules except privilege. 104(b): when relevance depends on a fact, the judge asks only whether a jury could reasonably find it.

## Set 1 · Q5 · Specialized Relevance Rules — Rule 407: product defects and nonparty measures
A rider is burned when an e-bike battery overheats. She sues the manufacturer on a strict-liability design-defect theory. The manufacturer concedes that a thermal cutoff switch was technologically and economically feasible but argues the original design was reasonable. The plaintiff offers (1) evidence that after her accident the manufacturer redesigned the battery to add a thermal cutoff, and (2) evidence that after her accident a national retailer, which isn’t a party, pulled the model from its shelves and posted warnings. How should the court rule?

A. Exclude item 1, but item 2 isn’t barred by Rule 407, though the court may weigh it under Rule 403.
B. Admit item 1, because it shows that a thermal cutoff was a feasible precaution.
C. Exclude item 2 only, because a retailer’s decision reveals nothing about the manufacturer’s design.
D. Admit both, because Rule 407 doesn’t apply to claims brought on a strict-liability theory.
E. Exclude both, because Rule 407 bars every post-accident measure concerning the product.

Answer: A
- A: Correct. Since 1997, 407 expressly covers measures offered to prove a defect in a product or its design, and feasibility isn’t disputed, so the redesign can’t come in that way. Most circuits hold that 407 doesn’t reach measures by non-parties (Millennium Partners), since third parties won’t be deterred from repairing. Their measures say little about this defendant, though, so some courts exclude them under 403.
- B: Feasibility is a permitted purpose only “if disputed.” The manufacturer conceded it, so feasibility can’t serve as a backdoor.
- C: That reverses the analysis. The manufacturer’s own redesign is the measure Rule 407 bars (it has applied to product claims since 1997), while a nonparty’s measure isn’t covered by Rule 407, though Rule 403 may weigh against it.
- D: Some pre-1997 decisions held that, but the amendment added “a defect in a product or its design” and “a need for a warning or instruction” to 407’s list.
- E: 407’s rationale, encouraging the defendant to repair, has little force for non-parties, and most courts don’t apply it to them.
Rule: Rule 407 applies to product-defect claims (1997 amendment). Feasibility is a permitted purpose only if disputed. Most circuits: non-party measures aren’t barred by 407, though 403 still applies.

## Set 1 · Q6 · Specialized Relevance Rules — Rule 408: no impeachment, but bias is allowed
During settlement talks in a product suit, the defendant's chief engineer says, “Between us, the brake design was rushed.” At trial he testifies the design was thoroughly tested. Separately, a former co-defendant settled with the plaintiff for $200,000 and then testified for the plaintiff. Which use is permitted?

A. The co-defendant's settlement may show bias, but the engineer can't be impeached with his statement.
B. Both uses are permitted, because impeachment is not an attempt to prove the claim's validity.
C. The engineer may be impeached, because he is a witness rather than a party to the negotiations.
D. The engineer may be impeached, because a side remark made “between us” wasn't part of the formal negotiations.
E. Neither use is permitted, because 408 bars all evidence of compromise for every purpose.

Answer: A
- A: Correct. 408(a) bars impeachment by prior inconsistent statement (since 2006), while 408(b) allows proof of a witness's bias or prejudice.
- B: 408 expressly bars impeachment by prior inconsistent statement or contradiction.
- C: The statement was made during the party's compromise negotiations; the bar applies.
- D: A remark made during settlement talks is a statement made during compromise negotiations, however informal. Rule 408(a) bars using it to impeach by a prior inconsistent statement or contradiction.
- E: 408(b) allows other purposes such as bias.
Rule: Rule 408(a) bars compromise offers and statements made during negotiations when offered to prove or disprove a disputed claim or to impeach by a prior inconsistent statement or contradiction. Rule 408(b) allows them for another purpose, such as proving a witness's bias.

## Set 1 · Q7 · Specialized Relevance Rules — Mezzanatto: a waiver triggered by argument
Before a proffer session, a defendant signs an agreement allowing the government to use his statements "to rebut any evidence or argument offered by or on behalf of the defendant that is inconsistent with them." In the session he admits he was at the warehouse on the night of the theft. At trial he does not testify, but in opening statement his lawyer tells the jury that the defendant "was never at that warehouse." What should the court do with the proffer statements?

A. Admit them to rebut the argument, because Rule 410's protections may be waived by a knowing and voluntary agreement.
B. Admit them only in a later perjury prosecution, because Rule 410 allows plea statements to be used only in that setting.
C. Exclude them, because Rule 410 reflects a public policy favoring plea bargaining that defendants cannot waive.
D. Exclude them, because a lawyer's opening statement isn't evidence and so can't trigger the waiver.
E. Exclude them, because a waiver can be enforced only to impeach a defendant who testifies inconsistently on the stand.

Answer: A
- A: Correct. Mezzanatto upheld waivers of 410. Many circuits enforce rebuttal waivers like this one, triggered by defense argument and not only by the defendant's testimony.
- B: This confuses the 410(b)(2) exception with the separate route of waiver by agreement.
- C: Mezzanatto rejected the claim that the rule cannot be waived.
- D: The agreement covers “evidence or argument offered by or on behalf of the defendant,” and courts enforce such waivers when defense counsel's opening contradicts the proffer.
- E: The waiver's text reaches defense arguments as well. Mezzanatto approved an impeachment waiver, and courts have since enforced broader ones.
Rule: Rule 410's protections may be waived by a knowing and voluntary agreement (United States v. Mezzanatto). Courts enforce proffer agreements that let the government rebut inconsistent evidence or argument offered for the defendant.

## Set 1 · Q8 · Character Evidence — Rule 415: civil sexual-assault cases
A client sues a licensed massage therapist for battery, alleging he sexually assaulted her during a session. Twenty days before trial, she discloses that she will call two other former clients, who will testify that he assaulted them in the same way during sessions. Neither reported it to police, and he was never charged. He moves to exclude their testimony. How should the court rule?

A. Admit it automatically, because Congress has decided that propensity evidence in sexual-assault cases outweighs any prejudice.
B. Exclude it unless the other assaults resulted in convictions or at least formal charges.
C. Admit it under Rule 415 for any relevant purpose, including propensity, subject to ordinary Rule 403 balancing.
D. Exclude it, because Rules 413 through 415 apply only in criminal prosecutions, where the defendant’s liberty is at stake.
E. Exclude it, because the plaintiff disclosed the testimony fewer than 30 days before trial.

Answer: C
- A: Rule 403 still applies; that is why Mound upheld Rule 413 against a due-process attack. Guardia requires a normal 403 weighing, neither tilted for nor against admission.
- B: Rep. Molinari’s floor statement says the past offense need not have produced a conviction. The Huddleston preponderance-sufficiency standard governs.
- C: Correct. 415 applies Rules 413 and 414 in civil cases based on sexual assault or child molestation (Carroll v. Trump is a recent example). No charge or conviction is needed; the threshold is presumably Huddleston’s. Disclosure at least 15 days before trial was satisfied. Under Guardia, the 403 balance is the ordinary one.
- D: Rule 415 exists specifically for civil cases involving a claim of sexual assault or child molestation.
- E: Rule 415(b) requires disclosure at least 15 days before trial, or later for good cause. Twenty days is timely.
Rule: Rule 413/414/415: in sexual-assault and child-molestation cases (415 for civil cases), the party’s other such acts are admissible for any relevant purpose, including propensity. No conviction is needed, ordinary 403 applies, and disclosure is due 15 days before trial.

## Set 1 · Q9 · Character Evidence — United States v. Guardia: an ordinary Rule 403 balance
In a federal prosecution of a physician for sexually abusing two patients during examinations, the government gives timely notice that it will offer four other patients’ testimony that he abused them the same way. The district judge excludes the testimony, explaining: “Evidence of other sexual assaults is always intensely prejudicial, so in Rule 413 cases I start with a strong presumption against admission.” The government appeals. What should the appellate court conclude?

A. The judge erred, because Rule 413 evidence gets an ordinary Rule 403 balance, with no presumption against it.
B. The judge was right, because Rule 413 requires that the other assaults have resulted in convictions.
C. The judge was right, because Rule 413 evidence is presumptively inadmissible absent special need.
D. The judge was right, because Rule 413 evidence must be more probative than prejudicial before it comes in.
E. The judge erred, because Rule 403 doesn’t apply to evidence offered under Rule 413.

Answer: A
- A: Correct. Guardia sets a threshold (sexual-assault charge, evidence of another sexual assault, relevance) and then requires a normal 403 weighing. Treating propensity as inherently too prejudicial would frustrate Congress’s purpose. In Guardia itself, exclusion was affirmed because of a specific risk of confusion: four more incidents with dueling experts on medical propriety. A judge may exclude for reasons like that, but not by presumption.
- B: Uncharged acts qualify. The standard of proof is presumably Huddleston’s, whether a jury could reasonably find the acts occurred.
- C: No such presumption exists. The rule says the court “may admit” the evidence “on any matter to which it is relevant,” including propensity.
- D: That reverses Rule 403’s test. Rule 413 evidence gets the ordinary balance: it is excluded only if unfair prejudice substantially outweighs probative value, with no presumption either way (United States v. Guardia).
- E: 403 applies to 413 evidence. Mound relied on that safeguard to uphold the rule against a due-process challenge.
Rule: Guardia: the 413 threshold (accused of sexual assault, evidence of another, relevance), then an ordinary 403 balance with no thumb on the scale.

## Set 1 · Q10 · Character Evidence — Rule 405(b): self-defense isn’t an element
A defendant charged with assault claims he punched the alleged victim in self-defense. He offers testimony from two strangers that the victim beat each of them in separate bar fights last year. The defendant didn’t know about either fight when he threw the punch. Under which theory, if any, is the testimony admissible?

A. Rule 405(b), because a claim of self-defense puts the victim’s violent character directly in issue as an element.
B. Rule 404(a)(2)(B), which allows a defendant to prove the victim’s violent character by specific acts.
C. Rule 404(b), to show the victim’s intent to attack the defendant.
D. None. Self-defense doesn’t make the victim’s character an essential element, and he didn’t know of the fights.
E. Rule 406, because two bar fights in one year show the victim’s habit of violence.

Answer: D
- A: The rule says “person,” and some argue the victim’s character is an element, but the element of self-defense is the defendant’s reasonable belief. Character is at most circumstantial.
- B: 404(a)(2)(B) allows the trait to be offered, but 405(a) controls the method: reputation or opinion on direct, specific instances only on cross.
- C: Two unrelated bar fights show the victim’s intent only through propensity, the inference Rule 404(b)(1) bars. And since the defendant didn’t know of them, they can’t explain his fear either.
- D: Correct. Under 404(a)(2)(B) the defendant may offer the victim’s violent trait, but 405(a) limits him to reputation or opinion. 405(b) allows specific instances only when the trait is an essential element, and self-defense turns on the defendant’s reasonable fear, not on the victim’s actual character. Because he didn’t know of the fights, there’s no state-of-mind route either (contrast James, where the records corroborated what the victim had told her). Offering even reputation evidence would open the door to his own violent character under 404(a)(2)(B)(ii).
- E: Two fights aren’t a habit. Habit is a regular, semi-automatic response to a repeated situation, not a general disposition toward violence.
Rule: Rule 405(b) allows specific acts only when character is an essential element (entrapment, truth in defamation, negligent entrustment, custody), not in self-defense. The victim’s violence comes in by reputation or opinion under 404(a)(2)(B).

## Set 1 · Q11 · Character Evidence — Rule 406: habit vs. general carefulness
A driver is killed when a train hits her car at a rural crossing with no gates. In her estate’s wrongful-death suit, the railroad offers (1) a coworker’s testimony that, riding with her to work twice a day for five years, he saw her cross those tracks hundreds of times without ever slowing or looking, and (2) her sister’s testimony that she was “a careless, distracted driver in general.” No one saw the crash. Which testimony is admissible to show how she drove that day?

A. Both, because in a wrongful-death case the decedent’s carefulness is an essential element under Rule 405(b).
B. Item 2 only, because a decedent’s general carefulness is the best evidence of how she drove that day.
C. Neither, because character evidence can’t be used to prove conduct in a civil case.
D. Item 1 only, as evidence of habit.
E. Item 1 only, but only because no eyewitness saw the crash.

Answer: D
- A: Carefulness is relevant to negligence, but character isn’t an essential element of the claim, as it would be in negligent entrustment or defamation.
- B: General carelessness is a character trait, and Rule 404(a)(1) bars it to prove conduct in a civil case. The coworker’s specific, repeated observations are habit under Rule 406.
- C: That is true of item 2, but habit isn’t character. 406 is a separate rule that applies in civil and criminal cases.
- D: Correct. 406 admits a person’s habit to prove conduct on a particular occasion. Habit is a specific, regular, semi-automatic response to a repeated situation, like crossing the same tracks the same way hundreds of times (compare Halloran’s regular use of a coil). Item 2 is a general trait offered to prove conduct in conformity, which 404(a)(1) bars.
- E: 406 says habit evidence may be admitted “regardless of whether it is corroborated or whether there was an eyewitness.” The lack of an eyewitness isn’t a requirement.
Rule: Rule 406: habit (a specific, regular, semi-automatic response) is admissible to prove conduct, with or without an eyewitness or corroboration. General traits are 404(a) character.

## Set 1 · Q12 · Impeachment and the Rape Shield — Rule 609(b): the clock runs from release
A defendant is on trial in 2026 for aggravated assault. If he testifies, the government wants to impeach him with his 2013 felony conviction for aggravated assault. He was released from prison in 2019. Which statement about the conviction’s admissibility is correct?

A. Rule 609(a)(1)(B) applies, and the crimes’ similarity makes the conviction more probative of truthfulness.
B. Rule 609(a)(1)(B) applies, because the ten years run from his 2019 release, and the crimes’ similarity weighs against admission.
C. The conviction must be admitted without balancing, because a violent felony bears on whether the defendant will respect his oath.
D. Rule 609(b) applies because the conviction is more than ten years old, so the government must show probative value substantially outweighing prejudice.
E. The conviction must be admitted, subject only to Rule 403, because aggravated assault is punishable by more than a year.

Answer: B
- A: Similarity cuts the other way. It raises the risk that the jury will use the conviction as propensity evidence, so it weighs against admission under the Gordon factors.
- B: Correct. 609(b)’s ten-year clock runs from the conviction or release from confinement, whichever is later (Brewer), so 609(b) doesn’t apply. Under 609(a)(1)(B), probative value on truthfulness must outweigh prejudice to the defendant. Among the Gordon factors, violent crimes say little about honesty, and a conviction for the same crime invites “he did it before” reasoning, so both point toward exclusion.
- C: Only 609(a)(2) crimes, those requiring proof of a dishonest act or false statement, come in automatically. Assault isn’t one of them.
- D: That was Brewer’s losing argument. The later of conviction or release starts the clock, and release in 2019 puts the conviction within ten years.
- E: 403’s lenient standard under 609(a)(1)(A) is for witnesses other than the accused. A testifying defendant gets the stricter 609(a)(1)(B) balance.
Rule: Rule 609(b)’s ten years run from the later of conviction or release (Brewer). For an accused, 609(a)(1)(B) applies, weighed with the Gordon factors: nature of the crime, time and later history, similarity, importance of the defendant’s testimony, and centrality of credibility.

## Set 1 · Q13 · Impeachment and the Rape Shield — Rule 608(a): witnesses only
In a civil real-estate fraud suit, the plaintiff never takes the stand; her case rests on the closing documents and her broker’s testimony. The defense calls a witness who testifies that the plaintiff “has a terrible reputation for truthfulness around town.” The plaintiff objects. How should the court rule?

A. Overrule the objection, because reputation testimony is always admissible in a fraud case.
B. Overrule the objection, because Rule 608 lets a party in a civil case attack the opposing party’s reputation for truthfulness.
C. Overrule the objection, because a fraud plaintiff’s honesty is an essential element of her claim under Rule 405(b).
D. Sustain the objection, because Rule 608 applies only in criminal cases.
E. Sustain it, because Rule 608(a) applies only to witnesses, and the plaintiff hasn’t testified.

Answer: E
- A: Character isn’t an essential element of a fraud claim, so 405(b) doesn’t apply, and 404(a)(1) bars character offered to show she acted dishonestly. Fraud cases have no special character rule.
- B: That was counsel’s sloppy statement of the rule in the Easerly clip. 608 applies in civil and criminal cases, to any witness, but not to a party who never takes the stand.
- C: A fraud plaintiff’s own truthfulness isn’t an element of her claim; the defendant’s alleged fraud is. And Rule 608(a) reaches only witnesses and hearsay declarants.
- D: 608 applies in civil and criminal cases alike, unlike the 404(a)(2) exceptions.
- E: Correct. 608(a) speaks of “a witness’s credibility.” Character for truthfulness matters only when a person’s testimony is before the jury, and being a party doesn’t make the plaintiff a witness. Offered against a party who hasn’t testified, the evidence is just character offered to prove conduct, barred by 404(a)(1). That was the point of the Easerly v. Lettwin clip.
Rule: Rule 608(a): character for truthfulness may be attacked by reputation or opinion, in any case, but only for someone who has testified (or whose out-of-court words are admitted).

## Set 1 · Q14 · Impeachment and the Rape Shield — False accusations fall outside Rule 412
A defendant is charged with molesting his 11-year-old stepdaughter, and there is no physical evidence. The defense wants to cross-examine her about an accusation she made two years ago that a music teacher touched her, which a classmate says she later admitted she “made up to get out of lessons.” The prosecution moves to exclude the cross-examination under the rape-shield rule. How should the court analyze the motion?

A. Rule 412 applies, but the cross-examination fits its exception for another source of injury.
B. The defense may cross-examine and, if she denies making it up, may call the classmate to prove the lie under Rule 608(b).
C. Rule 412 doesn’t apply; the court asks whether a jury could find the accusation false, then applies Rules 403 and 608.
D. The defense may cross-examine only if the judge first finds by a preponderance that the earlier accusation was false.
E. Rule 412 bars the cross-examination, because the earlier accusation concerned sexual contact.

Answer: C
- A: Rule 412 doesn’t apply at all, because a false accusation isn’t sexual behavior (State v. Smith). And the source-of-injury exception concerns physical evidence, not credibility.
- B: 608(b) permits inquiry on cross but bars extrinsic evidence of specific instances offered to attack character for truthfulness. The defense must take her answer unless some other theory, such as bias, applies.
- C: Correct. State v. Smith held that past false allegations aren’t “past sexual behavior,” so the shield rule doesn’t bar them; the ACN to FRE 412 agrees. The question is impeachment, governed by 608(b) and 403. The threshold is Huddleston’s: whether reasonable jurors could find she made a false accusation, not whether the judge believes it was false (Problem 5.4).
- D: That is what the Smith trial judge effectively did, and it was error. The judge doesn’t decide falsity; she asks only whether a jury could reasonably find it.
- E: 412 bars evidence offered to prove a victim’s other sexual behavior or predisposition. A false accusation is offered to show she lies about such matters, not that she engaged in sexual behavior.
Rule: Prior false accusations aren’t “sexual behavior” (Smith; ACN), so they fall outside 412 and are governed by 608(b) and 403. Threshold: could a jury reasonably find the accusation false?

## Set 1 · Q15 · Definition of Hearsay — Rule 801(a): machine output isn't a statement
Kemper is prosecuted for driving under the influence. A hospital's blood analyzer tested his sample and automatically printed a report reading “Ethanol: 0.19 g/dL.” No person entered or interpreted the number. The prosecution offers the printout through a lab supervisor, who testifies that the machine was calibrated that morning and is regularly used and relied on. Kemper objects that the printout is hearsay.

How should the court rule?

A. Sustain the objection, because the analyzer can't be cross-examined about how it reached its result.
B. Overrule the objection, because the printout is a recorded recollection the supervisor may read to the jury.
C. Sustain the objection, because a result produced for a criminal case is barred by Rule 803(8).
D. Sustain the objection unless the printout qualifies as a business record under Rule 803(6).
E. Overrule the objection, because a machine's output is not a statement by a person, so it is not hearsay.

Answer: E
- A: Cross-examination tests a person's perception, memory, narration, and sincerity. A machine has none of these; errors in its process are tested through the authentication foundation and cross-examination of the supervisor.
- B: Rule 803(5) covers a record of a witness's own once-known knowledge. The supervisor never knew the result except from the printout, and no hearsay exception is needed anyway.
- C: Rule 803(8)'s law-enforcement limits apply to public records, which are hearsay statements. A machine's output isn't a statement at all, and a hospital isn't a law-enforcement agency.
- D: Rule 803(6) is needed only for statements. The hearsay rule applies to a “statement” by a “declarant,” and Rule 801(a)–(b) define both in terms of a person.
- E: Correct. Hearsay is a person's assertion (Rule 801(a)–(c)). A reading generated by a machine without human input is not a statement, so it isn't hearsay. Rule: Hearsay requires a statement by a person (Rule 801(a)–(c)). Machine-generated data with no human assertion is not hearsay, and its reliability is an authentication question under Rule 901(b)(9).

## Set 1 · Q16 · Opposing Party and Prior Statements — Rule 801(d)(2)(D): a fired employee’s statement
Paloma sues Ridgeline Freight, Inc. and its former driver, Hatch, for injuries suffered when a Ridgeline tractor-trailer that Hatch was driving on Ridgeline business ran a red light and struck her car. Ridgeline fired Hatch the morning after the crash. That afternoon, a newspaper reporter visited Hatch at home, and Hatch said: “Look, it was on me. I was texting dispatch and never saw the light change.”

Hatch and Ridgeline are tried together. Paloma calls the reporter to repeat Hatch’s statement. Both defendants object.

How should the court rule?

A. Admit it against both defendants, because the statement concerned a matter, the operation of the truck, that was within the scope of Hatch’s employment.
B. Admit it against both defendants as a statement against interest, because it exposed Hatch to civil liability.
C. Exclude it entirely, because by the time Hatch spoke he had been fired, so the statement could not have been against his interest when made.
D. Admit it against Hatch only and, on request, instruct the jury not to consider it against Ridgeline.
E. Exclude it as to Hatch, because a party’s statement is admissible only if made on personal knowledge, and a driver who “never saw” the light change cannot know when it changed.

Answer: D
- A: The scope-of-employment element is satisfied, but the timing element is not. Rule 801(d)(2)(D) requires the statement to be made while the employment relationship existed, and Hatch had already been fired.

## Set 1 · Q17 · Opposing Party and Prior Statements — Rule 801(d)(2)(E): a post-arrest confession

Independent evidence, including phone records and cash deposits, shows that Kell and Lomax conspired until Kell's arrest. The government offers Kell's interview against Lomax.

How should the court rule?

A. Exclude it, because invoking the privilege doesn't make Kell unavailable when the government could immunize him.
B. Admit it as a co-conspirator statement, because independent evidence shows the conspiracy and Lomax's membership in it.
C. Admit it, because under Bourjaily the judge may rely on the statement itself to find that it furthered the conspiracy.
D. Admit it as a statement against Kell's penal interest, because Kell is unavailable and admitted selling cocaine.
E. Exclude it, because a statement to agents after arrest was not made during and in furtherance of the conspiracy.

## Set 1 · Q22 · Confrontation, Bruton, and the Defense — Arbitrariness: Rock, Scheffer, Egelhoff, and Holmes
Each rule below was applied to exclude evidence offered by a criminal defendant. Which application is most likely to violate the defendant's constitutional right to present a defense?

A. A per se rule barring hypnotically refreshed testimony, applied to keep the defendant from describing what she now remembers.
B. A rule excluding third-party-guilt evidence that raises only a bare suspicion, applied to a vague rumor about another man.
C. A statute making voluntary intoxication irrelevant to mental state, applied to exclude evidence that he was drunk.
D. A per se rule barring polygraph evidence, applied to exclude a favorable polygraph result the defendant offered.
E. Rule 403, applied to exclude the defendant's fourth alibi witness as cumulative of the other three.

Answer: A
- A: Correct. Under the arbitrariness test of Washington v. Texas, Rock, and Holmes, a rule is unconstitutional if it infringes a weighty interest of the accused and is arbitrary or disproportionate to its purposes. Rock v. Arkansas held that a per se bar on a defendant's own hypnotically refreshed testimony is arbitrary, because it excludes all of it without asking whether this testimony is reliable.
- B: Holmes v. South Carolina approved ordinary third-party-guilt rules that exclude evidence raising only a bare suspicion. What it struck down was judging the defense evidence solely by the strength of the prosecution's case.
- C: Montana v. Egelhoff upheld such a statute. A state may decide that voluntary intoxication is irrelevant to mental state, which makes the evidence immaterial rather than arbitrarily excluded.
- D: United States v. Scheffer upheld a per se ban on polygraph evidence: given the scientific disagreement about its reliability, the ban is neither arbitrary nor disproportionate to its purposes.
- E: Holmes reaffirmed that rules like Rule 403, which exclude evidence whose probative value is outweighed by dangers such as needless cumulation, are well within constitutional limits.
Rule: A rule violates the right to present a defense if it infringes a weighty interest of the accused and is arbitrary or disproportionate to its purposes (Washington v. Texas; Rock v. Arkansas; Holmes). Per se polygraph bans (Scheffer), bare-suspicion rules, and Egelhoff-type statutes survive.

D. Introduce the confession unredacted, because Ash’s confession interlocks with a confession that Bly also gave.

Rule: Under the federal common law that Rule 501 applies, client identity and fee arrangements are generally not privileged. They are protected only when disclosing them would reveal the substance of a confidential communication (In re Osterhoudt; Baird v. Koerner).

## Set 1 · Q30 · Privileges — Crime-fraud: advice about past acts vs. future crimes
Pharmacist Omar Stone consults a lawyer and asks, in confidence, two questions: (1) whether he could be prosecuted for opioid prescriptions he filled last year without checking the prescribers, and (2) how he could structure cash deposits from future sales so the bank wouldn't file reports. The lawyer answers both, not realizing that Stone plans to keep selling illegally. A grand jury later subpoenas the lawyer.

What must the lawyer disclose?

A. Both conversations, because once a client discusses crimes, the privilege is forfeited for the whole consultation.
B. Nothing, because the lawyer didn't know of Stone's plan, and the exception requires the lawyer's knowing participation.
C. Nothing, until the government proves beyond a reasonable doubt that Stone committed a crime.
D. The conversation about last year's prescriptions, because the privilege doesn't protect admissions of completed crimes.
E. The conversation about future deposits, but not the one about last year's prescriptions.

Answer: E
- A: The exception applies communication by communication. Seeking advice about past conduct remains fully protected, even in the same meeting.
- B: The exception turns on the client's purpose. A client who seeks advice to further a crime or fraud loses the privilege for that communication even if the lawyer is unaware of the plan.
- C: Zolin set a lower threshold for in camera review, and lower courts use standards such as a prima facie showing or probable cause to establish the exception. Proof beyond a reasonable doubt isn't required.
- D: That reverses the rule. Confidential discussion of past crimes, to get legal advice, is exactly what the privilege protects.
- E: Correct. The privilege's purpose ceases where the advice sought concerns future wrongdoing rather than past wrongdoing (Wigmore, quoted in United States v. Zolin). Asking how to avoid bank reporting on future illegal proceeds seeks help with a future crime, so that conversation isn't privileged. Asking about exposure for last year's conduct is the heart of the privilege.
Rule: The crime-fraud exception removes protection from communications made to obtain advice or help in committing a future or ongoing crime or fraud. Communications about past crimes remain privileged, and the client's purpose controls (United States v. Zolin).

## Set 2 · Q3 · Relevance and Rule 403 — People v. Collins: invented, dependent probabilities
In a robbery trial, witnesses saw the robber flee in a green pickup with a ladder rack, driven by a man with a red beard and a neck tattoo. The prosecution calls a statistics professor who, using estimates he says are “reasonable” (one in 10 trucks is green, one in 20 has a ladder rack, one in 15 men has a red beard, one in 25 has a neck tattoo), multiplies the figures and testifies that there is only a 1-in-75,000 chance that a random man would match. The defendant matches. What is the defense’s best objection?

A. The professor isn’t qualified, because only a forensic scientist may testify about how often physical traits occur.
B. The testimony is irrelevant because the defendant has no burden to prove someone else committed the crime.
C. None, because experts may rely on reasonable estimates, and any weakness goes to weight, not admissibility.
D. The figures are invented and may not be independent, and they invite the jury to equate a rare description with guilt.
E. Statistical evidence offered to prove identity is inadmissible as a matter of law after People v. Collins.

Answer: D
- A: A statistician may well be qualified to do the arithmetic. The defects are in the inputs, which are invented and not independent, and in the leap from a rare description to guilt (People v. Collins).
- B: That confuses burdens with relevance. The real issues are foundation and the misleading leap from a description’s rarity to the probability of guilt.

Rule: Collins: probability evidence needs a foundation (real data, independent factors) and can’t turn a description’s rarity into a probability of guilt. Probabilistic evidence isn’t barred per se (DNA).

## Set 2 · Q4 · Relevance and Rule 403 — Spark-of-life evidence vs. the victim’s character

Which ruling is most consistent with the rules as discussed in class?

B. Both are admissible under the “spark-of-life” doctrine, which lets the prosecution humanize the victim.

E. Item 1 is within the court’s Rule 403 discretion, but item 2 is improper character evidence.

## Set 2 · Q5 · Specialized Relevance Rules — Rule 407: “subsequent” means after the injury
A tenant is hurt when her apartment building’s elevator drops two floors. She sues the landlord for negligence. Which of the following may she offer to prove the landlord was negligent?

A. An inspector’s report, delivered to the landlord three months before the accident, recommending new elevator brakes.
B. A new building policy, adopted after the accident, requiring monthly elevator inspections.
C. A warning sign the landlord posted in the elevator after the accident, offered to show that a warning was needed.
D. Testimony that the landlord fired the elevator-maintenance company the day after the accident.
E. The landlord’s replacement of the brake assembly the week after the accident.

Answer: A
- A: Correct. 407 covers measures taken after the injury that “would have made an earlier injury or harm less likely to occur.” A report the landlord received before the accident isn’t a subsequent measure at all. It shows notice of the danger, and 407 has nothing to say about it.
- B: Policy changes count as measures too. 407 isn’t limited to physical repairs.
- C: 407 expressly bars subsequent measures offered to prove “a need for a warning or instruction.”
- D: Firing the contractor after the accident is a measure that would have made the injury less likely, so Rule 407 bars it to prove negligence.
- E: This is the classic subsequent remedial measure. It can’t prove negligence, though it might come in to impeach or to prove disputed ownership, control, or feasibility.
Rule: Rule 407 applies only to measures taken after the injury. Pre-accident reports, inspections, and recommendations aren’t covered and may show notice.

## Set 2 · Q6 · Specialized Relevance Rules — Rule 408: a report prepared for the talks

## Set 2 · Q8 · Character Evidence — Identity: a common method isn’t a signature
A defendant is charged with robbing a convenience store at knifepoint. The clerk’s identification is shaky, and the defense is misidentification. The prosecution offers the defendant’s conviction, four years earlier, for robbing a gas station at knifepoint, arguing that it proves identity. How should the court rule?

A. Admit it, because identity is one of the exceptions to the propensity ban listed in Rule 404(b)(2), and identity is disputed here.
B. Admit it, because identity is disputed and both crimes were armed robberies of small stores committed with knives.
C. Admit it, because a conviction is reliable proof that the earlier robbery happened.
D. Admit it, because four years is recent enough that the earlier robbery stays probative of identity.
E. Exclude it, because a knifepoint robbery is too common a method to show that one person committed both crimes.

Answer: E
- A: The 404(b)(2) purposes are not exceptions. They are non-character routes to relevance, and a chain that runs through the propensity box is still barred (Zackowitz; Kirsch).
- B: A dispute over identity makes identity a proper purpose but doesn’t make every similar crime probative of it. The likeness must be idiosyncratic, not generic.
- C: Reliability of proof answers the Huddleston question, whether the other act happened. It doesn’t supply a non-propensity reason the act is relevant.
- D: Recency doesn’t create signature-level similarity. A knifepoint robbery is too common a method to show the same person did both, so the inference runs through propensity.
- E: Correct. Identity through modus operandi requires similarities so distinctive that the crime “could not be anyone else’s” (Trenkler). A single prosaic commonality like a knife isn’t enough (Pisari). Without a signature, the only route to identity is that he robbed before, so he robbed again, which is the forbidden inference.
Rule: Rule 404(b) identity (modus operandi) requires signature-level similarity, not prosaic commonalities (Trenkler vs. Pisari). The listed purposes aren’t exceptions.

A. It is a statement against interest, because Fenwick is unavailable and characterizing the transfer as a loan exposed him to tax liability on the interest.
B. It is an opposing-party statement, because Fenwick’s estate is a party to the action and Fenwick’s statements are attributable to it.
C. It is admissible under the residual exception, because no specific exception applies and the statement was made at the moment of the transfer.
D. It is admissible under Rule 803(3) as a statement of Fenwick’s memory of the terms on which he had agreed to transfer the money.
E. It is not hearsay, because Fenwick’s words accompanying the transfer are legally operative, whether or not they are true.

Answer: E
- A: Calling the transfer a loan created a $60,000 asset for Fenwick, which is in his pecuniary favor. Speculative tax consequences do not make it a statement a reasonable person would make only if true.
- B: Rule 801(d)(2) admits statements only when offered against the party. Here the executor, standing in Fenwick’s shoes, is offering Fenwick’s statement for itself.
- C: Rule 807 is a last resort that requires notice and special findings. There is no need to reach it when the words are not hearsay.
- D: Rule 803(3) expressly excludes statements of memory or belief offered to prove the fact remembered. Fenwick was also not describing a memory; he was characterizing the transfer as it happened.
- E: Correct. Words that accompany and characterize a transaction are legally operative and are not offered for their truth.
Rule: Words that have independent legal significance, sometimes called verbal acts or legally operative words, are not hearsay. Examples include the words of an offer or acceptance, a defamatory statement in a defamation case, and words that accompany and characterize a transfer of property. They are offered because they were said, not to prove the truth of anything asserted.

## Set 2 · Q16 · Opposing Party and Prior Statements — Rule 801(d)(1)(B)(ii): rebutting a memory attack
Lucia Reyes, the only eyewitness to a hit-and-run, testifies that the car was a silver hatchback. On cross-examination, defense counsel brings out that it was dark, that two years have passed, and that Reyes is now “filling in” details she can't really remember. Counsel doesn't suggest that Reyes has any motive to lie. The prosecution then offers a recorded call Reyes made to a friend two hours after the crash, in which she said the car was a silver hatchback.

How should the court rule?

A. Exclude it, because a prior consistent statement is admissible only if it was given under penalty of perjury.
B. Admit it as substantive evidence, because any prior consistent statement of a testifying witness is not hearsay.
C. Admit it as substantive evidence under Rule 801(d)(1)(B)(ii), because it rebuts the attack on her memory.
D. Admit it only to rehabilitate Reyes's credibility, with an instruction that it is not evidence of the car's type.
E. Exclude it, because under Tome a consistent statement is admissible only if made before a motive to fabricate arose.

Answer: C
- A: Only the old Rule 801(d)(1)(A), for inconsistent statements, had an oath requirement, and the December 1, 2026 amendment removed it. Rule 801(d)(1)(B) never required one.
- B: A consistent statement is not hearsay only when it rebuts a charge of fabrication or improper motive (clause (i)) or rehabilitates against another kind of attack (clause (ii)). Without an attack, offering it would be improper bolstering.

- E: Tome's premotive rule applies to clause (i), statements offered to rebut a charge of recent fabrication or improper motive. The defense attacked memory, not motive, so clause (ii) applies.
Rule: Rule 801(d)(1)(B): a testifying declarant's prior consistent statement is not hearsay if offered (i) to rebut a charge of recent fabrication or improper influence or motive (made before the motive arose, under Tome) or (ii) to rehabilitate credibility attacked on another ground, such as faulty memory.

Corwin was hired to sell the property and earn a commission; another firm did the legal work. As in People v. Gionis, the question is whether the lawyer was consulted in his professional capacity, and here he wasn't.
Rule: The lawyer-client privilege protects only confidential communications made to obtain professional legal services. A lawyer acting as a business agent, broker, or friend isn't acting in a professional capacity (People v. Gionis).

## Set 2 · Q30 · Privileges — Trammel and marital confidences together
Marcus Reed is charged with smuggling. His wife, Maya, agrees to testify for the government in exchange for immunity. She plans to describe (1) seeing Marcus unload packages from a boat at night, (2) his remark to her and his business partner at dinner that the shipments “pay for the house,” and (3) his whispered statement to her alone in their bedroom that the packages held heroin. Marcus invokes both spousal privileges.

What may Maya testify to?

A. Items 1 and 3, because a confession of a crime is never a protected marital communication.
B. Nothing, because a defendant may bar his spouse's adverse testimony in a criminal case.
C. Items 1 and 2, but not item 3.
D. Item 1 only, because both of Marcus's statements were made during the marriage.
E. All three, because Maya, as the witness-spouse, holds every marital privilege and has chosen to waive them.

Answer: C
- A: A confession of a crime made in confidence is a protected marital communication. The joint-crime exception would apply only if Maya were taking part in the crime with him, which the facts don't show.
- B: That was the rule of Hawkins v. United States. Trammel modified it: in federal court the witness-spouse alone holds the testimonial privilege, so Marcus can't stop a willing Maya from testifying.
- C: Correct. Under Trammel, Maya may choose to testify against Marcus, so the testimonial privilege doesn't bar items 1 and 2. But the separate marital confidences privilege protects confidential communications made during the marriage, and Marcus may claim it. ## Set 3 · Q1 · Jury Secrecy and Competency — Rule 606(b)(2)(B): outside influence, not its effect
During deliberations in a federal arson trial, the court security officer escorting the jurors to lunch remarks, “Don’t waste much time on this one. Everybody at the courthouse knows he torched his last place too.” After a guilty verdict, a juror tells defense counsel what the officer said and adds that the remark persuaded her and two other holdouts to vote guilty. The defendant moves for a new trial. What juror testimony may the court receive?

A. Only testimony from the officer himself, because jurors may never testify about events during deliberations.
B. Testimony about the remark, but only if the officer confirms it, because juror testimony needs corroboration.
C. Testimony that the officer made the remark to the jurors, but not testimony about how it affected anyone’s vote.
D. Testimony about both the remark and its effect, because the defendant can’t show prejudice without proof that it changed votes.
E. None, because the remark came during deliberations, and Tanner treats everything that happens during deliberations as internal.

Answer: C
- A: Nonjuror evidence, such as the officer’s own account, is always available. But 606(b)(2) also lets jurors testify that extraneous information or an outside influence reached the jury.
- B: Rule 606(b)(2)(B) has no corroboration requirement. A juror may testify that an outside influence was improperly brought to bear on any juror; the officer’s account would help on the facts but isn’t a precondition.
- C: Correct. A court officer’s comment about the defendant’s supposed past is both extraneous prejudicial information and an outside influence, so 606(b)(2)(A)–(B) let a juror testify that it reached the jury. But 606(b)(1) still bars testimony about “the effect of anything on that juror’s or another juror’s vote.” The court judges prejudice objectively, asking how the remark would likely affect a typical juror.
- D: The exceptions let a juror testify only about whether the outside matter reached the jury. Proof of its actual effect on votes stays barred, and courts don’t need it: they ask whether the information was likely to prejudice an average juror.
- E: Tanner turned on the nature of the matter, not its timing. Drugs and alcohol were internal, like fatigue or illness. A court officer’s comment about the defendant is the classic outside influence, the kind the common law always let jurors report.
Rule: Rule 606(b)(2)(A)–(B): a juror may testify that extraneous prejudicial information or an outside influence reached the jury, but never about its effect on any juror’s vote or mental processes. The court judges prejudice objectively.

## Set 3 · Q2 · Relevance and Rule 403 — Rule 401: relevance through corroboration (United States v. James)
A woman is charged with aiding and abetting a killing and claims she acted in fear for her and her son’s lives. She testifies that the deceased had bragged to her about beating two men so badly that they ended up in the hospital. The prosecution suggests in cross-examination that she made the stories up. She then offers certified police reports showing that the deceased did hospitalize two men in exactly the beatings she described. She concedes she never saw the reports before the killing. The prosecution objects that the reports are irrelevant. How should the court rule?

A. Sustain the objection, because she never saw the reports, so they could not have affected her fear.
B. Overrule the objection, because the reports show the deceased’s violent character, which is an essential element of self-defense under Rule 405(b).
C. Overrule the objection, because once the prosecution attacks her credibility, any supporting evidence comes in without limit.
D. Overrule the objection, because the reports corroborate her testimony about what he told her, which bears on her fear.
E. Sustain the objection, because her own testimony about what he told her is the only permissible evidence of her state of mind.

Answer: D
- A: That was the trial court’s framing, which the en banc court rejected. Relevance can run through corroboration of her account of what he told her, even though the reports themselves never reached her.
- B: A victim’s violent character is not an essential element of self-defense; what matters is the defendant’s reasonable fear. Specific acts don’t come in under 405(b) for that reason. The reports are relevant for a different, non-character purpose: corroboration.
- C: Relevance is the right ground, but “without limit” is wrong. The reports remain subject to Rule 403, which the dissent in United States v. James stressed because jurors may conclude the deceased deserved to die, and to a limiting instruction.
- D: Correct. This is United States v. James (en banc). The trial judge asked whether the reports were relevant to her state of mind, and they weren’t, since she never saw them. The right question was whether they were relevant to her testimony about her state of mind, and they were: if the beatings really happened, it’s more likely he told her about them and less likely she invented the stories. Rule 403 remains available (Judge Kleinfeld’s dissent).
- E: No rule limits proof of state of mind to the defendant’s own words. Any evidence with a tendency to make her account more or less probable is relevant under 401.
Rule: Rule 401: relevance can run through corroboration. In James, police reports she never saw were relevant to her testimony about her state of mind, though 403 remained available.

## Set 3 · Q3 · Relevance and Rule 403 — Flight: the weak link in the chain
A defendant is charged with burglarizing a jewelry store on a Monday night. On Thursday, two officers responding to a neighbor’s noise complaint knock on the door of the defendant’s apartment building. When they ask the defendant, who is on the front steps, whether he lives there, he runs. The prosecution offers his flight as evidence of consciousness of guilt of the burglary. Which is the defense’s strongest argument for excluding it?

A. Flight is an assertion of guilt by conduct, so it is hearsay that no exception covers.
B. Commonwealth v. Warren makes evidence of flight from police inadmissible as a matter of law.
C. Nothing shows he knew he was a burglary suspect when he ran, so the flight says little about guilt of this crime.
D. The prosecution must first prove beyond a reasonable doubt that he was fleeing rather than simply leaving.
E. Flight is character evidence showing he is the kind of person who runs from police, so Rule 404(a)(1) bars it.

Answer: C
- A: Running isn’t intended as an assertion, so it isn’t a “statement” under Rule 801(a). The problem with this flight is the weak link from running to guilt of this burglary, not hearsay.
- B: Warren, a Massachusetts case, held that flight may carry little weight and that a qualified community witness may explain other reasons for it. It didn’t create a rule excluding flight evidence.
- C: Correct. Flight evidence depends on a chain of inferences: (1) the behavior was flight; (2) flight shows consciousness of guilt; (3) of this crime; (4) so actual guilt. Here link 3 is weakest. The flight came three days later, from officers asking about a noise complaint, and he had no reason to think they were there about the burglary (Thelma & Louise clip). Commonwealth v. - D: Preliminary facts of this kind are at most 104(b) questions, which require evidence sufficient to support a finding. No beyond-a-reasonable-doubt threshold applies.
- E: Flight isn’t offered to show a character trait. It’s conduct offered to show his state of mind about a particular crime. The proper attack is on the inferential chain and under 403.
Rule: Flight rests on four links: the behavior was flight, flight shows consciousness of guilt, of this crime, and so actual guilt. Attack the weak link and argue 403 (Warren: there are other reasons to flee).

## Set 3 · Q4 · Specialized Relevance Rules — Rule 407: impeachment and disputed feasibility

At trial the hotel’s general manager testifies, “That lobby was as safe as it could possibly be on a rainy night. There was nothing more we could have done.” The plaintiff then offers evidence of the runner mats. How should the court rule?

A. Exclude it, because Rule 407 bars evidence of subsequent remedial measures for every purpose.
B. Exclude it, because the mats were a change in policy rather than a physical repair of the floor itself.
C. Admit it for feasibility only, because a manager’s opinion that premises were safe can’t be impeached with later measures.
D. Admit it to impeach the manager and to show feasibility, which he put in dispute, but not as proof of negligence.
E. Admit it to prove negligence, because the manager opened the door by vouching for the hotel’s safety, so the hotel can’t invoke Rule 407.

Answer: D
- A: 407 is a limited exclusion. Its second sentence lists permitted purposes, including impeachment and disputed feasibility. Rule 410 is the specialized rule that bars evidence for nearly all purposes.
- B: 407 covers any “measures” that would have made the harm less likely, including new policies, warnings, and procedures, not just physical repairs.
- C: Feasibility is one proper purpose, but so is impeachment: “nothing more we could have done” is directly contradicted by the mats, so both permitted purposes apply.
- D: Correct. 407 bars subsequent remedial measures only when offered to prove negligence or culpable conduct. - E: Opening the door doesn’t change the purpose for which the jury may use the evidence. It comes in for impeachment and feasibility only, and the hotel is entitled to an instruction saying so.

## Set 3 · Q5 · Specialized Relevance Rules — Rules 408 and 409: what each protects

## Set 3 · Q15 · Definition of Hearsay — Rule 801(a): implied assertions after Wright v. Tatham
In a will contest, the contestants claim the testator, Arden, lacked mental capacity when he signed his will in 2024. The proponent offers letters that three longtime business associates wrote to Arden during 2024, asking his advice on complicated investment decisions and discussing the answers he had given them. None of the writers testifies. The contestants object that the letters are hearsay, because they are offered to show that the writers believed Arden was competent.

Under the Federal Rules, how should the court rule?

A. Sustain the objection unless the proponent shows that the writers are unavailable and had personal knowledge.
B. Sustain the objection, because the letters' value depends on the writers' belief in Arden's competence.
C. Overrule it, because the writers didn't intend to assert that he was competent, so the letters aren't hearsay.
D. Overrule the objection, because letters Arden kept in his files are records of his regularly conducted activity.
E. Overrule the objection, because the letters show the writers' then-existing state of mind under Rule 803(3).

Answer: C
- A: Unavailability matters only for Rule 804 exceptions. Because the letters aren't offered for the truth of anything the writers intended to assert, no exception, and no showing of unavailability, is needed.
- B: That was the common-law answer: Wright v. Doe d. Tatham treated letters like these as implied assertions and excluded them. The Federal Rules rejected that approach by defining a statement as an assertion the person intended as one (Rule 801(a)).
- C: Correct. Under Rule 801(a), words or conduct are a statement only if the person intended them as an assertion. The writers asked for advice; they didn't intend to assert that Arden was competent, so the letters aren't hearsay when offered to show their belief. The Advisory Committee Note puts it plainly: nothing is an assertion unless intended to be one (compare United States v. Zenni).
- D: Keeping letters he received doesn't make them Arden's business records. The writers weren't acting under any business duty to him, and in any case no exception is needed.
- E: Rule 803(3) covers the declarant's own state of mind when that state of mind matters. The point here is Arden's competence, not the writers' feelings, and no exception is needed because the letters aren't hearsay.
Rule: Under Rule 801(a), only an assertion the declarant intended as one is a statement. Words or conduct offered to show the declarant's unstated belief are not hearsay under the Federal Rules, which rejected the implied-assertion rule of Wright v. Doe d. Tatham.

## Set 3 · Q16 · Opposing Party and Prior Statements — Rule 801(d)(2)(E) and Bourjaily

He drives it up from Miami every other Thursday. Buy two keys and he’ll knock off ten percent.” Two weeks later, agents watch Ferris’s car arrive at Quill’s house on a Thursday night. Ferris carries a gym bag inside and leaves without it.

Quill is never charged. At Ferris’s trial for conspiracy to distribute cocaine, the prosecution offers Quill’s recorded statement. Ferris objects.

How should the court proceed?

A. Admit the statement only if evidence other than the statement itself establishes the conspiracy and Ferris’s membership in it by a preponderance of the evidence.
B. Exclude the statement, because Agent Pratt, as a government agent, was not a member of the conspiracy, so a statement to her could not further it.
C. Submit the preliminary question to the jury, instructing it to disregard the statement unless it finds beyond a reasonable doubt that Ferris and Quill were co-conspirators.
D. Exclude the statement, because Quill was never charged, and a co-conspirator’s statement is admissible only against a co-defendant charged in the same conspiracy.
E. Admit it if the judge finds by a preponderance, considering the statement and the surveillance, that it was made during and in furtherance of their conspiracy.

- A: This is the pre-Bourjaily independent-evidence rule. The court may consider the statement itself, though it cannot rely on it alone.

- D: The co-conspirator need not be charged, tried, or even identified, as long as the court finds the conspiracy and both memberships.
- E: Correct. This is the Bourjaily standard as codified: the judge decides by a preponderance, may consider the statement, and needs some corroborating evidence.

The court decides the preliminary facts under Rule 104(a) by a preponderance of the evidence and may consider the statement itself. Bourjaily v. United States. But the statement alone does not establish the existence of the conspiracy or the party’s participation in it. FRE 801(d)(2) (final sentence).

## Set 3 · Q19 · Hearsay Exceptions — Rule 804(b)(1): similar motive at the grand jury

B. Admit the testimony, because statements made under oath and under a grant of immunity carry sufficient guarantees of trustworthiness.
C. Exclude the testimony, because a declarant who invokes a privilege is not “unavailable” when the government has the power to immunize him again.
D. Admit it only if the government had a similar motive to develop Vance’s testimony, a question its reasons for not confronting him bear on.

## Set 3 · Q20 · Hearsay Exceptions — Palmer v. Hoffman and Rule 803(6)(E)

## Set 3 · Q21 · Confrontation, Bruton, and the Defense — Davis v. Washington and Hammon: no ongoing emergency

C. Admit the oral statements but exclude the affidavit, because only formalized, sworn statements are testimonial.
D. Exclude both, because the primary purpose of the questioning was to establish past events for a possible prosecution, not to meet an ongoing emergency.
E. Admit both, because Lena is the alleged victim, and the Confrontation Clause is directed at accusatory statements by accomplices and other suspects.

- A: Unavailability alone is not enough. Crawford requires both unavailability and a prior opportunity for cross-examination.

## Set 3 · Q22 · Confrontation, Bruton, and the Defense — Bruton: the confessing codefendant testifies

## Set 3 · Q28 · Authentication and Best Evidence — Rule 1001(d): a printout of electronic data is an original
In a suit over a software license, the plaintiff offers a printout of an email in which the defendant's sales manager promised free upgrades for five years. A paralegal testifies that she printed it from the plaintiff's email account and that the printout accurately shows the email as stored. The defendant objects that the “original” is the electronic file on the server, so the printout violates the best evidence rule.

How should the court rule?

A. Sustain the objection unless the plaintiff shows the electronic file is lost or destroyed under Rule 1004.
B. Overrule the objection, because an accurate printout of electronically stored information is an original.
C. Overrule the objection, because the best evidence rule doesn't apply to emails, which aren't writings.
D. Overrule the objection, but only if the defendant was shown the electronic file before trial.
E. Sustain the objection, because the paralegal didn't write the email and can't testify to its contents.

Answer: B
- A: No excuse is needed, because the printout is itself an original. Rule 1004 matters only when the proponent offers other evidence of content instead of an original or a duplicate.
- B: Correct. Rule 1001(d) provides that for electronically stored information, “original” means any printout or other output readable by sight if it accurately reflects the information. The paralegal's testimony supports that, so Rule 1002 is satisfied.
- C: Rule 1001(a) defines writings to include letters, words, or numbers set down in any form, which covers emails. The rule applies, and the printout satisfies it.
- D: There's no such condition. A printout that accurately reflects the stored data is an original; a dispute about accuracy goes to authentication and to the jury under Rule 1008.
- E: The paralegal isn't testifying to the email's content from memory; she authenticates the printout under Rule 901(b)(1). The printout proves the content, and it is an original.

## Set 3 · Q29 · Privileges — Rule 502(b): the holder's burden
In a federal employment suit, the city produces 140 pages of personnel files. Page 9 is a privileged memo from the city attorney. Two weeks later the city sends a letter asking that the memo be returned. The plaintiff doesn't respond, and the city does nothing more for two years. When the plaintiff lists the memo as a trial exhibit, the city moves to exclude it. Its only evidence of its review process is its current lawyer's statement that “a paralegal reviewed the documents.”

How should the court rule?

A. Exclude the memo, because an inadvertent disclosure never waives the lawyer-client privilege.
B. Exclude the memo, because the city's prompt written request for its return was enough to preserve the privilege.
C. Admit the memo, because producing a privileged document in discovery always waives the privilege.
D. Admit the memo, because the city didn't show reasonable steps to prevent disclosure or prompt steps to fix it.
E. Exclude the memo, because the plaintiff's failure to answer the letter violated the discovery rules.

Answer: D
- A: Inadvertence is only the first of Rule 502(b)'s three elements. The holder must also show reasonable steps to prevent disclosure and prompt, reasonable steps to rectify the error.
- B: A single request followed by two years of silence isn't a prompt, reasonable effort to rectify. The privilege must be jealously guarded by its holder (Williams v. District of Columbia).
- C: Rule 502(b) exists to prevent automatic waiver. An inadvertent disclosure doesn't waive if the holder meets the rule's requirements.
- D: Correct. Under Rule 502(b), the holder bears the burden of showing that the disclosure was inadvertent, that it took reasonable steps to prevent it, and that it promptly took reasonable steps to rectify it. - E: Whatever the plaintiff's duties under Rule 26(b)(5)(B), the question is whether the city met its own burden under Rule 502(b). Two years of inaction defeat that claim.
Rule: Rule 502(b): an inadvertent disclosure in a federal proceeding doesn't waive the privilege if the holder took reasonable steps to prevent it and promptly took reasonable steps to rectify it. The holder bears the burden on each element.

## Set 4 · Q2 · Relevance and Rule 403 — Rule 104(b): knowledge as a conditional fact (Cox)
A diner owner is charged with setting fire to a rival restaurant. To prove motive, the prosecution calls the rival’s owner, who testifies that two days before the fire he told a newspaper he planned to open a second restaurant directly across the street from the defendant’s diner. The prosecution shows that the article ran the next morning and that the defendant subscribed to the paper. The defendant testifies that he never reads it. The defense objects that the rival’s plan is irrelevant unless the defendant knew about it. What should the judge do?

A. Decide herself under Rule 104(a) whether the defendant more likely than not read the article, and exclude the testimony unless she is persuaded.
B. Admit the testimony if a reasonable jury could find, by a preponderance, that the defendant knew of the plan.
C. Exclude the testimony, because the defendant’s sworn denial creates a factual dispute that makes the evidence speculative.
D. Admit the testimony without any showing of knowledge, because a rival’s plans are always relevant to motive.
E. Admit the testimony only if the prosecution proves beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant read the article.

Answer: B
- A: 104(a) is for preliminary questions such as privilege and competency. When relevance depends on a fact, 104(b) applies, and the jury decides whether the fact exists.
- B: Correct. This is conditional relevance under 104(b), as in Cox. The judge doesn’t weigh credibility or make her own finding (Huddleston). She asks only whether a jury could reasonably find the conditional fact. The subscription and the article’s timing are enough, as Cox’s near-daily visits to Hammer’s mother’s house were in that case.
- C: A denial doesn’t defeat conditional relevance. If a reasonable jury could find knowledge, the evidence comes in and the jury weighs the denial.
- D: The plan supplies a motive only if the defendant knew about it. That is the missing link 104(b) exists to police, and without a sufficient showing the evidence should be excluded or struck.
- E: Conditional relevance needs only evidence from which a reasonable jury could find the fact by a preponderance. Proof beyond a reasonable doubt applies to the elements at the end of the case, not to preliminary facts.
Rule: Rule 104(b) conditional relevance: admit if a reasonable jury could find the conditional fact by a preponderance. The judge doesn’t weigh credibility (Huddleston, Cox).

## Set 4 · Q3 · Relevance and Rule 403 — Rule 104(b): when the connection never comes
In an extortion trial, the prosecution offers a threatening letter signed only “J.” Defense counsel objects that nothing ties the letter to the defendant. The prosecutor says a handwriting examiner will testify later that the defendant wrote it, and the judge admits the letter on that condition. The prosecution then rests without calling the examiner, and no other evidence links the defendant to the letter. What should the court do on the defendant’s motion?

A. Let the letter stand, because once evidence is admitted, its weight is for the jury.
B. Strike the letter and tell the jury to disregard it, because the condition for admitting it was never met.
C. Let the letter stand if the judge personally believes, by a preponderance, that the defendant wrote it.
D. Declare a mistrial automatically, because no instruction can cure conditionally admitted evidence that fails.
E. Deny the motion, because the defense waived the issue by not objecting again when the prosecution rested.

Answer: B
- A: Conditional admission is just that. If no evidence would let a reasonable jury find the conditional fact by a preponderance, the letter has no proper path to relevance and can’t stay before the jury.
- B: Correct. The letter is relevant only if the defendant wrote it. 104(b) lets the court admit evidence “on the condition that the proof be introduced later.” When that proof never comes, the evidence fails the test of conditional relevance, and the court must instruct the jury to disregard it (or declare a mistrial if an instruction can’t cure the harm).
- C: Under 104(b) the judge doesn’t decide the conditional fact. She asks only whether a reasonable jury could find it (Huddleston). With no evidence of authorship, no jury could.
- D: Under Rule 104(b) the usual remedy is to strike the evidence and instruct the jury to disregard it. A mistrial is for the rare case where an instruction can’t cure the prejudice, and it isn’t automatic.
- E: The defense objected when the letter was offered, and a motion to strike is the proper way to raise the failure to connect it up.
Rule: Rule 104(b): evidence whose relevance depends on a fact may be admitted on condition of later proof. If the proof never comes, the court strikes it and tells the jury to disregard it.

## Set 4 · Q4 · Specialized Relevance Rules — Rule 407: the narrow impeachment exception
A shopper trips over a hose stretched across a garden-center aisle and sues the store. After the accident, the store installed retractable hose reels at the end of every aisle. At trial the store manager testifies on direct, “When I walked that aisle an hour before she fell, I thought it looked reasonably safe.” The plaintiff offers the hose reels to impeach him. How should the court rule?

A. Admit the evidence, because any defense testimony that the premises were safe opens the door to impeachment with later repairs.
B. Admit the evidence to show that the store controlled the aisle, with a limiting instruction.
C. Exclude the evidence, because Rule 407 bars evidence of later repairs for every purpose.
D. Admit the evidence to prove feasibility, because the store didn’t stipulate that hose reels were feasible.
E. Exclude it, because a later improvement doesn’t contradict his belief that the aisle was reasonably safe then.

Answer: E
- A: That reading would let the exception swallow the rule. It reaches testimony the later measure actually contradicts, like the claim in the class hypothetical that a $55.26 carpet fix would have cost $40,000.
- B: Control is a permitted purpose only if disputed, and nothing suggests the store denied controlling its own aisle.
- C: 407 bars only the listed uses: negligence, culpable conduct, a defect, or a need for a warning. Impeachment and disputed ownership, control, or feasibility remain open. The evidence fails here because it doesn’t actually impeach.
- D: Feasibility is a permitted purpose only “if disputed.” The store never claimed hose reels were impractical, and it doesn’t have to stipulate to keep feasibility out of dispute.
- E: Correct. Courts read the impeachment exception narrowly, or it would swallow the rule, since nearly every defense witness denies negligence. A later improvement doesn’t contradict a belief that conditions were reasonably safe before the accident: wiser after isn’t foolish before. A sweeping claim, such as that no safer arrangement was possible, would be different.
Rule: Rule 407’s impeachment exception is narrow: the later measure must contradict the testimony, not merely a denial of negligence. Feasibility, ownership, and control are permitted purposes only if disputed.

## Set 4 · Q11 · Impeachment and the Rape Shield — United States v. Whitmore: foundation and good faith

## Set 4 · Q13 · Impeachment and the Rape Shield — Rule 412(b)(1)(A): another source of injury
A defendant is charged in federal court with sexual assault and claims he wasn’t the attacker. The examining nurse found bruising and abrasions consistent with intercourse. By written motion filed a month before trial, the defense seeks to offer (1) testimony that the complainant had intercourse with her boyfriend several hours before the alleged assault, to explain the injuries, and (2) testimony that she “was sleeping around with several men” that month. Which ruling is correct?

A. Item 1 is admissible only if the prosecutor offers it, because the accused may not offer evidence under Rule 412(b)(1)(A).
B. Item 1 may be admitted under Rule 412(b)(1)(A) after an in camera hearing, but item 2 is barred.
C. Both are barred, because Rule 412 excludes all evidence of a victim’s other sexual behavior in a criminal case.
D. Item 2 may be admitted to show she had many possible partners, but item 1 is barred as predisposition.
E. Both are admissible, because a defendant claiming misidentification may offer any evidence about other possible sources of the injury.

Answer: B
- A: The source-of-physical-evidence exception is typically invoked by the defense. The “offered by the prosecutor” language belongs to 412(b)(1)(B), on sexual behavior with the accused.
- B: Correct. 412(b)(1)(A) permits specific instances of a victim’s sexual behavior offered to show that someone other than the defendant was the source of semen, injury, or other physical evidence, and the motion followed 412(c)’s procedure (written motion at least 14 days before trial, notice, in camera hearing). Item 2 is general conduct or predisposition, not a specific instance, so it is barred (compare Walker, Problem 5.2).
- C: 412(b)(1) has three criminal-case exceptions, and explaining the source of physical evidence is the first.
- D: Reversed. Item 1 is a specific instance offered to explain the injuries (Rule 412(b)(1)(A)). Item 2 is general evidence of sexual predisposition, which Rule 412 bars.
- E: Only specific instances qualify. General evidence of sexual activity with others is exactly the inference 412 was designed to block.
Rule: Rule 412(b)(1)(A): in criminal cases, specific instances may show another source of semen, injury, or physical evidence; general conduct stays barred. 412(c) requires a written motion 14 days before trial and an in camera hearing.

## Set 4 · Q14 · Impeachment and the Rape Shield — Rule 412(b)(1)(C): motive to fabricate
A church youth volunteer is charged with sexually abusing a 13-year-old boy. The defense theory is that the boy made up the accusation to deflect attention from his own serious trouble at home: months earlier, his parents had discovered that he sexually abused his younger sister and sent him to counseling. The defense seeks to cross-examine the boy about the incident with his sister. The prosecution objects under Rule 412. How should the court analyze the objection?

A. The evidence must be excluded, because Rule 412 has no exceptions in cases involving child victims.
B. It is admissible under Rule 412(b)(1)(A), because it explains the source of the boy’s sexual knowledge.
C. The evidence must be admitted, because evidence offered to show bias is always constitutionally protected.
D. Rule 412 doesn’t apply, because the evidence concerns the boy’s misconduct toward someone else, not his own victimization.
E. It is other sexual behavior under Rule 412, admissible only if exclusion would violate the right of confrontation.

Answer: E
- A: 412’s exceptions, including the constitutional exception in 412(b)(1)(C), apply in every criminal case, whatever the victim’s age.
- B: Rule 412(b)(1)(A) covers another source of semen, injury, or other physical evidence, not knowledge. The defense theory is a motive to fabricate, so the route is the constitutional exception.
- C: Olden protects cross on a prototypical form of bias, but the constitutional exception isn’t automatic. The court weighs how strongly the evidence supports the motive and whether the defense can make its point without it.
- D: The rule covers any “other sexual behavior” of the alleged victim, not only behavior in which he was a willing participant or a victim.
- E: Correct. 412(a) reaches evidence “that a victim engaged in other sexual behavior,” whoever the other person was. The defense’s route is 412(b)(1)(C), evidence whose exclusion would violate the defendant’s constitutional rights. Olden and Davis protect cross-examination showing a prototypical motive to lie. The court must decide whether this theory is strong enough to require admission over the shield.
Rule: Rule 412(b)(1)(C): evidence otherwise barred by 412 comes in if excluding it would violate the defendant’s constitutional rights, as with cross-examination on a prototypical motive to fabricate (Olden, Davis).

## Set 4 · Q15 · Definition of Hearsay — Bridges: a statement offered to show knowledge
Seven-year-old Nora was lured from a playground into a stranger’s house and assaulted. When she got home an hour later, she described the place to her mother: a front room with a green couch, a fish tank with one large orange fish, a staircase with a broken bottom step, and a picture of a white horse over the fireplace.

Nora does not testify at the defendant’s trial. The prosecution calls the mother to repeat the description, and a detective then testifies that the defendant’s house, where Nora had never been before that day, contains each of those features. The defendant objects to the mother’s testimony as hearsay.

What is the best argument for admitting the mother’s testimony?

A. The description is a present sense impression, because Nora described the room as she remembered seeing it.
B. It isn’t offered to prove the house had those features, but to show Nora knew things she could have learned only inside it.
C. The description is an excited utterance, because Nora had just been assaulted.
D. The description is admissible under Rule 803(3) as a statement of Nora’s then-existing state of mind.
E. The description is a statement of identification, because it identifies the defendant’s house as the place Nora perceived earlier.

Answer: B
- A: A present sense impression must be made while or immediately after perceiving the event. Nora spoke an hour later, from memory.
- B: Correct. The statement is circumstantial evidence of Nora’s knowledge, which is the Bridges theory.
- C: An hour later, at home, giving a detailed description, Nora was not clearly under the stress of the event. This is weaker than the non-hearsay argument.
- D: Rule 803(3) excludes statements of memory offered to prove the fact remembered. A description of a room is memory.
- E: Rule 801(d)(1)(C) applies only if the declarant testifies and is subject to cross-examination. Nora does not testify.
Rule: A statement offered as circumstantial evidence of the declarant’s knowledge, rather than to prove the facts it describes, is not offered for the truth of the matter asserted. Bridges v. State.

## Set 4 · Q16 · Opposing Party and Prior Statements — Rule 801(d)(2)(D): no personal-knowledge requirement
Tess Albin sues Little Acorns Daycare after her son breaks his arm there. The daycare's director, Glen Moss, was in a meeting when it happened. An hour later, after talking with staff, Moss called Albin and said, “One of our aides dropped him while lifting him off the slide. I'm so sorry.” At trial, Albin testifies to Moss's statement. The daycare objects that Moss had no personal knowledge of how the injury happened.

How should the court rule?

A. Overrule the objection, because Moss's apology makes the statement one against the daycare's pecuniary interest.
B. Sustain the objection, because every hearsay declarant must have personal knowledge of the facts asserted.
C. Sustain the objection as to the description of the fall, but admit the apology as an expression of sympathy.
D. Overrule it, because the statement concerned a matter within Moss's job, and personal knowledge isn't required.
E. Sustain the objection, because only a statement the daycare specifically authorized Moss to make is admissible against it.

Answer: D
- A: Rule 804(b)(3) requires an unavailable declarant and a statement against the declarant's own interest when made. Neither applies, and no exception is needed for an opposing party's statement.
- B: The personal-knowledge requirement applies to declarants whose statements come in under the Rule 803 and 804 exceptions. An opposing party's statement, including an agent's, need not rest on personal knowledge (Advisory Committee Note to Rule 801(d)(2); Mahlandt).
- C: Nothing in the rules separates the apology from the description. The whole statement is the daycare's under Rule 801(d)(2)(D), and Rule 409 covers only offers to pay medical expenses.
- D: Correct. Under Rule 801(d)(2)(D), a statement by a party's employee on a matter within the scope of that relationship, made while it existed, is admissible against the party. Telling a parent how a child was hurt is within a daycare director's job, and Mahlandt holds such statements need not be based on personal knowledge.
- E: That describes Rule 801(d)(2)(C). Rule 801(d)(2)(D) separately admits an employee's statements on matters within the scope of employment, whether or not the employer authorized that particular statement.
Rule: Rule 801(d)(2)(D) admits against a party its agent's or employee's statement on a matter within the scope of that relationship while it existed. Opposing-party statements need not rest on the declarant's personal knowledge (Mahlandt).

## Set 4 · Q17 · Opposing Party and Prior Statements — Tome: a consistent statement made after the motive arose
Dalton is charged with sexually abusing his eight-year-old daughter, M., on a military base. M. and her mother lived with Dalton until a bitter custody fight began in January, when Dalton sought sole custody. M. testifies to the abuse. On cross-examination, defense counsel suggests that M.’s mother has coached her since January to fabricate the accusations so that the mother would win custody.

The prosecution then calls M.’s babysitter, who testifies that in March, M. told her the same account M. gave on the stand. The defense objects.

How should the court rule?

A. Admit the testimony only to rehabilitate M.’s credibility, not as substantive evidence of the abuse.
B. Admit the testimony, because M. testified and is subject to cross-examination about the prior statement, which is all Rule 801(d)(1) requires.
C. Admit the babysitter’s testimony as substantive evidence, because the 2014 amendment to Rule 801(d)(1)(B) eliminated the requirement that a consistent statement precede the alleged motive to fabricate.
D. Exclude the testimony under Rule 801(d)(1)(B), because M.’s statement was made after the alleged motive to fabricate arose.
E. Exclude the testimony, because a prior consistent statement is admissible only if it was given under penalty of perjury.

Answer: D
- A: Since 2014, a statement that properly rehabilitates under 801(d)(1)(B) comes in substantively. And a post-motive statement does not rehabilitate against a fabrication charge at all.
- B: Being subject to cross is necessary but not sufficient. Clause (B) also requires the statement to be offered for a permitted purpose and, under Tome, to predate the motive.
- C: The 2014 amendment did not disturb Tome. It extended substantive use to consistent statements that rehabilitate on other grounds, and the note says the premotive rule still governs fabrication charges.
- D: Correct. Under Tome, a consistent statement offered to rebut a charge of improper influence must predate that influence.
- E: Only prior inconsistent statements ever needed an oath, under 801(d)(1)(A), and the December 1, 2026 amendment removes that requirement too.
Rule: A testifying declarant’s prior consistent statement is not hearsay if offered (i) to rebut an express or implied charge that the declarant recently fabricated it or acted from a recent improper influence or motive, or (ii) to rehabilitate the declarant’s credibility when attacked on another ground. FRE 801(d)(1)(B). Under Tome v. United States, a statement offered under clause (i) must have been made before the alleged improper motive arose.

## Set 4 · Q18 · Hearsay Exceptions — Rule 803(1): a calm 911 call
At 4:12 p.m., a motorist calls 911 and, in an even, unhurried voice, says: “There’s a red Silverado, plate JKT-4471, that just passed me on I-95 northbound doing probably a hundred, weaving across all three lanes. I’m at mile marker 12.” The caller gives no name and hangs up.

At 4:21 p.m., a red Silverado with that plate crosses the median near mile marker 19 and kills another driver. In the estate’s wrongful-death suit against the Silverado’s driver, the estate offers the 911 recording, properly authenticated, to prove that the Silverado was speeding and weaving. The caller has never been located.

Is the recording admissible?

A. No, because the caller’s calm demeanor shows that he was not under the stress of excitement caused by a startling event.
B. Yes, as a present sense impression, because the caller described the truck’s driving while or immediately after perceiving it.
C. No, because a call made to a government 911 operator is testimonial.
D. No, because an unidentified declarant’s personal knowledge cannot be established.
E. Yes, but only if the estate shows that it could not locate the caller despite reasonable efforts.

Answer: B
- A: Calm demeanor would hurt an excited-utterance argument under 803(2), but 803(1) has no excitement requirement.
- B: Correct. The statement describes an event while or immediately after the declarant perceived it.
- C: The Confrontation Clause applies only in criminal prosecutions. Even in a criminal case, a 911 call reporting an ongoing danger is typically nontestimonial under Davis v. Washington.
- D: Personal knowledge can be inferred from the content and circumstances of the statement. Courts scrutinize anonymous declarants carefully, but anonymity is not a per se bar.
- E: Availability is immaterial under Rule 803. Unavailability matters only for the Rule 804 exceptions.

## Set 4 · Q19 · Hearsay Exceptions — Hillmon: intent to meet another person
Nineteen-year-old Lark disappears. At 6:30 p.m. on the night she vanished, she told a friend: “I’m meeting Brody at the Taco Hut lot at nine to pick up some weed. He says he’s got a good deal.” Lark was never seen again.

At Brody’s federal kidnapping trial, the prosecution offers the friend’s testimony to prove (1) that Lark went to the Taco Hut lot and (2) that Brody met her there. Brody objects.

Which statement most accurately describes the admissibility of Lark’s statement?

A. It is admissible to show Lark went to the lot; whether it may show Brody’s conduct is disputed, and many courts require corroboration.
B. It is inadmissible for any purpose, because Lark’s reference to what Brody “says” is a statement of memory about a past conversation.
C. It is admissible for both purposes without limitation, because Rule 803(3) admits any statement of a declarant’s then-existing intent.
D. It is admissible to prove that Brody met Lark, but not to prove Lark’s own conduct, because a declarant’s stated intentions do not reliably predict her own behavior.
E. It is inadmissible, because the statement-of-intent exception applies only when the declarant is unavailable, and the prosecution has not proven that Lark is dead.

Answer: A
- A: Correct. This states the Hillmon rule and the current split over the Pheaster use.
- B: The embedded statement is Brody’s own, admissible against him under 801(d)(2)(A). Lark’s main assertion looks forward, not backward.
- C: This overstates the rule. Using one person’s stated intent to prove another person’s conduct is the contested part, and the legislative history cuts against it.
- D: This reverses the doctrine. The declarant’s own future conduct is the uncontroversial use; the third party’s conduct is the contested one.
- E: Rule 803 exceptions apply regardless of the declarant’s availability.

## Set 4 · Q20 · Hearsay Exceptions — Rule 804(b)(2): only the cause and circumstances
Teddy is on trial for the murder of Vance, who was stabbed in an alley. Paramedics found Vance bleeding heavily. As they worked on him, Vance said: “I’m done for. Tell my mom I love her. The prosecution offers all of Vance’s statement.

How should the court rule?

A. Admit the statements that Teddy stabbed Vance over the money he owed, but exclude the statement about Ray Moss.
B. Exclude the entire statement, because Vance’s belief that death was imminent must be shown by evidence other than his own words.
C. Admit only “Teddy stabbed me.” The reference to the debt must be excluded as a statement of motive, which falls outside the exception.

E. Exclude the entire statement, because statements to paramedics are testimonial and Vance cannot be cross-examined.

- A: Correct. Identity and motive are part of the cause and circumstances; another killing is not.

## Set 4 · Q21 · Confrontation, Bruton, and the Defense — Chambers today: Rule 607 and amended Rule 801(d)(1)(A)
Cal Brooks is prosecuted in federal court, in a case filed in 2027, for shooting a park ranger. The defense calls Lyle Garner, who on three occasions told different friends, spontaneously and in detail, that he shot the ranger himself. On the stand Garner denies the shooting and denies ever saying he did it. Brooks then offers the three friends to repeat Garner's confessions. The government objects that Brooks can't impeach his own witness and that the confessions are hearsay that fits no exception, because Garner is available.

How should the court rule?

A. Admit it as statements against interest, because Chambers overrides the unavailability requirement.
B. Exclude the testimony, because Rule 804(b)(3) requires that Garner be unavailable.
C. Exclude the testimony, because a party may not impeach a witness it chose to call.
D. Admit the testimony only to impeach Garner, because his confessions weren't made under penalty of perjury at a proceeding.
E. Admit it as substantive evidence, because Garner testified and is subject to cross about the inconsistent confessions.

Answer: E
- A: Chambers didn't rewrite the hearsay rules; it held that applying them mechanistically to exclude critical, trustworthy defense evidence can violate due process. Here the Federal Rules already admit the confessions.
- B: Rule 804(b)(3) does require unavailability, and Garner is available. But the confessions need no exception, because they are excluded from hearsay as a testifying witness's inconsistent statements.
- C: That is the common-law voucher rule Chambers called “archaic.” Rule 607 provides that any party, including the party that called the witness, may attack the witness's credibility.
- D: That was the law before December 1, 2026, when Rule 801(d)(1)(A) required a statement given under penalty of perjury at a proceeding. The amendment governs a case filed in 2027, so the confessions are substantive.
- E: Correct. Rule 607 lets any party, including the one that called him, attack a witness's credibility, so the voucher rule Chambers condemned is gone. And under Rule 801(d)(1)(A) as amended December 1, 2026, a testifying declarant's prior inconsistent statement is not hearsay, sworn or not, if he is subject to cross-examination about it. Rule 613(b)'s foundation is met because Garner is on the stand and has denied the statements.
Rule: Rule 607 abolishes the voucher rule: any party may impeach any witness. Under Rule 801(d)(1)(A) as amended December 1, 2026, a testifying witness's prior inconsistent statement is substantive evidence whether or not it was sworn, if he is subject to cross-examination about it. Chambers remains a due process backstop when evidence rules are applied mechanistically.

## Set 4 · Q22 · Confrontation, Bruton, and the Defense — Ohio v. Clark: a child’s statement to a teacher

## Set 4 · Q23 · Confrontation, Bruton, and the Defense — Bruton: a joint bench trial
Jae Kim and Lou Fenn are charged together with arson. Kim confessed to police, naming Fenn as his partner, and Kim won't testify. Fenn validly waives his right to a jury, and the two are tried together before the judge alone. The prosecution offers Kim's confession only against Kim. Fenn objects.

What is the best ruling?

A. Exclude the confession entirely, because Bruton applies with full force in any joint trial.

## Set 4 · Q24 · Lay and Expert Opinion — General Electric v. Joiner: the analytical gap
A painter who used Solvex paint thinner for twenty years develops a rare liver cancer and sues the maker. His expert relies on two studies: one in which infant mice injected with massive doses of Solvex's main chemical developed a different kind of tumor, and one finding a slightly higher cancer rate among refinery workers exposed to many chemicals, whose authors said it showed no link to that chemical. The district court excludes the expert's opinion that Solvex caused the cancer and grants summary judgment. The painter appeals.

What is the appellate court most likely to hold?

A. Affirmance, because animal studies are inadmissible as a matter of law to prove causation in humans.
B. Reversal, because a court reviews only an expert's methodology, not his conclusions, and both studies used accepted methods.
C. Reversal, because an exclusion that leads to summary judgment gets de novo review.
D. Reversal, because conflicts between the studies and the opinion go to weight and are for the jury.
E. Affirmance, because the court could find too great an analytical gap between the data and the opinion.

Answer: E
- A: Animal studies aren't barred as a category. The question is whether these studies, given the dose, the species, and the tumor type, support this opinion.
- B: General Electric Co. v. Joiner rejected a clean split: conclusions and methodology are not entirely distinct, and a court needn't admit an opinion connected to the data only by the expert's say-so.
- C: Joiner held that abuse-of-discretion review applies to rulings admitting or excluding expert testimony, even when the ruling decides the case.
- D: Under Rule 702 as amended in 2023, whether the opinion stays within what the basis and method can support is for the judge to decide by a preponderance, not a question of weight for the jury.
- E: Correct. On facts much like these (infant mice, massive doses, a different tumor, and epidemiology whose authors disclaimed a link), General Electric Co. v. Joiner upheld exclusion because of “too great an analytical gap between the data and the opinion proffered,” and held that such rulings are reviewed for abuse of discretion.
Rule: Under General Electric Co. v. Joiner, a court may exclude an opinion when there is too great an analytical gap between the data and the conclusion, and appellate review of expert rulings is for abuse of discretion. Rule 702(d) now requires each opinion to reflect a reliable application of the method to the facts.

## Set 4 · Q26 · Authentication and Best Evidence — Rule 901(a): the judge screens, the jury decides
In an extortion trial, the prosecution offers a typed, unsigned letter demanding $50,000, which the victim received. The victim testifies that the letter uses a private nickname only she and the defendant used, refers to a loan she made the defendant two weeks earlier, and arrived the day after the two argued about that loan. The defendant testifies that he didn't write it. The judge says, “I'm not persuaded he more likely than not wrote it,” and excludes the letter.

Was the ruling correct?

A. Yes, because an unsigned typed letter can be authenticated only by handwriting or expert analysis.
B. No, because the defendant's denial goes to weight, so the letter must be admitted without any foundation.
C. No, because the evidence would let a reasonable jury find that the defendant wrote the letter.
D. Yes, because the judge decides authenticity herself under Rule 104(a), by a preponderance of the evidence.
E. Yes, because the victim's testimony is self-serving and can't alone authenticate a letter she received.

Answer: C
- A: Rule 901(b)(4) allows authentication by distinctive characteristics such as contents and substance, taken together with all the circumstances. Handwriting is only one route, and a typed letter has none to compare.

Rule: Rule 901(a) requires evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims, a conditional-relevance standard (Rule 104(b)). The judge screens and the jury decides. Distinctive contents and circumstances can authenticate a document (901(b)(4)).

E. A loan agreement accompanied by a notary's certificate of acknowledgment.

Answer: A
- A: Correct. Rule 902 lists specific self-authenticating items, and ordinary web pages aren't among them. The proponent needs evidence, such as testimony from someone who viewed the site or a certification under Rule 902(13) or (14), that the printout accurately reflects the company's page.
- B: Rule 902(4) makes a copy of a public record self-authenticating if it is certified as correct by the custodian or another authorized person.
- C: Rule 902(7) makes self-authenticating an inscription, sign, tag, or label purporting to have been affixed in the course of business and indicating origin, ownership, or control.
- D: Printed material purporting to be a newspaper or periodical is self-authenticating under Rule 902(6). Forging an entire newspaper is unlikely.
- E: Rule 902(8) covers a document accompanied by a certificate of acknowledgment lawfully executed by a notary public.
Rule: Rule 902 lists items that need no extrinsic evidence of authenticity, including certified copies of public records (902(4)), newspapers (902(6)), trade labels (902(7)), acknowledged documents (902(8)), and certified records under 902(11)–(14). The opponent may still dispute authenticity, and self-authentication isn't a hearsay exception.

## Set 5 · Q2 · Relevance and Rule 403 — Old Chief: stipulations outside legal status

## Set 5 · Q3 · Relevance and Rule 403 — Rule 403: “substantially outweighed”
In a civil fraud trial, the plaintiff offers a recorded phone call in which the defendant brags about “taking that sucker for everything he had.” The judge excludes it, explaining: “This recording is moderately probative, but the risk of unfair prejudice is somewhat greater, so it stays out.” The plaintiff loses and appeals. What is the plaintiff’s strongest argument?

A. Rule 403 doesn’t apply to a party’s own recorded words, which are always admissible against that party.
B. The judge applied the wrong standard: Rule 403 requires that unfair prejudice substantially outweigh probative value.
C. Rule 403 allows exclusion only for unfair prejudice, not for confusion, delay, or the other dangers it lists.
D. A party’s own admission can never be unfairly prejudicial.
E. The judge should have given a limiting instruction instead, because Rule 105 always displaces Rule 403.

Answer: B
- A: 403 applies to all relevant evidence, including a party’s own statements.
- B: Correct. 403 tilts toward admission: “The court may exclude relevant evidence if its probative value is substantially outweighed by a danger of” unfair prejudice or another listed danger. Finding prejudice “somewhat greater” than probative value doesn’t meet that standard. Rulings are reviewed for abuse of discretion, but applying the wrong legal test is an abuse of discretion.
- C: 403 lists unfair prejudice, confusing the issues, misleading the jury, undue delay, wasting time, and needlessly cumulative evidence. The error here is the weighing standard, not the kind of danger.
- D: Admissions can carry unfair prejudice, through crude or inflammatory language, for example. The point is that the judge had to find that danger substantially outweighed probative value, and he didn’t.
- E: A limiting instruction is one alternative a court weighs under Rule 403, but Rule 105 doesn’t displace Rule 403. The error is the standard: the judge excluded on a “somewhat greater” balance instead of requiring that unfair prejudice substantially outweigh probative value.
Rule: Rule 403 allows exclusion only when probative value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice or another listed danger. The test favors admission.

## Set 5 · Q13 · Impeachment and the Rape Shield — Olden: motive to fabricate beats a shield law
In a state rape prosecution, the defendant admits intercourse and claims it was consensual. His theory is that the complainant invented the rape because her live-in boyfriend saw her getting out of the defendant’s car and she feared he would leave her. The trial court, applying the state’s rape-shield law, bars all evidence that she lived with the boyfriend, even after she testifies that she lives with her mother. The defendant is convicted. On review, what is the strongest ruling?

A. The exclusion was proper, because cohabitation is “other sexual behavior” and rape-shield laws have no exception for bias.
B. The exclusion was improper only if the defendant showed that her cohabitation made it more likely she consented.
C. The exclusion violated the Confrontation Clause by barring proof of a key witness’s motive to fabricate.
D. The exclusion was proper, because the defendant could argue the motive to lie in closing without introducing evidence of the relationship.
E. The exclusion was proper, because the complainant’s living arrangements are irrelevant to whether she consented.

Answer: C
- A: Shield laws must yield where exclusion would violate the defendant’s constitutional rights (412(b)(1)(C); Olden; Davis v. Alaska). Showing bias is at the core of confrontation.
- B: That would be the forbidden inference, sexual history used to prove consent. The evidence was admissible for a different reason: motive to fabricate.
- C: Correct. This is Olden v. Kentucky. The defendant’s theory was bias, a motive to lie, not her sexual character. - E: The evidence isn’t offered on consent. It shows her motive to fabricate, a classic form of bias the Confrontation Clause protects (Olden).

## Set 5 · Q14 · Definition of Hearsay — Rule 801(a): unintended implied assertions (United States v. Zenni)
Federal agents execute a search warrant at Donnelly’s apartment, which the government contends is the hub of an illegal sports-betting business. During the two-hour search, the apartment’s landline rings eleven times. Agent Ruiz answers each call. Each caller, unprompted, says something like “Put two hundred on the Eagles, minus three” or “Give me the over on the Sixers.” None of the callers is identified.

At Donnelly’s trial for operating an illegal gambling business, the government calls Agent Ruiz to recount the calls to prove that the apartment was being used to accept bets. Donnelly objects on hearsay grounds.

How should the court rule?

A. Overrule the objection, because each call is a present sense impression describing the caller’s own conduct while he was engaged in it.
B. Overrule the objection, but only if the court finds the callers unavailable, because each caller’s attempt to place an illegal bet was a statement against penal interest.
C. Sustain the objection, because the callers’ words are offered to prove their belief that bets could be placed at the apartment, and that belief is probative only if it is accurate.
D. Overrule it, because the callers didn’t intend to assert that the apartment took bets, so their words aren’t offered for anything they asserted.
E. Sustain the objection unless the government produces at least one caller for cross-examination, because the sincerity of anonymous declarants cannot otherwise be tested.

Answer: D
- A: A present sense impression must describe or explain an event or condition. A betting instruction describes nothing, and no exception is needed for words that are not hearsay in the first place.
- B: Rule 804(b)(3) requires unavailability and, in a criminal case, corroborating circumstances. More basically, it presupposes hearsay. The calls are not hearsay, so the exception is beside the point.
- C: This is the common-law reasoning of Wright v. Doe d. Tatham, which treated implied assertions as hearsay. The Federal Rules rejected that approach by defining “statement” by reference to the declarant’s intent to assert.
- D: Correct. The callers did not intend to assert that the apartment was a betting operation, so their utterances are not hearsay when offered to prove it.
- E: Nothing in the hearsay rule requires producing a declarant whose words are not hearsay. Anonymity can bear on weight or Rule 403, but it does not turn non-hearsay into hearsay.
Rule: A “statement” is a person’s oral or written assertion, or nonverbal conduct the person intended as an assertion. FRE 801(a). Hearsay is a statement offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted in it. FRE 801(c). Under the Federal Rules, the declarant’s intent controls: words or conduct not intended to assert the fact they are offered to prove are not hearsay, even when they reveal the declarant’s belief in that fact.

## Set 5 · Q15 · Definition of Hearsay — Effect on the listener: notice, with a Rule 105 instruction
Lorna slipped on spilled soda in aisle 7 of a grocery store and sued the store for negligence. The store contends there was no spill at all and that Lorna tripped over her own feet.

At trial, Lorna calls a cashier, who testifies that about 40 minutes before the fall, a shopper she did not know walked up to the store manager and said: “Somebody dropped a big bottle of soda in aisle 7. It’s all over the floor.” The shopper had passed through aisle 7 roughly twenty minutes before she said it. The store objects.

What is the proper ruling?

A. Exclude the statement, because the shopper is unidentified and her personal knowledge cannot be shown.
B. Admit the statement for all purposes as an opposing-party statement, because the store’s manager heard it.
C. Exclude the statement entirely, because notice matters only if there was a spill, so the statement’s relevance depends on its truth.
D. Admit the statement for all purposes as a present sense impression.
E. Admit it to show the store had notice of a possible spill, with an instruction on request that it doesn’t prove a spill existed.

Answer: E
- A: When a statement is offered only for its effect on the listener, the declarant’s knowledge is irrelevant.
- B: The shopper was not the store’s agent, and nothing shows the manager adopted the statement.
- C: Notice is a separate element from the existence of the hazard. A warning is relevant to what the store knew regardless of its truth; Lorna must prove the spill with other evidence.
- D: A twenty-minute gap is too long for “while or immediately after perceiving.”
- E: Correct. The statement is admissible for notice, with a limiting instruction on request.
Rule: A statement offered to show its effect on the listener, such as notice, knowledge, or a reason for later action, is not offered for its truth and is not hearsay for that purpose. The court must, on timely request, restrict the evidence to its proper purpose and instruct the jury accordingly. FRE 105.

## Set 5 · Q16 · Opposing Party and Prior Statements — Rule 801(d)(2)(B): adoption by silence
At a crowded bar the night after a jewelry-store robbery, Pruitt’s longtime friend Gage said to Pruitt, loudly enough for three others at the table to hear: “You need to lie low, man. The cops are all over town looking for whoever hit Kessel’s Jewelers, and that was some sloppy work you did.” Pruitt shrugged, laughed, and ordered another round. No police were present, and Pruitt had not been arrested or questioned.

At Pruitt’s robbery trial, the prosecution calls one of the others at the table to recount the exchange.

Which statement is most accurate?

A. The testimony is inadmissible, because silence can amount to adoption only when the accusation is made by a police officer.
B. The testimony is inadmissible, because using a defendant’s silence as evidence of guilt violates the Fifth Amendment and due process.
C. Gage’s statement is admissible as a co-conspirator statement, because Gage was helping Pruitt avoid detection.
D. The testimony is admissible only to show Pruitt’s state of mind, and the jury must be instructed not to consider it as evidence that he committed the robbery.

Answer: E
- A: Adoption by silence most often arises with private accusers. Police accusations, especially after arrest, are actually more problematic.

Under Rule 801(d)(1)(A) as amended effective December 1, 2026, how should the court rule?

B. Admit it as substantive evidence, and instruct the jury that it is sufficient by itself to support a conviction.
C. Exclude the detective’s testimony, because Rule 613(b) bars extrinsic evidence once a witness denies a prior statement.

## Set 5 · Q18 · Hearsay Exceptions — Rule 803(18): learned treatises
In a malpractice suit over a delayed diagnosis of appendicitis, the defense expert testifies that waiting twelve hours for an ultrasound met the standard of care. On cross-examination, the expert concedes that Harmon's Textbook of Emergency Medicine is a reliable authority in the field. Plaintiff's counsel then reads aloud a passage stating that suspected appendicitis in a child “requires imaging within four hours,” and moves to admit the textbook as an exhibit for the jury to take into deliberations.

How should the court rule?

A. The passage is inadmissible hearsay, because the expert didn't rely on the textbook in forming her opinion.
B. The passage is admissible only if its author testifies, because a treatise can't be cross-examined.
C. The passage is substantive evidence of the standard of care, but the textbook may not be received as an exhibit.
D. The textbook is admissible as an exhibit, because the expert conceded that it is a reliable authority.
E. The passage may be considered only to impeach the expert, not as proof of the standard of care.

Answer: C
- A: Reliance on direct is one route. The other is calling the passage to the expert's attention on cross-examination, which plaintiff's counsel did.
- B: No author testimony is needed. Reliability is established by the expert's admission, another expert's testimony, or judicial notice.
- C: Correct. Rule 803(18) admits a statement in a treatise that is called to an expert's attention on cross-examination (or relied on by the expert on direct) and established as a reliable authority by testimony, admission, or judicial notice. If admitted, the statement may be read into evidence but not received as an exhibit, so the jury won't give a book undue weight.
- D: Rule 803(18) says the statement “may be read into evidence but not received as an exhibit.” The expert's concession establishes reliability, not exhibit status.
- E: That was the common-law limit. Rule 803(18) makes a qualifying passage substantive evidence, admissible for its truth.
Rule: Rule 803(18): a statement in a treatise, periodical, or pamphlet is admissible for its truth if it is called to an expert's attention on cross or relied on by the expert on direct and is established as a reliable authority. It may be read into evidence but not received as an exhibit.

## Set 5 · Q19 · Hearsay Exceptions — Rule 803(5): read into evidence, not an exhibit
Dock supervisor Weller saw a box truck pull away from a loading bay moments after a pallet of laptops was stolen. While the truck was still in view, she wrote its license plate number on the back of a shipping invoice.

At the thief’s trial eleven months later, Weller testifies that she wrote the number down accurately while looking at the truck, but that she cannot remember the number now, even after looking at the invoice. The prosecution moves to admit the invoice and hand it to the jury.

How should the court rule?

A. Permit the notation to be read into evidence, but do not receive the invoice itself as an exhibit unless the defense offers it.
B. Exclude the invoice, because a writing that fails to refresh a witness’s memory cannot be used for any purpose.
C. Admit the invoice as an exhibit, because it satisfies the recorded-recollection exception.
D. Exclude the invoice, because Weller remembers the theft, and the recorded-recollection exception applies only when the witness has no memory of the events.
E. Admit the invoice as a business record, because it was written on a company form during the workday.

Answer: A
- A: Correct. This is the final sentence of Rule 803(5).
- B: Failure to refresh is exactly what triggers Rule 803(5). Refreshing under Rule 612 and recorded recollection are separate tools.
- C: The rule permits the record to be read into evidence, but only the adverse party may introduce it as an exhibit.
- D: The standard is insufficient memory to testify fully and accurately about the matter recorded, here the plate number. Total amnesia about the events is not required.
- E: One supervisor’s ad hoc notation of a plate is not a record of a regularly conducted activity made as a regular practice. Rule 803(6) does not fit.

If admitted, the record may be read into evidence but may be received as an exhibit only if offered by an adverse party. FRE 803(5).

## Set 5 · Q21 · Confrontation, Bruton, and the Defense — Melendez-Diaz and Bullcoming: a surrogate analyst

## Set 5 · Q22 · Confrontation, Bruton, and the Defense — Bruton redaction: a unique description
Rory Tate and Eli Burke are tried jointly for a bank robbery, and neither testifies. The prosecution offers Burke's confession to police, redacted to remove Tate's name: “I drove. The other guy, the one with the scorpion tattoo on his neck, went in with the gun.” Tate has a scorpion tattoo on his neck, which the jury can see, and no other defendant has one. The court gives a limiting instruction.

Which statement is most accurate?

A. There is no violation, because the jury must link the description to Tate through evidence outside the confession.
B. There is a Bruton violation only if the prosecutor points out the tattoo in closing argument.
C. There is no violation, because Samia permits replacing a defendant's name with a neutral descriptor.
D. There is a Bruton violation, because the description identifies Tate as clearly as his name would.
E. There is no violation, because Burke's confession stops being testimonial once it has been redacted.

C. Exclude it, because the 20-year authentication rule was repealed when the hearsay exception was narrowed.
D. Admit it, because a document kept in a public office is self-authenticating and admissible as a public record.
E. Exclude it, because a document can't be authenticated without a chain of custody from its author.

Answer: A
- A: Correct. Rule 901(b)(8) authenticates a document that is in a condition creating no suspicion, was in a place where it would likely be, and is at least 20 years old, so the 2003 letter is authenticated. But Rule 803(16) now covers only documents prepared before January 1, 1998, so the letter's assertions remain hearsay with no exception.
- B: That was true before December 1, 2017. Since then, Rule 803(16) covers only a document prepared before January 1, 1998, whose authenticity is established. Rule 901(b)(8)'s 20-year rule didn't change, so the two now diverge.
- C: The 2017 amendment narrowed only the hearsay exception in Rule 803(16). Rule 901(b)(8)'s 20-year authentication rule remains.
- D: A surveyor's private letter placed in a public file isn't a record of a public office's own activities or observations under Rule 803(8), and it isn't a certified copy under Rule 902(4).
- E: Ancient documents are authenticated by age, condition, and custody, without a strict chain of custody, as United States v. Stelmokas held for wartime archival records.
Rule: Authentication and hearsay are separate hurdles. Rule 901(b)(8) authenticates documents at least 20 years old found in natural custody and in unsuspicious condition. Rule 803(16) excepts only documents prepared before January 1, 1998.

## Set 5 · Q28 · Privileges — Psychotherapist privilege: threats the patient knew would be relayed
Gene Rusk, in court-ordered therapy, repeatedly told his psychologist he would “take out” the claims adjuster handling his disability benefits if his payments were cut. At the first session the psychologist had explained, and Rusk had acknowledged in writing, that she would warn anyone he threatened. She warned the adjuster, and Rusk was charged with making threats. At trial the government calls the psychologist to repeat his statements. Rusk invokes the psychotherapist-patient privilege.

How should the court rule?

A. Reject the privilege, because Rusk knew his threats would be passed on, so they weren't confidential.
B. Uphold the privilege unless the court finds, by balancing, that the government's need outweighs Rusk's privacy.
C. Uphold the privilege, because a duty to warn permits a warning but never testimony in a prosecution.
D. Reject the privilege, because federal courts have never recognized a psychotherapist-patient privilege.
E. Uphold the privilege, because Jaffee recognized an absolute privilege for statements made in psychotherapy.

Answer: A
- A: Correct. The psychotherapist-patient privilege covers only confidential communications. Rusk was told, and acknowledged, that threats would be passed on to their targets, so he couldn't have expected these statements to stay confidential. The Fifth Circuit so held in a case like this one.
- B: Jaffee rejected case-by-case balancing: making the privilege depend on a later weighing of need would eviscerate its effectiveness.
- C: That is the strongest argument for the privilege, and it has support. But on similar facts the Fifth Circuit held the threats unprivileged because the patient knew they would be relayed.
- D: Jaffee v. Redmond recognized the psychotherapist-patient privilege under Rule 501 and extended it to licensed clinical social workers.
- E: Jaffee protects confidential communications made in the course of psychotherapy. Confidentiality is an element, and a statement the patient knows will be disclosed lacks it.
Rule: The psychotherapist-patient privilege (Jaffee) protects confidential communications made in the course of psychotherapy, without case-by-case balancing. A statement the patient knows will be disclosed is not confidential.

## Set 5 · Q29 · Privileges — Privilege: a lawyer's permitted disclosure doesn't waive it
During a bitter custody fight, Elena Voight told her lawyer, “Sometimes I think I should just kill him and be done with it.” The lawyer told her he couldn't be part of anything like that. Later, believing she might be dangerous, he reported the remark to the family-court judge, as the state's ethics rules permitted. Months later Voight's husband is shot, and Voight is charged with conspiracy to murder him. The prosecution calls the lawyer to repeat her remark.

What is the strongest basis for keeping the remark out?

A. Statements made during divorce or custody proceedings are absolutely privileged.
B. The remark is inadmissible hearsay, because Voight made it out of court.
C. The privilege is hers, so the lawyer's disclosure didn't waive it, and she didn't seek his help in a crime.
D. A lawyer who discloses a client's confidence under an ethics rule commits malpractice, so his testimony is barred.
E. The lawyer-client privilege has no exceptions in criminal cases.

Answer: C
- A: There's no such blanket rule. Communications are privileged because of the lawyer-client relationship, not because of the kind of case.
- B: Offered by the prosecution against Voight, her own statement isn't hearsay under Rule 801(d)(2)(A). Privilege is the real issue.
- C: Correct. Only the client can waive the privilege, so a lawyer's ethically permitted disclosure to prevent harm doesn't waive her evidentiary privilege. And the crime-fraud exception applies only if the client sought the lawyer's advice or help to further a crime or fraud; a remark the lawyer rebuffed isn't an attempt to enlist him.
- D: Here the ethics rules permitted the disclosure. In any event, rules about when a lawyer may disclose don't decide whether the evidentiary privilege survives.
- E: The privilege does have exceptions in criminal cases, most importantly the crime-fraud exception. The argument is that it doesn't apply here.
Rule: The lawyer-client privilege belongs to the client, and only the client can waive it. A lawyer's permitted disclosure under an ethics rule doesn't waive the evidentiary privilege. The crime-fraud exception requires that the client sought the lawyer's services to further a crime or fraud.

## Set 5 · Q30 · Privileges — Marital confidences: relayed through a friend
Phil Carver is charged with murder. His wife, Dee, is willing to testify, but Phil invokes the marital confidences privilege to bar her from repeating what he told her privately the night of the killing. The prosecution then calls Dee's friend, who will testify that the next morning Dee burst into tears and blurted, “He did it! Phil told me he killed that woman!” Phil never consented to Dee's disclosure.

How should the court rule on the friend's testimony?

A. Admit it, because Dee waived the privilege by voluntarily telling her friend.
B. Admit it, because Dee's outburst is an excited utterance, and a hearsay exception overrides the privilege.
C. Exclude it, because a spouse's statements about the other spouse are always inadmissible hearsay.
D. Exclude it, because it would disclose Phil's confidential communication, and he never waived the privilege.
E. Admit it, because the marital confidences privilege bars only testimony by a spouse, not by others.

Answer: D
- A: Under the federal view, either spouse may prevent the other from disclosing the confidential communication. A disclosure the communicating spouse never consented to doesn't waive his privilege; the proposed waiver rule, which Congress never enacted, likewise required the holder's own voluntary disclosure.
- B: Even if the outburst qualifies under Rule 803(2), a hearsay exception only removes the hearsay objection. It doesn't overcome a privilege.
- C: There is no such categorical rule. The friend's account might fit a hearsay exception such as Rule 803(2); the obstacle is the privilege.
- D: Correct. Phil holds the privilege and can bar disclosure of his confidential communication. Dee's unauthorized disclosure to a friend didn't waive it, and letting the friend repeat it would let the prosecution do indirectly what it can't do through Dee. The privilege, not the hearsay rule, decides the question.
- E: The privilege protects the communication, not just a spouse's testimony. Letting a third party relay it would gut the protection.
Rule: The marital confidences privilege protects the communication itself. A disclosure by the other spouse without the communicating spouse's consent doesn't waive it, and a hearsay exception doesn't overcome a privilege.
