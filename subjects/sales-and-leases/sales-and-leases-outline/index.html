Exam game plan — run every fact pattern through this sequence

1. Governing law? Goods (§ 2-105, § 2-107) · hybrid (§ 2-102(2)) · disguised security interest (§ 2-102(3)(a)).

2. Agreement formed? §§ 2-204 → 2-206 → 2-205 → 2-207.

4. Terms? Express terms → CoP/CoD/UoT (§ 1-303) → express warranties (§ 2-313) → gap fillers → disclaimers/remedy limits → parol evidence rule (§ 2-202).

5. Changed? Modification · SOF/NOM · waiver · estoppel · course of performance.

I. The UCC & Which Law Governs

week 1

The UCC and the common law

  • What it is: a model code (ULC + ALI) adopted by the states to standardize commerce and protect those in business transactions. Not law until enacted; check each state's version. Louisiana never enacted Art. 2/2A.
  • § 1-103(a) purposes (construe liberally to promote): (1) simplify, clarify, modernize commercial law; (2) permit continued expansion of commercial practices through custom, usage, and agreement; (3) make the law uniform among jurisdictions.
  • § 1-103(b): unless displaced by a particular UCC provision, common law and equity (capacity, agency, estoppel, fraud, misrepresentation, duress, coercion, mistake, bankruptcy, other validating/invalidating causes) supplement the Code.
  • Sales law is broader than commercial law — buying and selling goods by individuals, businesses, and others; a cumulative subject, not one law.
  • Role of sales systems: bring buyers and sellers together; supply standard terms; supply mechanisms for performing; supply mechanisms for resolving disputes.
  • Recurring themes: certainty · responsiveness · laissez-faire · simplicity · efficiency · international.

Statutory interpretation (slides + text)

  • Three rules: (1) read it all, then read it again — parse grammar, find the pauses, test hypotheticals; (2) find the definitions (§ 1-201 for all Articles; §§ 2-103 to 2-106; "purchase" includes a gift; "goods" includes unborn animals); (3) read in context of the provision's and the Code's purposes.
  • Then consult: Official Comments (most important aid, but not law — the text controls if they conflict), case law (other states are persuasive), treatises, state legislative history, earlier drafts; last resort, a tie-breaker policy.
  • Keep the client's goals in view. Good drafting: understand the deal; know the rules and risk-allocation options; attention to detail; concise, clear, simple language — goal: an enforceable agreement that clearly reflects the deal.

Which law governs — four factors (in this order)

1. Type of property (goods, real estate, services, IP…);

2. Nature of the rights transferred (sale, lease, license, lien, gift);

3. Location of the parties (domestic vs. international/CISG);

4. The parties' agreement on governing law.

Is it a "good"? — § 2-105(1), § 2-107

rule

Goods = all things (including specially manufactured goods) movable at the time of identification to the contract, except (1) the money in which the price is to be paid, (2) investment securities (Art. 8), and (3) things in action. Also includes unborn young of animals, growing crops, and things attached to realty under § 2-107.

  • § 2-107(1): minerals (oil, gas) or a structure to be removed from realty → goods only if severed by the SELLER.
  • § 2-107(2): growing crops, timber to be cut, other things severable without material harm → goods whether buyer or seller severs.

2022 Amendments — § 2-102(2)

Two tiers

Goods predominate → Art. 2 applies. Goods don't predominate → only provisions relating primarily to the goods apply.

  • § 2-106(5) "hybrid transaction": a sale of goods plus services, a lease of other goods, or a sale/lease/license of non-goods property (provided by the seller).
  • Still apply when goods don't predominate (cmt. 5): warranties (§§ 2-312–2-318), tender & risk of loss, acceptance/rejection/cure, buyer's remedies for nondelivery/nonconforming goods. Don't apply: § 2-201, §§ 2-204–2-207, § 2-209.
  • Predominance factors (cmt. 3): agreement's language; share of price for the goods (an agreed allocation usually binds); parties' purposes; nature of businesses. Manufacturing services count toward goods; design services don't.
  • Problem 1-2 (Speedy Offset prints & binds 500 art books): printing/binding = services in manufacturing goods → goods predominate → Art. 2 applies; claims concern conformity of the books, so warranty rules apply either way.
  • Problem 1-4: Art. 2 governs merchant→merchant, merchant→consumer, consumer→merchant, and consumer→consumer (garage sale) — scope never depends on merchant status.
  • Disguised security interest: a "sale" with a mandatory repurchase at a higher price is really a secured loan → Art. 9, not Art. 2 (§ 2-102(3)(a)).

II. Formation

week 1

Common law vs. UCC; agreement vs. contract

Common law

UCC Article 2

Mutual assent; based on promise

Based on bargain, as to goods

Mirror-image rule — acceptance must match the offer

Contract formed "in any manner sufficient to show agreement"; acceptance need not mirror

All material terms needed

Open terms OK; fewer formalities; many ways to form

  • "Agreement" (§ 1-201(b)(3)) = the parties' bargain in fact, from language and CoP/CoD/UoT. "Contract" (§ 1-201(b)(12)) = the total legal obligation that results, as determined by the UCC and other law. The contract can be more (gap fillers, implied warranties) or less (SOF, unconscionable term) than the agreement.
  • Questions to ask: (1) Offer? (common law — Restatement § 24: manifestation of willingness to bargain that justifies another in understanding assent is invited and will conclude it) (2) Firm offer by a merchant (§ 2-205)? (3) Properly accepted (§ 2-206)? (4) Do the terms conflict (§ 2-207)?

§ 2-204 — formation in general

rule

(1) Contract may be made in any manner sufficient to show agreement, including conduct by both parties. (2) OK even if the moment of making is undetermined. (3) Open terms don't defeat a contract if the parties intended to contract AND there is a reasonably certain basis for giving an appropriate remedy.

§ 2-206 — offer & acceptance

  • (1)(a) Unless unambiguously indicated otherwise, acceptance in any reasonable manner and medium. (Offeror can still be "master of the offer.")
  • (1)(b) An order for prompt or current shipment may be accepted by a prompt promise to ship or by prompt shipment of conforming OR nonconforming goods — but shipping nonconforming goods is not an acceptance if seller seasonably notifies it's an accommodation. (No notice → acceptance and breach.)
  • (2) Where beginning performance is a reasonable mode of acceptance, an offeror not notified within a reasonable time may treat the offer as lapsed.

§ 2-205 — firm offers

rule

An offer by a merchant in a signed record that by its terms assures it will be held open is irrevocable without consideration for the time stated (or a reasonable time) — never more than 3 months. If the assurance is on a form supplied by the offeree, the offeror must separately sign it.

  • Common law: irrevocable only with consideration (option contract). Only the offeror must be a merchant.

§ 2-207 — battle of the forms

  • (1) A definite and seasonable expression of acceptance (or written confirmation) is an acceptance even with additional/different terms, unless expressly made conditional on assent to them. → Abolishes mirror image & "last shot."
  • (2) Additional terms = proposals. Between merchants they become part of the contract unless (a) the offer expressly limits acceptance to its terms, (b) they materially alter it, or (c) objection is given within a reasonable time.
  • (3) If the writings don't form a contract but conduct recognizes one → contract on the terms where the writings agree + UCC gap fillers.
  • Three questions: (1) Agreement despite deviation? (2) Which terms control? (3) If writings fail, does conduct create a contract?
case

Daitom v. Pennwalt (10th Cir. 1984): "expressly conditional" requires a clear statement of unwillingness to proceed without the new terms (Dorton) — boilerplate isn't enough. Different (conflicting) terms → knock-out rule (cmt. 6): both drop out; gap fillers apply (there, the 4-year § 2-725 SOL replaced a 1-year limit).

  • Material alterations (cmt. 4): warranty disclaimers, 90–100% delivery guarantees where trade allows leeway, power to cancel on any late invoice, unusually short complaint periods. Not material (cmt. 5): reasonable force-majeure terms, customary complaint periods, interest on overdue invoices, limits on rejection within trade tolerances.
  • How UCC changes offer/acceptance: any reasonable medium (2-206(1)(a)); accept by shipping (2-206(1)(b)); firm offers (2-205); no mirror image (2-207(1)); confirmation = acceptance (2-207(1)).

III. Statute of Frauds & Unconscionability

weeks 1–2

§ 2-201(1) — the rule

rule

A contract for the sale of goods for $500 or more is not enforceable by action or defense unless there is a record (1) sufficient to indicate a contract for sale was made, (2) signed by the party against whom enforcement is sought (or its agent/broker). A record isn't insufficient because it omits or misstates a term, but the contract isn't enforceable beyond the quantity shown.

  • Only quantity is essential; price/delivery can be missing or wrong. "Record" includes email (2022 amendments; UETA elsewhere); a typed name is a signature. Several papers can be read together if connected (Casazza).
  • Satisfying the SOF only opens the courthouse door — plaintiff must still prove the contract.
  • Flowchart: SOF applies? No → possible contract. Yes → sufficient signed record? Yes → possible contract. No → exception? Yes → possible contract; No → no enforceable contract.

case

Casazza v. Kiser (8th Cir. 2002): accepting the boat's navigation software wasn't part performance of the boat sale (not one commercial unit); no signed writing; hoping defendant will admit in discovery isn't enough; promissory estoppel didn't save the claim.

Unconscionability — § 2-302

  • Court decides as a matter of law, judged at the time of contracting; may refuse the whole contract, strike the clause, or limit it; parties get to present evidence of commercial setting.
  • Procedural (unfair surprise: fine print, no meaningful choice, unequal bargaining power) + substantive (oppressive, one-sided terms). Most courts want both (some use a sliding scale). Cannot be contracted away.

  • They explain and supplement the agreement. Construe consistently if reasonable; if not: Express terms > Course of performance > Course of dealing > Usage of trade (§ 1-303(e)). CoP can show waiver or modification, subject to § 2-209 (§ 1-303(f)). (Slides cite old §§ 1-205/2-208 — now § 1-303.)

Good faith — § 1-304; § 1-201(b)(20)

rule

Every contract imposes a duty of good faith in its performance and enforcement = honesty in fact (subjective — motive) + observance of reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing (objective).

  • A lens for interpreting the agreement — not a stand-alone claim; can't contradict express terms; applies to performance and enforcement, not negotiation.
  • Cannot be disclaimed, but parties may set standards for it if not manifestly unreasonable (§ 1-302(b)).
  • Objective prong = fair dealing, not negligence. Some states kept a subjective-only Art. 1 definition (merchants still held to the Art. 2 objective standard).
  • Market Street v. Frey (7th Cir., Posner): good faith lies between a fiduciary duty and merely avoiding fraud — you may exploit superior knowledge before contracting, but deliberately exploiting your partner's oversight during performance is bad faith.

Modification — § 2-209

rule

(1) A modification needs no consideration. (2) A signed agreement requiring modifications by signed record (NOM clause) is enforceable — but on a merchant's form, a non-merchant must separately sign it. (3) The SOF applies if the contract as modified is within § 2-201. (4) A failed modification can operate as a waiver. (5) A waiver of an executory term may be retracted by reasonable notification that strict performance is required, unless unjust because of a material change of position in reliance.

  • Analysis: (1) Did they agree to modify (formation rules §§ 2-204–2-207 apply)? (2) Is it enforceable — no consideration needed; must be in good faith; barred by SOF or NOM clause?
  • Common law contrast: modifications needed consideration (or R2d § 89 "fair and equitable"); NOM clauses were unenforceable.
  • Good-faith policing (cmt. 2): extortion without a legitimate commercial reason fails; a market shift that makes performance a loss can justify seeking a modification even if it wouldn't excuse performance.
case

Roth Steel v. Sharon Steel (6th Cir. 1983): good-faith test for seeking a modification — (1) objective: a reason that would lead an ordinary merchant to seek it (performance has come to involve a loss) ✓; (2) subjective (honesty in fact): genuine reason, not pretext, and no coercion/extortion. Sharon threatened to stop shipping → bad faith not rebutted → modification unenforceable.

Problem 3-5 (10,000 widgets/month, delivery to buyer by the 5th):

  • (A) One late delivery accepted: no course of performance (single occasion), no modification, no waiver of damages — buyer keeps its claim if it gives timely notice.

V. Express Warranties — § 2-313

week 2
rule

Created by (a) an affirmation of fact or promise relating to the goods, (b) a description of the goods, or (c) a sample or model — each must be part of the basis of the bargain. No formal words ("warrant") or intent needed. Not warranties: affirmations of value, or the seller's opinion or commendation.

  • § 2-313 rejects caveat emptor; it turns many representations (tort) into warranties (contract). Statements made after the deal (e.g., at delivery) can be warranties as modifications needing no consideration (cmt. 7).

case

Rogath v. Siebenmann (2d Cir. 1997) (painting attributed to Francis Bacon): the question is not whether the buyer believed the warranted fact, but whether he bought the seller's promise. A buyer who closes knowing the facts from the seller that make the warranty false waives the breach unless he expressly preserves his rights; knowledge from a third party or common knowledge does not bar recovery (warranty as insurance). Remanded on what the seller told the buyer.

  • Tobacco ads slide: specific health claims ("immediate relief in cases of asthma"; "20,679 physicians say Luckies are less irritating") look like facts; "It's toasted" is puffery.

VI. Gap Fillers & Implied Warranties

weeks 2–3

How gap fillers work

  • Open terms don't defeat a contract if the parties intended to contract and there's a reasonably certain basis for a remedy (§ 2-204(3)). Usage of trade and course of dealing fill some gaps; UCC gap fillers fill the rest. No gap = no gap filler.
  • Key gap fillers: warranties (§§ 2-312 title · 2-313 express* · 2-314 merchantability · 2-315 fitness) · price (§ 2-305) · output/requirements (§ 2-306) · price payable in money or otherwise (§ 2-304) · single lot (§ 2-307) · place (§ 2-308) · time (§ 2-309) · payment time (§ 2-310) · cooperation (§ 2-311) · tender/payment conditions (§§ 2-507, 2-511) · payment before inspection (§ 2-512). *Express warranties aren't gap fillers; § 2-313's creation rules are.
  • Problem 3-8: (A) signed deal for a skid loader, silent on delivery → enforceable; § 2-308 → delivery at seller's place of business → buyer breached by refusing to pick up. (B) history of seller delivering → course of dealing fills the term → seller breached.

  • Cumulation (§ 2-317): specs beat samples/general descriptions; a sample from bulk beats general description; express warranties displace inconsistent implied warranties except fitness.

Merchantability — § 2-314

rule

Goods must at least: (a) pass without objection in the trade under the description; (b) if fungible, be of fair average quality; (c) be fit for the ordinary purposes for which such goods are used (most important); (d) run of even kind, quality, quantity within permitted variations; (e) be adequately contained, packaged, labeled; (f) conform to promises/affirmations on the container or label.

  • Strict liability: usually a defect at the time of sale (or failure to give proper instructions); seller's lack of knowledge is no defense — not negligence-based.
  • Who is a merchant (§ 2-104(1)): one who deals in goods of the kind, or by occupation holds itself out as having knowledge/skill about the practices or goods, or to whom such skill is attributed through an agent/broker. For § 2-314 the narrow "goods" sense applies; isolated sellers aren't merchants (but must disclose known hidden defects — cmt. 3).
  • Used goods: warranty applies, but only to the quality appropriate for used goods.
  • Examples: detergent must clean, not damage fabric or the washer, and match its label; a pencil must write and erase with ordinary effort.

Fitness for a particular purpose — § 2-315

  • Four elements: seller has reason to know the purpose · buyer has a particular (not ordinary) purpose · seller has reason to know of reliance · buyer actually relies. Seller need not be a merchant; reliance need not be reasonable.
  • Particular vs. ordinary (cmt. 2): shoes for walking vs. for mountain climbing. The use needn't be unique — just different from customary use (Ingram River v. Pott).
  • No reliance if buyer gives precise specifications or insists on a brand.
  • Disclaiming fitness: must be in writing and conspicuous; needn't mention "fitness" — e.g., "There are no warranties which extend beyond the description on the face hereof"; "as is" also works.

Title and the power to transfer — § 2-312, § 2-403

  • Approach: (1) what rights were purported to be transferred (§ 2-403); (2) did buyer actually get good title (§ 2-312)? (3) if not, breach. Most courts: a colorable third-party claim breaches even if title proves good — buyer shouldn't have to litigate to protect title (cmt. 1).
  • § 2-403(1): a purchaser gets the title its transferor had or had power to transfer; a buyer of a limited interest gets only that. A transferor with voidable title (fraud, dishonored check, identity deception, "cash sale," larcenous fraud) can pass good title to a good-faith purchaser for value. A thief has void title — passes nothing.
  • Entrustment § 2-403(2)–(3): entrusting goods to a merchant who deals in goods of that kind lets it pass all the entruster's rights to a buyer in ordinary course (jewelry left for repair, sold by the jeweler).
  • "Purchase" includes sale, lease, pledge, security interest, gift, any voluntary transaction (§ 1-201(b)(29)).
  • Infringement claims: the claim must cast a "substantial shadow" on buyer's use; buyer need not prove the claim valid — only that it's substantial and reasonably likely to bring litigation.

VII. Quantity & Price

week 3

Output & requirements — § 2-306

rule

Quantity measured by output or requirements = actual output/requirements occurring in good faith, except no quantity unreasonably disproportionate to a stated estimate (or, if none, to normal or comparable prior output/requirements). Exclusive dealing implies best efforts by both sides.

  • Not really a gap filler — it interprets a quantity term the parties chose. Two limits: good faith and not unreasonably disproportionate (expectations of the parties; degree of risk).
  • A shutdown for lack of orders can be good faith; one merely to curtail losses isn't; sudden plant expansion isn't covered; a market price far above the contract price signals opportunism (cmt. 2). Decreases: some courts apply only good faith; others both tests.
  • Orange & Rockland factors (disproportion): size of excess over estimate · seller's ability to foresee it · market vs. contract price gap · whether the market rise was fortuitous · reason for the increase.
  • Problem 4-1: (a) fixed 50,000 sq ft/yr → buyer can't cut to 20,000 without seller's assent (fixed quantity; demand risk is buyer's). (b) "requirements," no estimate: 25,000 → 32,500 → 35,000 → 60,000 (plant capacity) when market rose to $1.15 → seller can resist as disproportionate and opportunistic. (c) drop to 5,000 in a recession at 90¢ market → seller argues disproportion and buying elsewhere; a genuine lack of orders may be good faith.
  • Problem 4-2 (90,000, then 100,000): good faith but disproportionate — buyer honestly lands a huge new customer and needs 400,000. Proportionate but bad faith — buyer orders a normal 110,000 to resell at a higher market price, or 85,000 because it's secretly buying cheaper elsewhere.

Open price — § 2-305

rule

If the parties intend to contract without a settled price, the price is a reasonable price at the time for delivery when (a) nothing is said, (b) it's left to be agreed and they fail to agree, or (c) an agreed market/third-party standard isn't set. (2) A price set by one party must be set in good faith. (3) If the price fails to be fixed through one party's fault, the other may cancel or fix a reasonable price. (4) If they intended not to be bound without agreement on price → no contract (return goods/payments).

  • Cmt. 3 safe harbor: in the "normal case," a posted/market price satisfies good faith.
case

Mathis v. Exxon (5th Cir. 2002): a price within competitors' range can still be bad faith if set with subjective intent to drive franchisees out (evidence: plan to replace them with company stores; price above rack + transport; barred buying from jobbers; franchisees unprofitable). Majority disagrees — Shell v. HRN (Tex.), Autry v. BP (11th Cir.): a commercially reasonable, non-discriminatory price is good faith regardless of motive.

IX. Risk of Loss

weeks 4–5

Principles

  • Risk of loss = who bears a casualty to the goods (fire, theft, flood) — not the same as performance or breach, and not tied to title.
  • Three principles: risk goes to the party who controls the goods (best able to avoid loss), who is most likely insured (§ 2-509 cmt. 3), and who is in breach (§ 2-510).
  • If seller bears the risk and goods are destroyed: unique/identified goods → seller excused (§ 2-613); fungible goods → seller must still tender. If buyer bears the risk → buyer must pay (§ 2-709(1)(a)).
  • Sequence: (1) What is seller's tender obligation (§§ 2-307–2-309, 2-503, 2-504)? (2) Has anyone breached (§ 2-509 or § 2-510)? (3) Apply the rule for carrier / bailee / seller-tender (merchant vs. non-merchant) / other agreement.
roadmap

Risk-of-loss decision tree

1. Agreement on risk? → follow it (§ 2-509(4)); sale on approval: risk stays with seller until acceptance (§ 2-327).

2. Seller in breach? Nonconforming tender → risk stays with seller until cure or acceptance (§ 2-510(1)). Rightful revocation → treat as on seller from the beginning, to the extent of buyer's insurance gap (§ 2-510(2)).

3. Buyer in breach (repudiates) as to identified conforming goods → risk on buyer for a commercially reasonable time, to the extent of seller's insurance gap (§ 2-510(3)).

4. Carrier, shipment contract (presumed) → passes on due delivery to the carrier (§ 2-509(1)(a)).

5. Carrier, destination contract → passes when duly tendered at the destination (§ 2-509(1)(b)).

6. Bailee, goods not moved → on receipt of a negotiable document, the bailee's acknowledgment, or a reasonable time after a non-negotiable document/delivery order (§ 2-509(2)).

7. Everything else (seller delivers or buyer picks up) → merchant seller: on buyer's RECEIPT (physical possession); non-merchant seller: on TENDER (§ 2-509(3)).

  • Framework: Contract + Breach + Damages + Causation. A disclaimer removes the breach (no warranty); a remedy limitation limits damages after a breach (liquidated damages, limited remedy, consequential exclusion). Always ask: what happened to the goods, and how are damages calculated?
  • Damages types: compensatory · incidental (reasonable costs of responding — inspection, transport, storage, arranging cover) · consequential (losses from needs the seller had reason to know at contracting that buyer couldn't reasonably prevent, plus injury to person/property from breach of warranty — e.g., lost profits from a production shutdown; fire from a defective oven) · liquidated.

XII. Parol Evidence Rule — § 2-202

weeks 6–8
rule

Terms in a record the parties intended as a final expression of their agreement (as to the terms it includes) may not be contradicted by evidence of any prior agreement or a contemporaneous oral agreement, but may be explained or supplemented by (a) course of dealing, usage of trade, or course of performance, and (b) consistent additional terms — unless the court finds the record was also intended as a complete and exclusive statement of the terms.

  • PER = admissibility of evidence about terms. SOF = enforceability. Merger clause = prior and contemporaneous agreements; NOM clause = future modifications.
  • Analysis: (1) What extrinsic evidence is offered? (2) For what purpose? The PER applies only to evidence of an alleged term of the agreement — not to evidence of a separate agreement, interpretation, a modification (later deal), a formation defect (fraud, mistake, duress), or a condition precedent.
  • Classify the writing: Unfinished (tentative draft) → PER doesn't apply · Final / partial integration (states its terms definitively but not the whole deal) → no contradiction, but consistent additional terms come in · Complete & exclusive / full integration → no contradiction or supplementation (except CoD/UoT/CoP).
  • Classify the evidence: explanatory · supplemental · contradictory. Some courts require ambiguity before admitting explanatory evidence (not for CoD/UoT/CoP — cmt. 1(c)). Tests for contradiction: directly negates a term · lacks reasonable harmony · parties would certainly have included it (cmt. 3) · vs. supplementary if it might naturally be omitted (R2d § 216).

XIII. Assignment & Delegation — § 2-210

week 8
  • Rights are assigned; duties are delegated. Structure: (1),(6) delegation · (2),(3) assignment · (4) reading prohibition language · (5) what an assignment "of the contract" transfers.
rule

Delegation (§ 2-210(1)): allowed unless otherwise agreed or the other party has a substantial interest in the original promisor performing. Delegation never relieves the delegator of its duty or liability. The other party may treat a delegating assignment as grounds for insecurity and demand assurances from the assignee (§ 2-210(6)).

rule

Assignment (§ 2-210(2)): all rights assignable unless it would materially change the other party's duty, materially increase its burden or risk, or materially impair its chance of return performance. A right to damages for breach of the whole contract, or a right from the assignor's full performance, is assignable despite a contrary agreement.

  • § 2-210(4): a ban on assigning "the contract" bars only delegation, not assignment of rights. "May not assign its rights" may only make an assignment a breach; to make it ineffective, say "any attempted assignment is void." § 9-406(d): a ban on assigning a right to payment (account) is ineffective — and assigning it is not a breach.
  • § 2-210(5): assigning "the contract" (or "all my rights") = assignment of rights plus delegation; the assignee's acceptance is a promise to perform, enforceable by the assignor or the other party.
  • The assignee steps into the assignor's shoes; once notified, the obligor must pay the assignee but keeps its defenses.

XV. Insecurity & Repudiation — §§ 2-609 to 2-611

week 8
rule

§ 2-609: If reasonable grounds for insecurity arise, a party may demand in writing adequate assurance and, if commercially reasonable, suspend its own performance until it gets it. Between merchants, grounds and adequacy are judged by commercial standards. Failure to give adequate assurance within a reasonable time, not over 30 days, after a justified demand = repudiation → aggrieved party may cancel and pursue remedies (§§ 2-610, 2-703(f), 2-711(1)).

  • Three questions: (1) reasonable grounds for insecurity? (2) assurances requested reasonable? (3) response adequate?
  • Grounds are objective and need not relate to this contract (e.g., late on other accounts; market conditions). Delegation is a statutory ground (§ 2-210(6)). Adequacy depends on the party's reputation and facts (cmt. 4). Contract clauses can define adequacy reasonably, but arbitrary standards are ineffective (cmt. 6). § 2-609 is optional — not a precondition to suing.
roadmap

§ 2-609 / § 2-610 flowchart

1. Reasonable grounds for insecurity? No → the request itself could be a repudiation.

2. Yes → demand in writing (or electronic record)? No → no right to suspend performance.

3. Yes → demand seeks only adequate assurance? No → demanding more than adequate can be a repudiation, and no right to suspend.

4. Yes → may suspend performance without breaching until adequate assurance arrives.

case

Top of Iowa Coop. v. Sime Farms (Iowa 2000): an inverted grain market plus public statements that the contracts might be illegal (and the farmer's silence) could give reasonable grounds — grounds need not be specific to the party. The Coop's demand (pay margins or post a letter of credit, and confirm delivery) wasn't unreasonable as a matter of law: "all demands for adequate assurance call for more than was originally promised." Contrast PDM v. Brookhaven: § 2-609 doesn't let a party redraft the contract (personal guarantee, escrow, ownership stake = too much).

  • Problem 6-3 (custom goods; pay 15 days after each delivery; buyer's credit downgraded, paying others late, changed banks): (a) don't demand cash on delivery — § 2-702(1) allows that only if buyer is insolvent; unilaterally changing credit terms risks repudiation → use § 2-609. (b) seller demands a letter of credit and withholds delivery; buyer only professes ability to pay → grounds are reasonable; an LOC is a common assurance (Top of Iowa) but insisting only on it may be too much; a bare promise from a shaky buyer is likely inadequate, but real proof of solvency may be adequate — then seller's continued withholding becomes the breach. (c) Demand letter: list the specific grounds; ask for adequate assurance with several options (LOC, guaranty, payment in advance/on delivery, "other assurances might also be adequate"); reserve the right to suspend; set a deadline within 30 days.
  • Problem 6-4 (Big Discount's one-sided clauses): the clause letting Big Discount demand "whatever assurances it deems appropriate" whenever it wants sets an arbitrary standard → ineffective (cmt. 6; § 1-302(b); cf. § 1-309). Barring the supplier from ever demanding assurance is vulnerable as unconscionable given the take-it-or-leave-it market power.

Repudiation — § 2-610; retraction — § 2-611

  • Not a failure to perform when due (that's breach). Repudiation = signaling before performance is due that you won't perform (words or acts). It also results from failing to answer a justified § 2-609 demand, or from making an unjustified or excessive demand. Demanding more than the contract gives is not by itself a repudiation — unless you condition your performance on it (cmt. 2).
  • If the repudiation substantially impairs the contract's value, the aggrieved party may suspend its performance and (a) await performance for a commercially reasonable time, or (b) resort to remedies immediately.
  • Retraction (§ 2-611): allowed until the next performance is due, unless the aggrieved party has canceled, materially changed position, or indicated the repudiation is final; must include any assurance justifiably demanded; reinstates the contract.
  • "Game of chicken": call it a repudiation and you may be the one in breach if a court disagrees.
  • Problem 6-5 (Ace's email: first valve installment will be late; "we expect you will cover the extra expenses"; will catch up by the second): a definite repudiation of installment 1; the cost demand isn't a repudiation unless performance is conditioned on it; canceling the whole requires substantial impairment of the whole contract (§§ 2-610, 2-612). Buyer's real motive (needing only half the valves) is not a valid reason. Advice: don't cancel yet — send a § 2-609 demand (perform at the contract price, give a catch-up schedule), reserve rights, suspend payments; if Ace conditions performance on extra money or gives no adequate assurance → repudiation → cancel, recover the 25% advance, cover.

  • Contracts allocate risk; excuse is applied sparingly. Foreseeability matters most but isn't decisive. All of these yield to contrary agreement (e.g., force majeure clauses). Excuse decides who pays damages; risk of loss decides who absorbs the goods' value.
rule

§ 2-613 — casualty to identified goods: goods identified when the contract was made, destroyed without fault of either party (negligence counts as fault) before risk passes → total loss: contract avoided; partial loss/deterioration: buyer may inspect and either avoid or accept with a price allowance (no further claim). Fungible goods → no excuse.

rule

§ 2-614 — substituted performance: if agreed loading/berthing facilities, carrier type, or manner of delivery becomes commercially impracticable without fault, a commercially reasonable substitute must be tendered and accepted. If the agreed payment method fails due to governmental regulation, seller may withhold delivery unless buyer offers a substantially equivalent payment method.

rule

§ 2-615 — impracticability (seller): delay or non-delivery isn't a breach if performance became impracticable due to a contingency whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption (or good-faith compliance with government regulation) — but seller must allocate fairly among customers if capacity is partly affected and seasonably notify buyers. Increased cost or market swings alone don't excuse (cmt. 4); war, embargo, crop failure, shutdown of major supply sources can. § 2-616: buyer may terminate or take its quota; silence for 30 days = contract lapses as to affected deliveries.

  • Buyer's excuse: common-law frustration of purpose (§ 1-103(b); R2d § 265); § 2-615 cmt. 9 — a supply contract tied to a specific construction venture may excuse the buyer. A fixed-price contract puts market-drop risk on the buyer (Posner, NIPSCO).

exam tips

Top traps

• Art. 2 covers consumer-to-consumer sales; merchant status matters only for specific rules.

• Minerals/structures are goods only if the seller severs; timber and crops either way.

• Firm offer: only the offeror must be a merchant; 3-month cap; offeree's form → separately signed.

• SOF record needs quantity + signature of the party charged — not price.

• Modification: no consideration, but good faith and the SOF if the modified deal is $500+.

• Accepting a late delivery isn't a waiver of damages, but notice of breach is required (§ 2-607(3)(a)).

• Good faith can't be disclaimed and isn't a stand-alone claim.

• Express warranty: no intent or magic words; opinion/value/praise don't count; reliance may not be required.

• Fitness: any seller; particular purpose + actual reliance. Title: every seller; "as is" doesn't disclaim it.

• Merchantability disclaimer must say "merchantability" (conspicuous if written); fitness needs a conspicuous writing.

• Examination exclusion requires an actual exam or refusal after seller's demand.

• Remedy limits needn't be conspicuous; consequential-damage exclusions for consumer personal injury are prima facie unconscionable.

• Possession ≠ acceptance; payment ≠ acceptance; failing to reject after inspection is acceptance.

• Risk: merchant seller → buyer's receipt — even if buyer paid; seller's own truck isn't a carrier; shipment contract is presumed.

• PER never bars later agreements or fraud; UoT/CoD/CoP can supplement even a complete writing.

• A ban on assigning "the contract" bars only delegation; the right to payment is assignable despite a "void" clause.

• § 2-609 demand must be in writing; no adequate assurance within 30 days = repudiation.

• Cost increases alone never excuse; § 2-613 needs identified goods + no fault + risk not yet passed.
