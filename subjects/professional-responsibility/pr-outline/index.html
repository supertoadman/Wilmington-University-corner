Part I — Regulating Lawyer Conduct

1. Sources of Law and the Model Rules

week 1

Reading: MR 8.1–8.5, 5.5; Chs. 1–3; Bradwell v. The State (1873). Where the rules come from, who enforces them, and which ones bind you.

Sources of law

  • States regulate lawyers (Leis v. Flynt), usually through the state's highest court. The ABA Model Rules bind no one on their own. Every state adopted a version, none verbatim, and a state's adopted rule, as its high court reads it, controls discipline there. The MPRE tests the ABA text.
  • Binding vs. persuasive. ABA and state or local bar ethics opinions (e.g., New York City, Los Angeles) and the Restatement (Third) of the Law Governing Lawyers are persuasive only. For "binding authority in New Jersey," only a New Jersey court decision counts; an ABA Formal Opinion (e.g., Op. 492) does not. A Model Rule violation as such can't be the basis for discipline; the state's version of the Rule is (Atkinson MC, slides).
  • Other regimes have their own ethics rules: federal and state judges, law clerks and court staff, bankruptcy lawyers, securities lawyers, and government lawyers (Part VII).
  • History: the first state code was Alabama's (1887); the 1908 ABA Canons were 32 aspirational precepts; the 1969 Model Code had Canons, Ethical Considerations ("should"), and Disciplinary Rules (only the DRs enforceable); the 1983 Model Rules (Rules + Comments, every Rule enforceable) followed Watergate.
  • The Rules govern discipline only. Civil liability, sanctions, and disqualification come from other law, though the Rules inform it.
rule

Preamble and Scope ¶ [20]

A violation "should not itself give rise to a cause of action against a lawyer nor should it create any presumption in such a case that a legal duty has been breached," and does not necessarily warrant disqualification. The Rules are relevant evidence of the standard of care, but there is no negligence per se.

2. Bar Admission and Discipline (MR 8.1, 8.2, 8.4)

week 1

Admission: character and fitness

  • Two requirements everywhere all required: (1) minimum competence (bar exam and MPRE); and (2) character and fitness. The applicant bears the burden.
  • Inquiries must rationally relate to fitness to practice (Schware). Lawful political membership, or responsibly seeking counseling, is not a ground for denial. Rehabilitation counts: bank robbers have been admitted, but Stephen Glass (the fabricating journalist) was not.
  • Candor matters most. Bars compare your law school application with your bar application, and nondisclosure usually hurts more than the underlying fact. Fix omissions now.
main case

Bradwell v. The State, 83 U.S. 130 (1873) — women barred from the Illinois bar

Source: assigned reading, Week 1 syllabus.

Facts: Illinois refused to license Myra Bradwell, a qualified applicant who edited a leading legal newspaper, because she was a married woman.

Held: The Supreme Court upheld the refusal: admission to a state's bar is not a privilege or immunity of national citizenship protected by the Fourteenth Amendment, so each state controls it. Justice Bradley's concurrence said "the paramount destiny and mission of woman are to fulfil the noble and benign offices of wife and mother."

Why it matters: States control admission, and the profession's gatekeeping has long carried bias. It is the historical counterpoint to the modern rule that admission criteria must rationally relate to fitness, and to MR 8.4(g) (Part XII).

Problem 3A-2 quick takes (slides)

The test is honesty, trustworthiness, and fitness, and candor about an item usually matters more than the item.

Item

Likely treatment

Student-loan default; 14 bounced checks

Not automatic bars, but can show financial irresponsibility or dishonesty. Bars ask whether the applicant dealt with them responsibly.

Recovering alcoholic; therapy and antidepressants

Responsibly seeking treatment is not a ground for denial. The question is current fitness. Disclose honestly when asked.

Minor, though a pattern can suggest disregard for the law.

rule

MR 8.1 — bar applicants and lawyers in disciplinary matters

In connection with a bar application or a disciplinary matter, a lawyer or applicant shall not (a) knowingly make a false statement of material fact; or (b) fail to disclose a fact necessary to correct a misapprehension known to have arisen, or knowingly fail to respond to a lawful demand for information from an admissions or disciplinary authority. But MR 8.1 does not require disclosing information protected by MR 1.6: you may decline to reveal a confidence; you may not lie.

  • Lexa MC (slides): an old lie to the press, then "No" to "Have you ever made a public false statement?" The most likely ground for denial is the knowing false statement of material fact on the application (MR 8.1(a)), not the original lie.
trap

Ignoring the grievance letter

Ignoring a bar grievance because the complaint is frivolous is itself a violation (MR 8.1(b)), even if the grievance is later dismissed. The Fifth Amendment must be asserted properly; it doesn't license silence.

Discipline: MR 8.4 misconduct

rule

MR 8.4 — it is professional misconduct to any one suffices:

(a) violate or attempt to violate the Rules, knowingly assist or induce another to do so, or do so through the acts of another (e.g., sending an investigator to contact a represented party you couldn't contact yourself, MR 4.2);

(b) commit a criminal act that reflects adversely on honesty, trustworthiness, or fitness as a lawyer (no charge or conviction needed);

(c) engage in conduct involving dishonesty, fraud, deceit, or misrepresentation;

(d) engage in conduct prejudicial to the administration of justice;

(e) state or imply an ability to influence improperly a government agency or official, or to achieve results by means that violate the Rules or other law (the statement alone is enough);

(f) knowingly assist a judge or judicial officer in conduct that violates the rules of judicial conduct; or

(g) engage in harassment or discrimination in conduct related to the practice of law (Part XII). Delaware's MR 8.4 has no paragraph (g) (slides).

  • Off-duty conduct counts (tax evasion, academic cheating). But MR 8.4(b) doesn't reach every crime: a petty civil-disobedience trespass usually doesn't reflect on fitness (MR 8.4 cmt [2]).
  • Process (in order): the complaint is privileged (no defamation suit against the complainant); then screening; investigation (cooperation required, subject to the Fifth Amendment); probable cause; and a hearing with due process (In re Ruffalo; Spevack v. Klein).
  • Sanctions protect the public; they aren't punishment. They run from private or public reprimand to suspension to disbarment, and a reprimand still follows you. Compensation comes from civil remedies.
  • MR 8.2(a): don't make a statement you know is false, or with reckless disregard for its truth, about the qualifications or integrity of a judge, adjudicatory officer, public legal officer, or candidate for judicial or legal office. Covered with advocacy in Part VIII.

3. Where You May Practice (MR 5.5, 8.5)

week 1
  • No national license. Practicing where you aren't admitted is UPL (a misdemeanor in most states). A firm office in State B doesn't license an A-only lawyer, and colleagues' licenses don't count.
  • Problem 3A-1 (slides): a PA-only lawyer can't practice in the firm's Camden office five minutes away. Borris MC: an out-of-state lawyer who advises clients from a Hawaii office, files papers in Hawaii courts, and shares the firm name is engaged in UPL even if he "only" handles his home state's law.
rule

MR 5.5 — unauthorized and multijurisdictional practice


(a) No practicing in a jurisdiction in violation of its regulation of the profession, or assisting another to do so.
(b) A lawyer not admitted may not establish an office or other systematic and continuous presence there for practicing law, or hold out as admitted there.
(c) A lawyer admitted and in good standing elsewhere may provide legal services on a temporary basis if the work any one suffices:
(1) is undertaken in association with an actively participating local lawyer;
(2) is in or reasonably related to a pending or potential proceeding before a tribunal where the lawyer is, or reasonably expects to be, authorized to appear (e.g., pro hac vice);
(3) is in or reasonably related to a pending or potential ADR proceeding, arising out of or reasonably related to the lawyer's home practice, that doesn't require pro hac vice admission; or
(4) otherwise arises out of or is reasonably related to the lawyer's home practice. (d) In-house lawyers for their employer, and services authorized by federal or other law, are also allowed.

  • Federal courts admit separately. Each federal court sets its own bar. Most district courts admit anyone admitted in the state where the court sits; some districts, and all federal courts of appeals, admit lawyers admitted in any state (slides). After removal, a state-admitted lawyer needs admission to that district or pro hac vice ("for this occasion only").
  • Problem 3A-3: Frank and his boss kept serving discovery after removal, so they need federal admission or pro hac vice (MR 5.5(a); see MR 5.5(c)(1)–(2)). Adam MC (slides): a lawyer not admitted to the district court can't make even a routine "coverage" appearance, and his partner's admission doesn't help.
  • McDade Amendment (28 U.S.C. § 530B): federal government lawyers follow the ethics rules of the states where they practice. Federal employment doesn't exempt anyone from a court's contempt power. (Federal lawyers may appear in any court nationwide: Part VII.)
rule

MR 8.5 — disciplinary authority and choice of law

(a) Authority. You are subject to discipline where you are admitted (wherever the conduct occurs) and where you provide or offer services. Both states can discipline you; most states impose reciprocal discipline.

(b) Choice of law. (1) For conduct in connection with a matter pending before a tribunal, apply the rules of the jurisdiction where the tribunal sits, unless the tribunal's rules provide otherwise. (2) For any other conduct, apply the rules of the jurisdiction where the conduct occurred or, if its predominant effect is elsewhere, the rules of that jurisdiction. Safe harbor: no discipline if your conduct conforms to the rules of a jurisdiction where you reasonably believe the predominant effect will occur.

4. Consequences: Civil Liability, Sanctions, and Other Law

week 1

"Malpractice" is just a collective term. Always name the specific duty breached and the remedy (Ch. 3).

Claim

Elements and key points

Professional negligence

Duty, breach, actual and proximate cause, and economic damages (emotional distress almost never). A blown deadline means a case within a case: prove what you would have recovered and collected. A few states use "substantial factor" causation.

Breach of fiduciary duty

Loyalty, confidentiality, or candor. No negligence or loss required (e.g., a secret finder's fee from the other side). Remedies: disgorgement; punitive damages if intentional. ADA Anna MC (slides): secretly feeding the defense her research to "throw" a case her office ordered her to defend breached her duty of loyalty to her client, however just her motive; she should have withdrawn or quit.

Breach of contract

The engagement implies all lawyer duties; damages and limitations periods differ. An express guarantee can create liability without negligence (Hawkins v. McGee). Mr. Kent MC: disclosing an invention despite a confidentiality clause supports breach of contract as well as fiduciary breach.

Abuse of process

An ulterior purpose plus an improper act in using process (e.g., an unauthorized lis pendens to pressure a debtor).

Malicious prosecution

A civil or criminal proceeding all required: (1) begun (in some states, continued) against the plaintiff; (2) by, or abetted by, the defendant; (3) without probable cause; (4) primarily for a malicious purpose; (5) terminated on the merits in the plaintiff's favor; (6) causing damages. Max-and-Chip MC: a spiteful frivolous suit, summarily dismissed, supports malicious prosecution, not fiduciary or contract claims (Chip was never the lawyer's client).

Disqualification

A court order to get out or stay out. Prophylactic: the opportunity and temptation to act adversely is enough, with no harm required. Mother and daughter on opposite sides of one case, both staying in despite an admitted conflict: the likely remedy is disqualification of one or both (MR 1.7(a)(2)), not recusal or discipline (slides).

Fee consequences

Setoff, reduction to actual value, or, for a serious fiduciary breach, forfeiture plus disgorgement of fees already paid. Typical fights: inefficiency, overstaffing, unnecessary tasks, or tasks the client said not to do.

Restitution

An improperly obtained benefit can be disgorged as unjust enrichment even without provable loss. Emotional distress becomes recoverable in some states if the breach was intentional; punitive damages almost always require intentional (in a few states, reckless) wrongdoing.

Court sanctions and other law

  • FRCP 11(b)–(c): certification after reasonable inquiry; monetary or nonmonetary sanctions on a party's motion or the court's own; the firm is jointly responsible absent exceptional circumstances. It reaches pro se parties too (Huey MC).
  • FRCP 26(g), 37: discovery certification and misconduct, from compensatory up to terminating sanctions. Lee MC: withholding a responsive, non-privileged memo brings discovery sanctions against the lawyer and violates MR 3.4(a); it isn't "malpractice" owed to the opponent.
  • 28 U.S.C. § 1927: excess costs for "unreasonably and vexatiously" multiplying proceedings.
  • Inherent power: a civil fee sanction must be compensatory, limited to fees the misconduct caused; punitive contempt requires criminal procedure; FRCrP 42(b) allows summary contempt in the court's presence.
note case · week 1

Goodyear Tire & Rubber Co. v. Haeger, 581 U.S. 101 (2017) — inherent-power fee sanctions

Facts: In a tire-failure suit, Goodyear and its lawyers withheld internal test results for years; after the case settled the plaintiffs learned of it and sought sanctions.

Held: A sanction under the court's inherent power to pay the other side's fees must be compensatory, covering only the fees the misconduct caused (a but-for test); anything more is punitive and requires criminal-type procedure.

Why it matters: The limit on civil sanctions in the taxonomy below: compensation through civil process, punishment through contempt.

  • Other law applies to you too, and a license narrows your freedom. Some states require you to report sanctions to the bar. Informal consequences (lost clients, partnership, reputation) can be worst of all; many referrals come from former opposing counsel.

note case · week 1

Hawkins v. McGee, 84 N.H. 114 (1929) — the "hairy hand" guarantee

Facts: A doctor promised a "hundred percent perfect hand" and the graft left the patient's hand hairy.

Held: An express promise of a result is enforceable in contract without proof of negligence.

Why it matters: Never promise results. Silence in the face of unrealistic expectations may be read as a guarantee, so correct them and use a no-guarantee clause.

5. Why Smart People Do Stupid Things (Ch. 4)

week 1

Good people do bad things, and orders don't excuse them.

Pressure or bias

What happens

Lawyer example

Diffusion of responsibility

Everyone assumes someone else will speak up, so no one does.

Colleagues "rounding up" hours; assuming other associates already reported a partner's racist comments (slides MC)

Conformity (Asch)

Subjects doubted their own eyes to match the group. One dissenter breaks the spell.

Everyone "fudges" billing

Authority (Milgram)

63% gave the maximum "XXX" shock when told "the experiment requires that you continue," though all predicted they'd refuse.

"That's just how it's done"

Cognitive dissonance

A good person who did something bad adopts a good-sounding justification, and the next time comes easier.

Perkins; Gellene; padding that starts as "borrowing"; "I do excellent work" to justify an affair with a client (slides MC)

Ethical fading / framing

Recasting a choice so the ethics drop out.

The Nevada lawyer with a gun at a deposition "forgot" he was a lawyer (6-month suspension); "Don't you want to win?" to justify coaching a witness to "bend the truth" (slides MC)

  • Zeal is no excuse. MR 1.3 cmt [1]: diligence doesn't require offensive tactics or preclude "courtesy and respect." Weasel words ("borrowing," "technically") mean you're too close to the line. MR 4.4(a) forbids means with no substantial purpose other than to embarrass, delay, or burden (Part XI), and courts are increasingly impatient with incivility (Lasalle v. Vogel, Cal. Ct. App. 2019: "an insidious decline in the standards of the profession").
  • Billing is where the biases bite (slides). Record time contemporaneously, in tenths. A mentor who says "firm practice is to round 10 hours up to 15" is asking you to misrepresent your time; you are personally responsible (MR 5.2(a), 8.4(c), 1.5(a)), whoever suggests it.
note case · week 1

Berkey Photo, Inc. v. Eastman Kodak Co. (S.D.N.Y. 1977) — the Kodak-Berkey discovery scandal

Facts: Kodak's lead partner, Mahlon Perkins, withheld an expert's documents and then swore they had been destroyed. Associate Joseph Fortenberry knew the documents existed, whispered a warning, and did nothing further.

Held: Perkins resigned, served a month in prison, and lost his license.

Why it matters: MR 5.2(a): orders don't excuse you. Fortenberry gets no MR 5.2(b) safe harbor, because withholding documents and a perjured affidavit is not an "arguable question" Perkins "resolved." A note left on a chair isn't enough: raise it directly, then go up the chain (another partner, the firm's ethics partner or general counsel) until the documents are produced and the false statements corrected (MR 3.3, 3.4(a)) (Problem 4A-1, slides).

The responsibility rules (first look; full treatment in Part IX)

  • MR 5.2(a): a lawyer is bound by the Rules even when acting at another's direction. MR 5.2(b): the narrow safe harbor covers only a subordinate acting on a supervisor's reasonable resolution of an arguable question of professional duty. It never covers a clear violation such as destroying evidence or lying.
  • MR 5.1, 5.3: partners, managers, and supervisors must make reasonable efforts to ensure compliance by lawyers and nonlawyers, and answer for violations they order, ratify, or fail to mitigate (Part IX).
  • MR 1.16(a)(2): a lawyer whose physical or mental condition materially impairs the representation must decline or withdraw.
  • Civil: you are personally liable for your own torts, even at a supervisor's direction; supervisors are liable for torts they directed or reasonably could have prevented; the firm is vicariously liable.

Reporting misconduct: MR 8.3

rule

MR 8.3 — the duty to report


(a) A lawyer who knows that another lawyer has committed a violation of the Rules that raises a substantial question as to that lawyer's honesty, trustworthiness, or fitness as a lawyer in other respects shall inform the appropriate professional authority.
(b) The same duty applies to a judge's violation of the judicial conduct rules that raises a substantial question as to fitness for office.
(c) The duty does not require disclosing (1) information otherwise protected by MR 1.6, or (2) information gained while participating in an approved lawyers assistance program.

  • Only about half the states have adopted MR 8.3, but the MPRE tests it. Theft and forgery qualify; a one-time cured calendaring slip doesn't. Telling the wrongdoer's supervisor isn't "the appropriate professional authority." "Knows" includes willful blindness. Your client telling you the prior lawyer stole from her is MR 1.6 information, so no duty to report without her consent. The details are in Part XI (Ch. 16).

Problem 4D-1 (Ted Toughtimes)

Partner Ted is distracted, irritable, and returning from lunch unsteady and smelling of liquor. Partner Sarah gives him a new matter "to help his hours" and assigns associate Maya to help.

  • Ted: must decline if his condition materially impairs him (MR 1.16(a)(2)); he risks MR 1.1 and 1.3 violations.
  • Sarah: as a partner and the assigning lawyer, she risks MR 5.1(a)–(b) (no reasonable measures or supervision), and MR 5.1(c)(2) if she knows of a violation in time to fix it and doesn't. She also risks civil liability and fee consequences for the firm. She should reassign or closely supervise the matter and steer Ted to help (a LAP or EAP).
  • Maya: bound by MR 5.2(a); she can't let the client's matter suffer because Ted is the lead. She should raise concerns with Sarah or the firm's ethics partner. MR 8.3 applies only if she knows of a violation raising a substantial question about fitness, and LAP information is exempt (MR 8.3(c)).

When you mess up, 'fess up

  • Error festers in darkness, and concealment almost always turns out worse. Confess in this order all required:
    (1) your supervisor;
    (2) the firm's ethics partner or committee;
    (3) the malpractice insurer (many policies require notice before you admit error to the client); and
    (4) outside counsel retained for advice, so the conversation is privileged. Then the client must be told of any material error (MR 1.4; Part IV).
  • Places to go for help (slides): your supervising lawyer (unless she's the one leading you astray), your firm's "ethics czar," the bar's or your insurer's ethics hotline, or your own lawyer. MR 1.6(b)(4) lets you reveal what's reasonably necessary to get advice about your own compliance.
  • Why confess early: the mistake may have no consequences, may not be a mistake, or can be fixed. Clients trust a lawyer who comes clean, explains the fix, and doesn't charge for fixing it.

Stress, impairment, and getting help

  • Practice stress mirrors law school stress: more stress means less sleep, and less sleep means fuzzy thinking and worse ethical judgment.
  • Warning signs of depression and anxiety: changes in sleep or eating, trouble making decisions, withdrawal. Flashbacks of a traumatic event point to PTSD, so it's the "not a common sign" answer (slides MC).
  • Get help: student services, EAPs, and Lawyer Assistance Programs. LAP help is confidential and generally isn't reported to the disciplinary authority while the lawyer complies with treatment (Linda MC); MR 8.3(c) exempts LAP information from the reporting duty.
  • Responsibly seeking treatment isn't a ground for denying admission, and impairment that materially affects the work triggers MR 1.16(a)(2).

7. Competence and Diligence (MR 1.1, 1.3)

week 2

Ch. 7. Be the lawyer you'd want to hire. Competence and diligence are disciplinary duties, and no harm is required. Care is the civil duty, and causation of monetary harm is required. Criminal defense adds the Sixth Amendment.

Competence: MR 1.1

rule

MR 1.1

"Competent representation requires the legal knowledge, skill, thoroughness and preparation reasonably necessary for the representation."

  • You can become competent (cmts [2], [4]): "A newly admitted lawyer can be as competent as a practitioner with long experience." You may accept a matter if competence can be achieved by reasonable preparation, or by associating with a lawyer of established competence. Neither client consent nor pro bono status waives the duty.
  • Cmt [1] factors weighed together:
    (1) the relative complexity and specialized nature of the matter;
    (2) the lawyer's general experience;
    (3) the lawyer's training and experience in the field;
    (4) the preparation and study the lawyer is able to give the matter; and
    (5) whether it is feasible to refer the matter to, or associate or consult with, a lawyer of established competence. "In many instances, the required proficiency is that of a general practitioner."
  • Cmt [2]: analysis of precedent, evaluation of evidence, and drafting are required in every matter, and "perhaps the most fundamental legal skill" is determining what kind of legal problems a situation involves. Cmt [5] all required:
    (1) inquiry into and analysis of the facts and law;
    (2) methods meeting the standards of competent practitioners; and
    (3) preparation proportional to what is at stake. A MR 1.2(c) scope limit can narrow what you're responsible for.
  • Emergency (cmt [3]): when referral or consultation is impractical, you may give advice beyond your experience, limited to what's reasonably necessary.
  • The "duty to be supervised": ask, insist, or find help elsewhere. "I asked and asked" is no defense (MR 5.2(a)). A looming deadline requires protective action, not letting it lapse.
  • Learning time vs. fees: becoming competent doesn't justify billing an unreasonable amount of learning time. Competence (MR 1.1) and fee reasonableness (MR 1.5(a)) are separate questions.
  • Forms ("go-bys") all required:
    (1) make sure it's a good example and fits;
    (2) never leave in, or simply delete, language you don't understand, and ask what's missing;
    (3) read any statute or case it relies on; and
    (4) customize it and check that the law hasn't changed. Thoughtless form use is a textbook competence and care failure.
  • Technology (cmt [8]): keep up with "changes in the law and its practice, including the benefits and risks associated with relevant technology." Scrub metadata (hidden comments can reveal settlement authority: MR 1.1 and 1.6(c)), secure data, and use real passwords. ABA Op. 512 (2024) is the first opinion on generative AI; Op. 18-483 covers data breaches (reasonable security, monitoring, client notice); Op. 477R covers reasonably secure email. Oklahoma v. Oliver: a lawyer suspended partly for technological incompetence. Alan MC (slides): a solo who sends a draft with unscrubbed client comments violates MR 1.1 even with no monetary harm.
  • Problem 7A-1 (Maya and sales tax): she may expand the engagement if she becomes competent through reasonable study (without billing all of it) or by associating with or consulting a tax lawyer, and should amend the engagement letter for the new scope. Liam MC: a trusts lawyer who takes a USPS slip-and-fall violated MR 1.1 by not learning that the FTCA requires an administrative claim first; telling the client it was his first tort case didn't lower the standard.
note case · week 2

Mata v. Avianca, Inc., 678 F. Supp. 3d 443 (S.D.N.Y. 2023) — AI-fabricated citations

Facts: Lawyers filed a brief citing six cases that ChatGPT had invented, then stood by them when the court and opposing counsel could not find them.

Held: The lawyers and their firm were sanctioned under Rule 11 for submitting the fake cases and for doubling down instead of correcting the record.

Why it matters: Technology competence (MR 1.1 cmt [8]), candor (MR 3.3(a)(1)), and supervision of tools (MR 5.3). A vendor's guarantee isn't a defense; check every cite.

Diligence: MR 1.3

  • "Reasonable diligence and promptness." Neglect and abandonment are among the most common sources of discipline. Cmt [3]: "Perhaps no professional shortcoming is more widely resented than procrastination." Ignoring calls for months violates MR 1.3 and 1.4 even if the client isn't harmed.
  • Use ticklers, calendars, and staff. Cmt [2]: "A lawyer's work load must be controlled so that each matter can be handled competently." Cmt [4]: when it's unclear whether the relationship continues, clarify, "preferably in writing."
  • Leanne MC (slides): the judge orally moved deadlines, she never told her assistant, and she missed the reply. That violates MR 1.3 even though the court allowed a late filing and she won. Delegating calendaring doesn't delegate responsibility.
  • Problem 7B-3 (Willie's unfunded living trust): he never retitled the assets, and the husband died eight months later. That breaches diligence (MR 1.3, so discipline) and care (damages for probate costs, delay, and taxes); the partner is personally liable and the firm vicariously so. "Busy practice" is exactly what cmt [2] forbids.
  • Problem 7B-2 (Stanley Sloppy): he let the limitations period run on a whiplash claim and ignored the client's calls: care, MR 1.3, and MR 1.4. To recover, Wanda must prove the case within a case all required:
    (1) the claim would have won;
    (2) for how much; and
    (3) the judgment would have been collectible.

8. The Civil Duty of Care and Effective Assistance

week 2

The duty of care

  • Standard: the skill, diligence, care, and knowledge of a reasonably careful and prudent lawyer in similar circumstances; a reasonable specialist's standard if you hold yourself out as one or were hired as one.
  • Losing isn't negligence. The standard guarantees no result and is only the minimum. Louise MC: a reasoned strategic choice not to impeach, discussed with the client beforehand, doesn't establish malpractice; the client must prove both breach and causation.
  • Never promise results (Hawkins v. McGee, Part I). Correct unrealistic expectations promptly and put a no-guarantee clause in the engagement letter.
  • Causation: a case within a case. A worthless underlying claim means no damages, but discipline remains possible.

The Sixth Amendment: ineffective assistance

  • The right attaches at the start of formal adversary proceedings (formal charge, preliminary hearing, indictment, information, or arraignment) and applies in and out of court through trial, sentencing, and the first appeal ("all of the above," slides).
  • Strickland mantra: the Constitution requires only a minimally competent defense, one that survives unless no reasonable lawyer would have provided it, plus a showing that the result would have differed. First identify which result might differ: the verdict, the sentence, or the plea.
note case · week 2

Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984) — the two-part test for ineffective assistance

Facts: At a capital sentencing, counsel skipped a psychiatric examination and character witnesses to avoid opening the door to rebuttal. Washington was sentenced to death and was executed.

Held: A defendant must show all required: (1) deficiency, meaning performance below an objective standard of reasonableness, judged with a strong presumption of competence and "wide latitude" for tactics; and (2) prejudice, meaning a "reasonable probability" of a different result. Counsel here was not deficient.

Why it matters: Most claims fail on prejudice. A Rule violation ordinarily shows deficiency, not prejudice (sleeping or drunk counsel, yet convictions affirmed on strong evidence).

note case · week 2

United States v. Cronic, 466 U.S. 648 (1984) — when prejudice is presumed

Facts: A young real estate lawyer was appointed to defend a complex check-kiting case and given 25 days to prepare.

Held: Not ineffective per se; remanded for a Strickland analysis. Prejudice is presumed only when the circumstances amount to no counsel at all any one suffices: (1) counsel is absent at a critical stage; or (2) counsel fails entirely to subject the prosecution's case to meaningful adversarial testing.

Why it matters: Rare. A hard assignment or an isolated error isn't Cronic. Problem 7C-3 (counsel asleep during openings, absent during his client's cross, conceding guilt in closing, absent at the verdict) adds up to absence at critical stages and no adversarial testing. Missing a sentencing is absence at a critical stage (Danny MC). An appointed lawyer who thinks his client is dangerous, skips the alibi witness, and puts on no case fails adversarial testing and has a personal-interest conflict (Larry MC).

note case · week 2

Roe v. Flores-Ortega, 528 U.S. 470 (2000) — the lost appeal

Facts: Counsel failed to file a notice of appeal.

Held: Ignoring a client's instruction to file a notice of appeal is deficient, and prejudice is presumed if the client would otherwise have appealed. No need to show the appeal would have won.

Why it matters: The appeal decision is the client's (MR 1.2(a)), and losing it entirely is treated like having no lawyer for that stage.

note case · week 2

Missouri v. Frye, 566 U.S. 134; Lafler v. Cooper, 566 U.S. 156 (2012) — ineffective assistance in plea bargaining

Facts: In Frye, counsel never told the client about a formal plea offer before it lapsed. In Lafler, counsel's bad legal advice led the client to reject a favorable offer and go to trial.

Held: The right to effective assistance extends to plea bargaining. Failing to convey a formal offer is deficient (Frye), and prejudice is a reasonable probability that the plea would have been accepted and the result less severe (Lafler).

Why it matters: Identify which result might differ: here, the plea. Conveying plea offers is also an MR 1.4(a)(1) duty.

  • Client choices aren't deficiency: a fully informed defendant who insists on testifying and is impeached has no claim (MR 1.2(a)). Problem 7C-1(b): if Steve advised testifying on an inexplicable guess that the jury would believe a three-time armed robber, the advice is arguably deficient, but she must still show a reasonable probability of acquittal had she stayed off the stand, and the choice was hers.
  • Consequences: the remedy is reversal and a new trial. Most states require actual innocence for criminal malpractice (Coscia v. McKenna & Cuneo; Dombrowski v. Bulson), so Steve's missed appeal deadline (Problem 7C-2(b)) supports damages only if the client was innocent and would more likely than not have been acquitted. Once accused of ineffectiveness, defense counsel's interests shift toward the prosecutor's, which creates a conflict.

9. Forming the Relationship and Identifying the Client

week 2

Ch. 9.A–B. Who is your client, and what do you owe people who aren't? The Bobby Boilermaker problem: Charlie Comp's letter said "worker compensation matter," Bobby reasonably understood "all my compensation," and his products-liability claim expired. Scope is judged from the client's point of view; forming a relationship instantly imposes all four duties (loyalty, care, confidentiality, candor), and the lawyer bears the risk of uncertainty.

Declining

  • You may decline for almost any reason (e.g., disliking an abrasive client), absent a court appointment or an unlawful reason such as invidious discrimination.
  • Intake red flags (intake mistakes are among the most common): unfamiliar law; revenge or "making a point"; unmanageable expectations; serial lawyer-changers; rudeness to staff; "I don't care what it costs"; Internet experts; shifting stories.
  • Court appointments: MR 6.2 allows seeking to avoid an appointment only for good cause (Part X). Distaste alone isn't enough.
  • Must decline under MR 1.16(a) (below), including the 2023 duty to inquire into and assess the facts of each representation.
rule

Forming the relationship — Restatement § 14

A client-lawyer relationship arises when any one suffices: (1) a person manifests to a lawyer the person's intent that the lawyer provide legal services, and either (a) the lawyer manifests consent, or (b) the lawyer fails to manifest lack of consent and knows or reasonably should know that the person reasonably relies on the lawyer to provide the services; or (2) a tribunal with power to do so appoints the lawyer.

  • Objective test with a subjective element: the client must actually and reasonably believe a relationship exists. Silence can be consent ("please handle my eviction," no reply, the hearing approaching).
  • Not required: fees, a conflict check, or an engagement letter, unless you told the person there's no relationship until those are done. "Use your words."
  • Status is matter by matter. One person can be a current, former, and prospective client at once (last year's PI client who now asks about estate planning is a former and a prospective client).
  • Appointment creates the relationship without any signed agreement: Abed, appointed to defend Troy, can't skip the arraignment because Troy hasn't signed a retainer (slides). Logan MC: Logan took the facts, never sent the retainer or ran conflicts, and Connor did nothing further; there's a relationship (and malpractice exposure) if an objective observer would find Connor reasonably relied on Logan.
  • Accidental client at a party (Problems 9A-1, 8A-5). Risk rises at each step: small talk (none); he describes his divorce (a possible prospective client, with confidentiality); he asks whether his lawyer is doing a good job (a request for advice); Maya comments on each piece of advice (legal advice he may rely on: Togstad care, maybe a relationship under § 14); handing over her card when he wants to switch lawyers suggests future representation. Fix: "I can't advise you, and I'm not a family lawyer. Please raise this with your lawyer or get a formal second opinion."
  • Open-ended engagements (occasional consulting) are never "completed." Recent contact, bills, and no disengagement letter mean a current client, so a new adverse matter needs consent (MR 1.7).

Prospective clients: MR 1.18

  • Who qualifies (MR 1.18(a), cmt [2]): a person who consults a lawyer about the possibility of forming a relationship, with an actual and reasonable belief that the talk was about possible representation. Likely yes: the lawyer specifically invites information without clear warnings. Likely no: unilateral communications prompted by general advertising, and "cookie-licking" (consulting every lawyer in town to conflict them out), which fails the subjective test (ABA Op. 492). Don't infer bad faith just because someone interviewed several firms.
  • MR 1.18(b): even when no relationship follows, you may not use or reveal information learned from the prospective client (only that information, not what you find independently), except as MR 1.9 would permit for a former client. Cmt [1]: prospective clients get "some but not all" of a client's protection.
  • Shola MC (slides): a prospective client who decides not to hire you is still owed confidentiality for what she told you; threatening to publicize it violates MR 1.18(b).
  • Avoid accidental prospective clients: "I'm not a family lawyer." Invite a real consultation only after a conflict check, and limit intake to what you need to decide (that limit is what lets the firm screen under MR 1.18(d): Part VI).

Organizational clients and unrepresented persons

  • MR 1.13(a): the lawyer for an organization represents the entity, acting through its constituents, who aren't automatically clients. MR 1.13(f): when the entity's interests are adverse to a constituent's, explain who the client is (an employee says "glad you're my lawyer too"; a founder asks whether the equity split is fair to him). Full treatment in Part IX.
  • MR 4.3 unrepresented persons all required:
    (1) don't state or imply you're disinterested;
    (2) correct a misunderstanding about your role that you know or reasonably should know of; and
    (3) if their interests conflict or may conflict with your client's, give no legal advice except to get a lawyer. Negotiating and explaining your client's position are fine (Part XI).
  • Problem 9A-2 (three founders): the VC (a firm client) expects the firm to represent him, but Maya drafts papers reflecting all three founders' deal and forms the corporation. Who is the client depends on what each founder reasonably believed. If the firm represents only the VC, Maya must make clear to the inventor and the sales lead that she isn't their lawyer and give them no advice (MR 4.3). Representing all three, or the new company too, requires MR 1.7 analysis and consent; once formed, the corporation is a distinct client (MR 1.13(a)). Identify the client before the meeting.

10. Scope of the Engagement and Duties to Non-Clients

week 2

Ch. 9.B–C.

Duty

Relation to scope

Care

Limited to scope, but covers everything within scope, asked for or not (e.g., deadlines).

Candor

Limited to scope.

Loyalty

Broader: no adversity to a current client in any matter.

Confidentiality

Broader: anything obtained in connection with the representation, and it survives the engagement.

rule

MR 1.2(c) — limiting scope

A lawyer may limit the scope of the representation if all required: (1) the limitation is reasonable under the circumstances; and (2) the client gives informed consent.

  • No writing is required, but use one. Unbundling (drafting papers the client files and argues, trial-only work, ghostwriting) is generally allowed. Cmt [6]: limits may track insurance coverage, the client's objectives, or tactics the lawyer finds repugnant or imprudent.
  • A label isn't informed consent. "Workers' comp only" without explaining the risks and alternatives isn't informed consent (Nichols v. Keller). Scope is what the client actually and reasonably believes, judged from the client's point of view, knowledge, and experience.
  • Lani MC (slides): a $15K flat fee "to represent you in a slip and fall case" reasonably covers the case through final judgment; the lawyer should have excluded appeals in writing. A written engagement letter's main benefit is clarifying scope; it doesn't bar malpractice claims or discipline.
  • Elias MC: the client signed a "breach of contract only" agreement, but Elias never mentioned the treble-damages fraud claim, so her consent wasn't informed and the limit is ineffective. Problem 9C-1: "trial court only, no appeals or enforcement" and "draft the complaint only (ghostwriting)" are both reasonable limits if the client understands what's excluded, the deadlines (e.g., the notice-of-appeal clock), and what to do next.
  • Three scope traps to address in every engagement letter:
    (1) post-trial work (appeals and enforcement);
    (2) tax; and
    (3) insurance coverage. Charlie Comp's fix, in plain terms: "Comp only. A separate suit may be worth more. The deadline is running. See a litigator now."
note case · week 2

Nichols v. Keller, 15 Cal. App. 4th 1672 (1993) — limiting scope without explaining what's left out

Facts: A workers' compensation lawyer handled only the comp claim and never told the injured client he might also have a third-party suit, which then became time-barred.

Held: The lawyer could be liable: even when the engagement is limited, a lawyer must alert the client to reasonably apparent related legal problems outside the scope.

Why it matters: MR 1.2(c) requires informed consent to a limit, and MR 1.0(e) requires explaining material risks and alternatives. The Charlie Comp problem.

rule

MR 1.8(h) — limiting malpractice liability

A lawyer shall not (1) make an agreement prospectively limiting the lawyer's malpractice liability unless the client is independently represented in making the agreement; or (2) settle a claim or potential claim for such liability with an unrepresented client or former client unless that person is advised in writing of the desirability of seeking, and given a reasonable opportunity to seek, independent counsel.

  • Written advice to get a lawyer isn't enough under (h)(1); the client must actually be represented. Timothy's standard "waive all future malpractice claims or I won't take you" clause is valid only if the client is independently represented in agreeing to it (slides). States disfavor these waivers substantively too.

Duties to non-clients

You can assume one duty without assuming them all. The most common are confidentiality and care.

Duty

How you assume it

Confidentiality

(1) A prospective-client consultation (MR 1.18); (2) a promise, e.g., anonymity to a witness (it can bind you and your client and conflict with candor, so check with the client first); (3) a client's confidentiality obligations to others, e.g., insurers. Disqualification can follow even without a client relationship (Westinghouse v. Kerr-McGee, Part VI).

Care

(1) Advice given while declining (Togstad); (2) intended beneficiaries (Restatement § 51(3); Lucas v. Hamm); (3) work that third parties are invited to rely on, e.g., opinion letters to a lender (MR 2.3; Restatement § 51(2)).

note case · week 2

Togstad v. Vesely, Otto, Miller & Keefe, 291 N.W.2d 686 (Minn. 1980) — liability for declining carelessly

Facts: Mrs. Togstad consulted a lawyer about a possible medical malpractice claim over her husband's paralysis. He told her "I don't think you have a case," charged nothing, and said nothing about the limitations period. She relied on him, consulted no one else, and the claim expired.

Held: A jury could find an attorney-client relationship (or negligent advice she relied on), and the verdict for the Togstads exceeded $600,000.

Why it matters: The duty came from how he declined. The Togstad-safe non-engagement letter all required:
(1) say you won't provide services;
(2) give no advice or evaluation of the claim;
(3) encourage them to seek other counsel promptly;
(4) warn that limitations periods and deadlines apply; and
(5) put it in writing.

note case · week 2

Lucas v. Hamm, 56 Cal. 2d 583 (1961) — duty to intended beneficiaries

Facts: A lawyer drafted a will whose trust provision violated the rule against perpetuities, and the intended beneficiaries lost their share.

Held: Intended beneficiaries of a will may sue the drafting lawyer for negligence despite the lack of privity. But the court found no negligence, because the rule against perpetuities was so confusing that a reasonable lawyer could err; no other jurisdiction follows that part.

Why it matters: Care can run to non-clients the client intended to benefit (Restatement § 51(3)). Problem 9B-3: the unborn descendant is an intended beneficiary; "Ted was my only client" isn't a defense, and the engagement letter doesn't bind the non-client.

  • Limits on third-party reliance: liability usually runs only to those specifically to or for whom the statement was made (Bily v. Arthur Young & Co.), and reliance must be reasonable (Goodman v. Kennedy).
  • Slide applications. 9B-1 (Wendy Waitress): Frank was right to clear the anonymity promise with the client first, because it binds the client too; they can still report the busboy's theft to the police but can't identify Wendy as the source. 9B-2 (doctor conflict): suing a current client is a MR 1.7(a)(1) conflict; if the firm won't seek the doctor's consent, it must decline, Togstad-safe (no view of the merits, flag the limitations period, recommend other counsel), and should just say "a conflict," because the doctor's identity as a client is confidential. 9B-4 (nephew's defense): the nephew is the client and Ted is a third-party payor (MR 1.8(f)); the opioid information is the nephew's confidence, so Frank and Willie can't tell Ted without the nephew's informed consent.
  • Hareem MC: a lawyer who declines and suggests another lawyer may have exercised reasonable care; the risk lies in not flagging the FTCA administrative-claim deadline. The answer is "maybe," not "no duty to a non-client." Duty-of-care MCs: a QDRO drafted for Keyan: duty to Keyan only, because an adversary in the divorce isn't an intended beneficiary. Owen's exposure assessment that the client said would go to the buyer: duty to both the hospital and the purchaser. A negligent opinion letter relied on by its addressee: professional negligence.
rule

Third-party payors — MR 1.8(f) and 5.4(c)

A lawyer may accept compensation from someone other than the client only if all required:
(1) the client gives informed consent;
(2) there is no interference with the lawyer's independence of professional judgment or with the client-lawyer relationship; and
(3) information relating to the representation is protected as MR 1.6 requires. MR 5.4(c): whoever recommends, employs, or pays the lawyer may not direct or regulate the lawyer's professional judgment (an employer can't veto witness interviews).

  • The payor relationship is arm's-length: no privilege with the payor (sharing may waive it) and no disclosure to the payor without the client's consent.

11. Clients with Decision-Making Limitations (MR 1.14)

week 2

The casebook doesn't treat MR 1.14; this section follows the Rule and its Comments as amended (the Rule now speaks of "decision-making limitations," not "diminished capacity").

rule

MR 1.14

(a) As far as reasonably possible, maintain an ordinary client-lawyer relationship with a client with decision-making limitations. A person has decision-making limitations if the person has substantial difficulty receiving and understanding information, evaluating information, or making or communicating decisions, even with appropriate supports or accommodations.

(b) When the lawyer reasonably believes that the client all required:
(1) has decision-making limitations;
(2) is at risk of substantial physical, financial, or other harm unless action is taken; and
(3) cannot adequately act in the client's own interest to address the risk, the lawyer may take reasonably necessary protective action to address the risk.

(c) Information relating to the representation is protected by MR 1.6, but when taking protective action under (b), the lawyer may reveal information to the extent reasonably necessary to protect the client's interests.

  • An ordinary relationship comes first (cmts [1]–[4]): limitations can be situational and vary by issue and over time. The client still decides objectives, is kept informed, and is treated with attention and respect. Try supports and accommodations first.
  • Family members and agents (cmts [5]–[7]): get the client's informed consent before others sit in (MR 1.6(a)). When a relative brings the client, identify who the client is. Even with an agent under a power of attorney, take direction from the client to the extent feasible.
  • Assessing limitations (cmt [13], weighed together):
    (1) the client's ability to articulate reasoning leading to a decision;
    (2) variability of state of mind and ability to understand consequences;
    (3) appreciation of the substantive fairness of a decision;
    (4) consistency of the decision with the client's known long-term commitments and values; and
    (5) whether supports or accommodations could alleviate the limitations. Focus on functional abilities rather than a diagnosis alone; no court or doctor needs to have made a finding (cmt [14]).
  • Protective action (cmts [12], [15]–[17]): start with the least intrusive measures, such as consulting family members, a reconsideration period, voluntary tools like a durable power of attorney, or support groups, healthcare professionals, or adult-protective agencies; consider a guardian or conservator only when necessary. Be guided by the client's wishes and values, best interests, and the goal of minimizing intrusion on autonomy. You may not advocate for a guardianship or conservatorship the client opposes, and as the respondent's lawyer you must advocate the client's objectives (cmt [16]). You must tell the client of protective action you take; it doesn't by itself require withdrawal, but check MR 1.16(a) and 1.7 (cmt [17]).
  • Disclosure is risky (cmt [18]): raising capacity can trigger involuntary civil commitment, so reveal only what's reasonably necessary, and first ask whether the person you consult is likely to act adversely to the client.
  • Criminal clients and minors (cmts [8]–[9]): competence may require seeking a competency evaluation; a minor's lawyer maintains an ordinary relationship as far as reasonably possible. A guardian ad litem advocates best interest, which may diverge from the person's objectives (cmt [10]).
  • Emergencies (cmts [19]–[20]): when a person's health, safety, or finances face imminent and irreparable harm, a lawyer may act even though no relationship can be formed, keeping the person's confidences as if dealing with a client.

12. Declining and Terminating the Representation (MR 1.16)

week 2

Ch. 9.D. Getting out properly, and what you still owe on the way out.

  • Ending the relationship. It ends by completion on its terms or by termination; open-ended engagements never complete. Send a disengagement letter: it confirms the end (and can start the tolled malpractice limitations period), lists tasks you won't handle, and addresses the file (MR 1.3 cmt [4]). Ending an open-ended relationship turns a current client into a former one, which helps with conflicts (Problem 9D-1, Leo Landlord: billed twice within nine months and never disengaged, so still a current client). rule

MR 1.16(a) — must decline or withdraw

A lawyer shall inquire into and assess the facts and circumstances of each representation to determine whether to accept or continue it (2023). Except as stated in (c), a lawyer shall not represent a client, or shall withdraw, if any one suffices:
(1) the representation will result in violation of the Rules or other law (e.g., a nonconsentable conflict);
(2) the lawyer's physical or mental condition materially impairs the lawyer's ability to represent the client;
(3) the lawyer is discharged; or
(4) the client or prospective client seeks to use or persists in using the lawyer's services to commit or further a crime or fraud, despite the lawyer's MR 1.2(d) and 1.4(a)(5) discussion of the limits on the lawyer's help (2023).

  • The 2023 duty to inquire: ABA Op. 513 calls for a risk-based, proportional inquiry at intake, repeated when circumstances change. The "must withdraw" threshold sits somewhere between naked suspicion and actual knowledge. No state had adopted these amendments as of the casebook, but the MPRE tests them.
rule

MR 1.16(b) — may withdraw

Except as stated in (c), a lawyer may withdraw if any one suffices:
(1) withdrawal can be accomplished without material adverse effect on the client's interests (the ordinary cost of switching doesn't count; a looming deadline does);
(2) the client persists in a course of action involving the lawyer's services that the lawyer reasonably believes is criminal or fraudulent;
(3) the client has used the lawyer's services to perpetrate a crime or fraud;
(4) the client insists on action the lawyer considers repugnant or fundamentally disagrees with (in a firm, ask to be reassigned);
(5) the client fails substantially to fulfill an obligation to the lawyer (e.g., payment) after reasonable warning that the lawyer will withdraw (so build a long runway);
(6) the representation will result in an unreasonable financial burden on the lawyer or has been rendered unreasonably difficult by the client; or
(7) other good cause exists.

Leave of the tribunal: MR 1.16(c)

rule

MR 1.16(c)

"A lawyer must comply with applicable law requiring notice to or permission of a tribunal when terminating a representation. When ordered to do so by a tribunal, a lawyer shall continue representation notwithstanding good cause for terminating the representation."

  • Why courts keep you in: to protect the calendar and prevent opportunism (firing lawyer after lawyer on the eve of trial). Until relieved, every duty continues, even if the client is at fault. Leave is most often denied close to trial or when the motion looks opportunistic.
  • Two routes out: substitution of counsel (almost always approved; look before you leap if you're the one coming in), or a motion to withdraw. Your reasons are confidential, so say only that "professional considerations require termination" (cmt [3]). If ordered to say more, MR 1.6(b)(6) permits it; ask for sealing or in camera review.
  • Nonpayment disclosures: ABA Op. 476 (2016) suggests you may disclose the fact of nonpayment under MR 1.6(b)(5) when withdrawing, but the case law is mixed, so avoid it unless the court presses. "Your lack of planning is not my emergency": courts deny last-minute motions.
  • Never threaten to skip trial to get paid. That's abandonment and violates MR 1.16(c), 1.1, and 1.3, with sanctions and liability to follow. Warn the client and move to withdraw instead.

Exit duties: MR 1.16(d)

rule

MR 1.16(d)

On termination, take steps to the extent reasonably practicable to protect the client's interests, such as (all that apply):
(1) giving reasonable notice;
(2) allowing time to employ other counsel;
(3) surrendering papers and property to which the client is entitled; and

What information is covered

  • Incredibly broad: information from any source (witnesses, documents, your own research); the client's identity and the fact of representation; even public records. Filings at the clerk's office aren't "generally known," so you can't confirm them to a reporter. Say "I can't discuss client matters," and don't lie.
  • Limits on what counts at all (limits, not exceptions):

    (2) "relating to" means "obtained in connection with": what Ron learned from Petrified is Petrified's, even though it's relevant to Mojo, and loyalty to one client never licenses breaching duties to another; and

trap

Use vs. reveal for former clients

MR 1.9(c)(1) lets you use generally known information to a former client's disadvantage. MR 1.9(c)(2) still bars revealing it except as the Rules permit. "Generally known" isn't a general disclosure license (some states, e.g., New York, exclude generally known information from the duty entirely).

What the duty requires

  • No voluntary disclosure, except to the client or its agent, or to people in your firm who need to know. "Voluntary" is the key word: confidentiality yields to legal compulsion (subpoenas, discovery), but privilege and work product must be asserted in good faith. Appeal where warranted; you need not defy a final order.
  • No use to the client's disadvantage (MR 1.8(b), 1.9(c)(1), 1.18(b)). Wrongful profit that doesn't hurt the client (insider trading on a deal) isn't a confidentiality-Rule violation, but brings disgorgement and criminal liability. General knowledge, research, and forms may be reused (MR 1.8 cmt [5]); client-specific facts may not.
  • Reasonable safeguards (MR 1.6(c)): reasonable efforts to prevent inadvertent or unauthorized disclosure of, or access to, client information. Cmt [18] factors weighed together:
    (1) the sensitivity of the information;
    (2) the likelihood of disclosure without more safeguards;
    (3) the cost of more safeguards;
    (4) the difficulty of implementing them; and
    (5) the extent to which they would hurt the lawyer's ability to represent clients. Reasonableness, not perfection: being mugged while carrying a locked briefcase isn't a violation. Cmt [19] (transmission): reasonable precautions against unintended recipients; special security isn't required if the method carries a reasonable expectation of privacy, but sensitive matters may warrant more.
  • In practice: no talk in elevators, lobbies, rideshares, or restaurants; no valet with files in the car; lock up market-moving material; a screen guard where others can see your laptop; electronic safeguards scaled to sensitivity (Op. 477R).

15. Exceptions to Confidentiality (MR 1.6(b))

weeks 3–4

Framing principles

  • Break information into its smallest units. Check whether each unit is confidential at all before hunting for an exception. "Not confidential" means only "may," not "should."
  • Every MR 1.6(b) exception is permissive, covers disclosure only (not use), and applies only "to the extent the lawyer reasonably believes necessary" all required:
    (1) the minimum information;
    (2) to the minimum people;
    (3) for the specific purpose. Exceeding any of the three is a violation. "Reasonably believes" is a subjective belief that is also objectively reasonable.
  • Consent and implied authorization. Express informed consent needs no particular form (confirm significant consents in writing). Implied authorization covers need-to-know sharing with the firm team and facts in a filed complaint (cmt [5]), but it doesn't override an express client restriction. Problem 8B-1: briefing a firm colleague helping review documents is implied; revealing where the bodies are, after explaining the goodwill and the incriminating effect and getting the client's agreement, is informed consent; previewing the client's anticipated testimony in an opening statement is implied (though it's wise to walk the client through it).
  • Exceptions also live outside MR 1.6: MR 1.13(c), 1.14(c), 3.3(c), and 4.1(b). States vary on MR 1.6(b) more than on any other provision.
rule

MR 1.6(b) — a lawyer may reveal information to the extent the lawyer reasonably believes necessary any one suffices:

(1) to prevent reasonably certain death or substantial bodily harm;

(2) to prevent the client from committing a crime or fraud reasonably certain to cause substantial injury to another's financial interests or property, in furtherance of which the client has used or is using the lawyer's services;

(3) to prevent, mitigate, or rectify substantial financial injury that is reasonably certain to result or has resulted from the client's commission of such a crime or fraud, in furtherance of which the client has used the lawyer's services;

(4) to secure legal advice about the lawyer's compliance with the Rules;

(5) to establish a claim or defense for the lawyer in a controversy with the client; to defend against a criminal charge or civil claim against the lawyer based on conduct involving the client; or to respond to allegations in any proceeding concerning the lawyer's representation of the client;

(6) to comply with other law or a court order; or

(7) to detect and resolve conflicts arising from the lawyer's change of employment or changes in a firm's composition or ownership, but only if the disclosure would not compromise privilege or otherwise prejudice the client.

§

How each exception works

(b)(1)

"Reasonably certain" means imminent, or a present and substantial threat (cmt [6]). Not limited to client crimes (e.g., a victim who will die without aid). Venting ("I'll wring her neck") doesn't qualify; a gun plus a threat does. Try to dissuade the client first if that's safe. No Model Rule duty to warn, though some states impose one. It doesn't apply once the harm is complete. 8B-2: "I choked her... she wasn't moving" lets you call 911, since aid may still prevent death; a visibly drunk client about to drive 45 minutes after you tried to dissuade him lets you call the troopers; for lead in a city's water causing neurological harm to children, (b)(1) can apply, but a newspaper reporter isn't the minimum audience. In-house counsel should report up (MR 1.13(b)), then out to health regulators (MR 1.13(c)) to the extent necessary.

(b)(2)

The client has used or is using the lawyer's services in furtherance; the lawyer needn't have knowingly assisted.

(b)(3)

Reaches completed frauds, but still requires use of your services. Pre-retention crimes, or crimes committed without your services, never qualify (an old embezzlement), and defending the past crime isn't "use."

(b)(4)

To keep it privileged, retain the adviser as your counsel. You must be the client, asking on your own behalf. Hallway chats, a retired aunt, or an uninvolved partner likely aren't privileged; a no-names hypothetical avoids disclosure altogether. 8B-4 (Gina Grayhair): a retired, inactive-status aunt likely isn't privileged; an office suite-mate with her own practice probably isn't either; a short email confirming she'll act as your lawyer for this limited purpose (paying her rate helps) makes it privileged; the senior partner on the case is part of the team (implied authorization); for the uninvolved litigation chair, disclosure within the firm is permitted, but privilege depends on the state's firm-counsel rule.

(b)(5)

Minimum relevant information only (the negotiation file yes; an irrelevant ten-year-old fraud judgment no). Cmt [10]: you may respond to an assertion of complicity before any proceeding is filed. Not for online reviews (ABA Op. 496): give a neutral, non-disclosing reply. 8B-5 (Bonnie Badmouth): in fee arbitration, emails showing she approved the tasks are OK; trust records and emails sent to the bar's disciplinary inquiry are OK; answering Dan's fraud accusation with Bonnie's documents that formed the basis for your statements is OK, but her ten-year-old fraud judgment is not; telling Carla at church about Bonnie's dementia is not OK (no proceeding, gratuitous); "I can't discuss Bonnie's affairs, but what she said isn't true" is risky, since even a denial reveals something; "I can't discuss client matters" is the safe line.

(b)(6)

Assert privilege, seek review, then comply with a binding order; seek sealing or in camera review. "Other law" includes discovery (FRCP 26–37), mandatory disclosures, and subpoenas. 8B-6: assets aren't privileged communications, so labeling secret offshore money in your trust account "privileged" on a court-mandated asset form was improper; once ordered, you must comply even over the client's objection (MR 1.6(b)(6), 3.3, 3.4(c)) and consider withdrawal. Filing the trade-secret description after seeking (but not getting) a stay is proper.

(b)(7)

Limited information only (identities and the general nature of matters). Not if it would compromise privilege or prejudice the client, e.g., an unannounced takeover (cmt [13]).

16. When Disclosure Becomes Mandatory; Hard Cases

weeks 3–4
  • MR 4.1(b): you must disclose a material fact when necessary to avoid assisting a client's crime or fraud, unless MR 1.6 prohibits it. This turns a MR 1.6(b)(2)–(3) permission into a duty (Part XI). See noisy withdrawal (Part II).
  • MR 1.13(b) report up: an organization's lawyer who knows of a constituent's violation likely to cause the organization substantial injury must act in its best interest, ordinarily reporting to higher authority up to the board. Unlike MR 1.6(b)(2), this doesn't require use of the lawyer's services; MR 1.13(d) excludes lawyers hired to investigate or defend the violation (Part IX).

  • MR 8.1 and 8.3 don't require revealing confidences.
  • Honesty and confidentiality together: when asked about a confidential plan ("Will you move to dismiss?"), decline to answer, and don't lie (MR 4.1(a), 8.4(c); In re Pressly). "I can't discuss that" satisfies both duties.

Part IV — Agency and Fiduciary Duty

17. The Lawyer as Fiduciary (Ch. 5)

week 4

Reading: MR 1.4; Chs. 5–6. "Not honesty alone, but the punctilio of an honor the most sensitive" (Meinhard v. Salmon).

Becoming a fiduciary

  • By status: lawyers (always, to clients, everywhere), agents, trustees, guardians, directors, general partners, doctors, clergy.
  • By circumstance: someone reposes special trust and the fiduciary accepts it (a neighbor given signature authority over an elderly friend's accounts; some states call this a "confidential relationship," sometimes treated as less than a full fiduciary relationship). It arises when the beneficiary offers, and the fiduciary accepts, trust because of any one suffices:
    (1) the special nature of the relationship;
    (2) the fiduciary's special skills; or
    (3) the beneficiary's entrusting of secret or confidential information.
  • Scope is limited by beneficiary (identified persons) and subject matter (the engagement).
  • Self-dealing claims: in many states the burden shifts to the fiduciary to prove fairness, and enhanced remedies (disgorgement, punitive damages) are available. Expect a higher standard even when you "weren't acting as a lawyer."

Meinhard v. Salmon, 249 N.Y. 458 (1928) — the punctilio of an honor

Facts: Salmon managed a hotel lease in a joint venture with Meinhard. Near the end of the lease, the landlord offered Salmon alone a much larger redevelopment deal, which he took without telling Meinhard.

18. Candor to the Client (MR 1.4)

week 4
rule

MR 1.4 — communication

(a) A lawyer shall all required:
(1) promptly inform the client of any decision or circumstance requiring the client's informed consent (so settlement offers and plea offers must be conveyed unless the client has already said how to handle them);
(2) reasonably consult with the client about the means of pursuing the client's objectives;
(3) keep the client reasonably informed about the status of the matter;
(4) promptly comply with reasonable requests for information; and
(5) consult with the client about any relevant limitation on the lawyer's conduct when the lawyer knows the client expects help the Rules or other law don't permit (e.g., backdating a document).

(b) Explain a matter to the extent reasonably necessary to permit the client to make informed decisions.

  • Materiality: what this client, or a reasonable client, would find significant to the engagement. Candor is limited to scope: private facts unrelated to the work (a lawyer's marriage, sexual orientation) aren't material. The client's reaction is a business risk, not a candor duty.
  • Your own mistakes (ABA Op. 18-481): disclose if material, meaning "reasonably likely to harm or prejudice a client" or of a nature that "would reasonably cause a client to consider terminating the representation even in the absence of harm." Disclose promptly, before any harm occurs. You may consult firm counsel and the insurer first, but don't let that delay the disclosure.
  • The don't-wanna test: the more you don't want to disclose it, the more likely it's material.
  • Delaying bad news (cmt [7]): you may delay transmitting information when the client is likely to react imprudently to immediate disclosure (e.g., a psychiatric diagnosis). You may never withhold to serve your own or another's interest. That's the frame for Problem 8A-8(e), Frank's worry that news of the wife's affair would devastate the husband; the affair is also irrelevant to the divorce, so it may not be within the scope of candor at all.
  • Disclose more than the minimum: "we do the minimum the law requires" isn't a slogan for your website. Agree early on what this client wants to hear and how often; use ticklers for periodic updates even when little is happening; consider "N/C" (no charge) for short status messages.
  • Consequences of silence: discipline, damages for avoidable losses, and a "cover-up" theory that turns an innocent mistake into punitive damages. "Conversations that begin 'Why didn't you tell me...' rarely end well."
  • Problem 5D-5 (deBilder's missing acceleration clause): an errant keystroke deleted the clause from a $2.4M installment note. That's material (reasonably likely to harm the client if the buyer defaults), so it must be disclosed promptly, however crusty the client. First tell your boss, who may need to notify firm management and the malpractice carrier; then tell the client, with a proposed fix (e.g., negotiating an amendment) and no charge for the fix.
  • Problem 7B-1 (Dan Distraught): candor includes realistic expectations. Explain, with compassion, that a civil lawyer can't put the boy in prison or on a sex-offender registry (the prosecutor's call), and what a civil claim can do. "Make it right" with friends from work calls for a MR 1.2(d)/1.4(a)(5) conversation; if a threat becomes reasonably certain bodily harm, MR 1.6(b)(1) is available.
  • MR 2.1 (advisor): exercise independent judgment and give candid advice; you may refer to moral, economic, social, and political factors, not just law. The client still decides objectives (Part VIII).

19. The Lawyer as Agent: Authority (Ch. 6; MR 1.2(a))

week 4

Fiduciary law governs lawyer and client. Agency adds the lawyer's power to change the client's legal relations with third parties.

  • Formation: the principal manifests assent that the agent act on the principal's behalf and subject to its control, and the agent consents (Restatement (Third) of Agency § 1.01). If you're someone's lawyer, you're their agent: authorized contracts bind the client, and for the agent's torts both principal and agent can be liable.
  • The Homeowners: "Honest Abe" did nothing, and they lost the foreclosure despite winning defenses. Careless decisions about means bind the client, so there's no automatic do-over; the remedy is against the lawyer. Otherwise third parties couldn't rely on agents, and debtors would hire lawyers to fail on purpose.
  • The book's three questions all required:
    (1) What kind of authority (actual, implied, apparent, ratification), from what facts?
    (2) What is its scope?
    (3) If exceeded, what are the consequences, and who bears them?

The four sources of authority

Source

Test

Key points

Actual (Agency § 2.01)

The agent reasonably believes, "in accordance with the principal's manifestations to the agent," that the principal wants it done.

"Not less than $25K" authorizes $30K, and buyer's remorse doesn't undo the deal. An uncommunicated change of mind doesn't revoke authority; a communicated revocation (a voicemail heard before acting) ends it.

Implied (§ 2.01 cmt b; MR 1.2(a))

What is "necessary, usual, and proper" to accomplish an express grant.

A form of actual authority, and the source of control over means (filing, scheduling extensions, recording the deed).

Apparent (§ 2.03)

A third party reasonably believes the actor has authority, and the belief is traceable to the principal's manifestations.

Hiring a lawyer alone creates no apparent authority to settle. The lawyer's own claims never count ("I'm her lawyer, aren't I?"). The client telling opposing counsel "whatever my lawyer agrees to is fine" does create it, even against a private floor.

Ratification (§§ 4.01, 4.05, 4.07)

Affirmance after the fact, by words or by conduct (keeping the benefits, e.g., depositing the check).

You must ratify the whole act (§ 4.07) and before the third party withdraws (§ 4.05). Never plan on it: you've already violated MR 1.2(a), and ratification doesn't erase the ethics violation.

  • Equal dignities (Agency § 3.02): if the law requires a signed writing for the act (e.g., certain land conveyances), oral authority to sign as agent isn't enough. Don't extend this to ordinary instructions.
  • The 13 words in § 2.01 (slides): "in accordance with the principal's manifestations to the agent." Problem 6A-2(c): Payne's change of heart, told only to her spouse and minister, doesn't revoke Frank's authority, so the $25K deal binds her. 6A-2(d): her call to Frank the night before does revoke it. 6A-2(a): Frank's firm earning more, for less work, on a quick contingent settlement creates no authority to accept against her instructions, and that incentive is itself a personal-interest pressure.
  • The 3Cs for ambiguous authority ("I guess you could take $150K. Maybe."):
    (1) check with the client;
    (2) clarify the implications (finality, no day in court, the net after fees); and
    (3) confirm in writing, and consider a signature, to avoid a swearing contest.

Exceeding authority: consequences

  • Discipline under MR 1.2(a) (failing to abide by the client's decision). A false claim of authority also violates MR 4.1(a) and 8.4(c).
  • Undoing the act: rescission, quiet title, reinstatement (FRCP 60), usually after litigating authority.
  • Liability to the client: an unreasonable belief in authority breaches care, and the client recovers the cost of cleanup.
  • Liability to the opponent: signing implies a warranty of authority; claiming authority you know you lack is fraud, and the firm is liable by respondeat superior. Misrepresentation (tort) and breach of warranty of authority (contract) carry different damages measures.
  • What the opponent does next: walk away (which doesn't excuse your ethics violation), or try to enforce the deal, often by motion in the pending case (Kokkonen v. Guardian Life). Either way, expect your client to sue you, fire you, and dispute your fees. The relationship can be saved only with informed consent, preferably in writing, and that's unlikely. Opposing counsel may have to report you (MR 8.3).
  • Ratification outcomes (Problem 6A-4):
    (a) she grumbles "have it your way," signs, then fires Frank: ratified and bound;
    (b) she refuses, the defense withdraws, then she wants the deal: too late (§ 4.05);
    (c) she X's out the agreement but deposits the check as a "down payment": conduct ratifies, and she can't take the benefit while rejecting the rest (§ 4.07), so the settlement is likely enforceable.
  • Negotiating safely: the "non-offer offer." "My own view is X. My client wants more, but I'd recommend X if your side would." Report it to the client, who decides.

Objectives vs. means: MR 1.2(a)

rule

MR 1.2(a)

A lawyer "shall abide by a client's decisions concerning the objectives of representation and, as required by Rule 1.4, shall consult with the client as to the means by which they are to be pursued," and may take action impliedly authorized to carry out the representation. The client decides (all reserved to the client):
(1) whether to settle; and, in a criminal case,
(2) the plea to be entered;
(3) whether to waive a jury trial; and
(4) whether to testify.

Client decides (objectives)

Lawyer decides after consulting (means)

Settle or not, and material settlement terms (gag or confidentiality clauses); binding arbitration (it waives trial: Blanton v. Womancare); high-low agreements (they set the outcome); the plea; jury waiver; testifying; whether to appeal. The four listed decisions are examples: anything that is a fundamental right or the end result the client hired you for.

Claims and theories to plead; discovery; which witnesses; the defense theory at trial; nonbinding mediation (but consult); professional courtesies such as a harmless extension ("you needn't be a mad dog"). Consultation is required only for material means. In practice clients have strong input, since they can fire you.

  • No end-runs: an advance blanket grant of settlement authority ("settle for any amount the lawyer considers reasonable") is void, but specific advance authority ("accept anything over $70K") is fine, subject to revocation and changed circumstances (cmt [3]).
  • Fee terms that penalize the client's decision are void or unconscionable: hourly fees that kick in if the client rejects a recommended settlement, or retroactive conversion to hourly. Ordinary deposits for real next steps are fine, but coercive timing is suspect.
  • Appointed criminal counsel may make means decisions over objection and needn't present a baseless theory. The client's right to discharge appointed counsel is limited (MR 1.16 cmt [5]). The client still controls the plea, jury, and testimony.
  • MR 1.2(b): representation, including by appointment, is not an endorsement of the client's views. MR 1.2(d): don't counsel or assist conduct you know is criminal or fraudulent, but you may discuss the legal consequences and help the client determine what the law requires (Part XI).
note case · week 4

Blanton v. Womancare, Inc., 38 Cal. 3d 396 (1985) — binding arbitration is the client's call

Facts: Without the client's consent, and against her instructions, her lawyer stipulated to binding arbitration of her medical malpractice claim.

Held: The stipulation did not bind her: agreeing to binding arbitration surrenders the right to trial, a substantial right that requires the client's authority.

Why it matters: An objectives decision (MR 1.2(a)). A lawyer's authority over procedure doesn't reach choices that waive a client's fundamental rights.

20. Fee Arrangements and Their Formalities (MR 1.5(b), (c))

week 5

Reading: MR 1.5, 1.15; Chs. 20–21; Delaware Rule 1.15. Fee agreements are contracts with special overlays, and trust money is sacred.

  • Fees vs. expenses: fees pay for professional services. Expenses are out-of-pocket costs specific to this matter, never rent or general salaries. In-house charges (copying, research) must reflect actual cost or a reasonable amount agreed in advance; a markup disclosed only on the invoice doesn't count (MR 1.5 cmt [1]).
rule

MR 1.5(b) — communicating the fee

The scope of the representation and the basis or rate of the fee and expenses for which the client will be responsible "shall be communicated to the client, preferably in writing, before or within a reasonable time after commencing the representation, except when the lawyer will charge a regularly represented client on the same basis or rate." Any change in the basis or rate must also be communicated (a long-time client's rate increase too). Best practice: a writing signed by the client (cmt [2]).

rule

MR 1.5(c) — contingent fees

A contingent fee agreement must be in a writing signed by the client and state all required:
(1) the method of determining the fee, including the percentage or percentages that accrue to the lawyer in the event of settlement, trial, or appeal;
(2) the litigation and other expenses to be deducted from the recovery;
(3) whether those expenses are deducted before or after the contingent fee is calculated; and
(4) any expenses for which the client will be liable whether or not the client is the prevailing party. At the end, the lawyer must give a written closing statement stating the outcome, the remittance to the client, and how it was calculated.

  • A signature doesn't cure missing terms, and the lawyer's own email plus the client's "sounds good" isn't a writing signed by the client. Any contingent component of a hybrid fee (a success bonus, reverse contingency, or shares payable on sale) needs the client's signed writing.

Arrangement

What it is and what to cover

Flat

A sum certain for defined tasks. Say when, and which portions, are earned if the engagement ends early (cmt [4]). Rewards efficiency, but risks curtailed effort when the money runs out (MR 1.1, 1.3).

Hourly

Hours times each timekeeper's rate, billed in tenths. Scalable as a matter grows or shrinks, but rewards inefficiency. Losing doesn't erase a reasonable hourly fee. Billing work not done is fraud (mail or wire fraud federally).

Contingent

A percentage of the recovery, in a signed writing. Can pit lawyer against client when a trial would cost the firm more than it gains.

Hybrid

Any mix within MR 1.5's limits. Contingent parts need a signed writing.

Salary

Associates and in-house lawyers draw a salary; owners share fee revenue less overhead and reserves.

  • MR 1.5 cmt [5]: no terms that might induce the lawyer to curtail services or act against the client. Don't cap services at a stated amount when more work is foreseeable unless you explain the situation (otherwise the client must bargain mid-matter). Defining services by the client's ability to pay is fine. Don't run up hourly fees with wasteful procedures.
  • MR 1.5 cmt [4]: you may require advance payment but must return any unearned part (MR 1.16(d)). You may take property as a fee (e.g., an ownership interest), but not a proprietary interest in the litigation's subject (MR 1.8(i)), and it may trigger MR 1.8(a) (Part VI).
tip

Contingent-fee math: costs first vs. fee first

$1M recovery, $100K costs, 30% fee. Costs first (more common, client-favorable): $900K x 30% = $270K fee, so the client nets $630K. Fee first: $300K fee, then $700K minus $100K costs, so the client nets $600K. After a loss, the client owes whatever costs the agreement says are owed regardless of outcome, plus possibly the opponent's taxable costs. "No fee unless we win" can mislead (MR 7.1) if clients still owe costs; MR 1.8(e)(1) lets lawyers make repayment of costs contingent on the outcome.

21. Limits on Fees, Retainers, and Fee Sharing (MR 1.5(a), (d), (e); 5.4; 7.2(b))

week 5
rule

MR 1.5(a) — no unreasonable fee

A lawyer shall not make an agreement for, charge, or collect an unreasonable fee or an unreasonable amount for expenses; the violation is complete on agreeing or charging, even without collecting. Factors (weighed together; none alone decides):
(1) the time and labor required, the novelty and difficulty of the questions, and the skill requisite;
(2) the likelihood, if apparent to the client, that the engagement will preclude other work;
(3) the fee customarily charged in the locality;
(4) the amount involved and the results obtained;
(5) time limitations imposed by the client or the circumstances;
(6) the nature and length of the professional relationship;
(7) the experience, reputation, and ability of the lawyer; and
(8) whether the fee is fixed or contingent. The client's ability to pay is not a factor.

  • Judge fees as of the agreement, accounting for the risk assumed. A quick $150K settlement a week after a 33% agreement doesn't make the fee unreasonable. Rush premiums agreed in advance are fine (factors 2 and 5), and different rates for different work are fine.
  • Statutory caps bind (workers' comp, med-mal, Social Security, probate, the FTCA's 25%). Goldfarb v. Virginia State Bar: bar minimum-fee schedules violated antitrust law, which is different from lawful statutory caps.
rule

MR 1.5(d) — prohibited contingent fees

No contingent fee (any one is barred): (1) in a domestic relations matter, contingent on securing a divorce or on the amount of alimony, support, or a property settlement in lieu thereof; or (2) for representing a defendant in a criminal case. Signatures and reasonableness don't cure either.

  • The book calls both anachronisms, but they're still the law. Civil plaintiff contingencies are fine. Cmt [6]: a contingent fee to collect post-judgment arrears of support, alimony, or other financial orders is allowed. Rationales: the domestic ban avoids discouraging reconciliation (McCarthy v. Santangelo: "The state does not favor divorces"); the criminal ban guards against corrupting justice (Peyton v. Margiotti).

Consequences and recovery

  • Prohibited fee: voidable, a defense to collection, and disgorgement. Lying about services rendered is fraud or theft (mail fraud in federal cases).
  • Formal defect (no signed writing): the agreed fee is unenforceable, leaving quantum meruit at most (usually a lodestar at a reasonable rate, often far below the contract; contingent fees usually eliminated). Some states allow nothing.
  • Discharged lawyers: the client may fire you anytime but owes for services, usually quantum meruit capped at the contract price; for a contingent fee, payable only if the contingency occurs. Fee terms that unduly burden discharge are void.

Don't give anything of value to anyone for recommending your services, except any one suffices:

MR 5.4 — professional independence

(a) No sharing legal fees with a nonlawyer, except any one suffices:
(1) payments over time after a lawyer's death to the lawyer's estate or designated persons;
(2) the purchase price of a deceased, disabled, or disappeared lawyer's practice (MR 1.17);
(3) nonlawyer employees in a compensation or retirement plan, even one based on profit-sharing; or
(4) court-awarded fees shared with a nonprofit that employed, retained, or recommended the lawyer in the matter. (b) No partnership with a nonlawyer if any of its activities are the practice of law. (d) No practicing in a for-profit professional corporation or association if (any one bars it):
(1) a nonlawyer owns any interest (except a lawyer's estate representative briefly);
(2) a nonlawyer is a director or officer; or
(3) a nonlawyer has the right to direct or control a lawyer's professional judgment.

22. Safeguarding Client Property (MR 1.15; Delaware Rule 1.15)

week 5

Ch. 21. "Property" includes money (the client's, unearned fees, or a third party's), documents, and physical evidence. The fiduciary standard is being "more than ordinarily careful." Green money (operating, yours) you can spend; red money (trust, theirs) you must safeguard. A segregation failure is a disciplinary violation by itself, but civil liability usually requires a monetary loss.

  • Three duties all required:
    (1) safeguard;
    (2) segregate; and
    (3) account. "Substantial compliance" is hardly ever enough. The duties are nondelegable: you may use staff but keep full disciplinary and financial responsibility, even for a trusted bookkeeper's unforeseeable theft. "Delegate; don't abdicate. Trust, but verify."
  • Two kinds of accounts: the operating account (the lawyer's money) and the client trust account (everyone else's).
rule

MR 1.15 — safekeeping property


(a) Hold others' property separate from your own. Funds go in a separate account in the state where your office is (elsewhere only with consent). Other property must be identified and safeguarded "with the care required of a professional fiduciary" (cmt [1]); bearer bonds are cash and belong in a safe or safe-deposit box. Keep complete records for five years after the representation ends.
(b) Your own funds may go in trust only to pay bank charges.
(c) Advance fees and expenses go in trust, withdrawn "only as fees are earned or expenses incurred."
(d) Promptly notify anyone with an interest when you receive funds, promptly deliver what they're entitled to, and on request promptly render a full accounting.
(e) Keep disputed property separate until the dispute is resolved, and promptly distribute the undisputed part.

Situation

Rule

Commingling

A violation by itself, even if temporary, quickly fixed, and every dollar is there. No loss is required. An advance deposited in operating "for convenience" is commingling.

Pooled trust account

Several clients' funds together is fine, with separate ledgers. Only mixing your money with theirs is barred.

Your money in trust

Only enough for bank charges. "Top off the tank, don't overfill it." No overdraft cushions.

Fees become earned

Move them promptly to operating. Too early is misappropriation; too late is commingling. Define "earned" in the agreement. "We'll put it back" never cures an early withdrawal.

Mixed check (fee + client money)

Deposit in trust, wait until it clears, then promptly move your share and pay the client. Never deposit it in operating.

Check clearing

Provisional credit can be charged back. The retainer scam: a fake cashier's check, then an urgent "refund" wire funded with other clients' money. Disburse only against good funds (cash, a cleared check, a cashier's check confirmed with the bank).

Paying from trust

Pay a client's expenses from that client's funds only. Never pay your own bills (bar dues) from trust.

Disputed funds (MR 1.15(e))

Hold only the disputed amount and distribute the rest. No self-help, and no holding undisputed money to coerce payment or a release (cmt [3]). Suggest arbitration. A client-vs-third-party dispute (a medical lien) may call for interpleader (cmt [4]).

"Promptly notify" anyone with a colorable claim, not just the undisputed owner. An accounting on request covers the required records, but don't reveal other clients' information in it.

Nominal or short-term pooled funds go to the state IOLTA program, which funds legal services for the poor or a client-protection fund. Large or long-term deposits go in a separate account earning interest for the client.

Client ledgers, journals, and monthly reconciliation. Imperfect records are a violation even if every dollar is present (the "Al Capone problem"). Trust overdrafts are reported to the bar.

Part VI — Conflicts of Interest

23. Conflicts Overview and Personal-Interest Conflicts (MR 1.8, 1.7(a)(2))

week 6

Reading: MR 1.7, 1.8, 1.18; Ch. 22.A–B. Ch. 22 is "several chapters in one." The recurring hypothetical: Ron Rainmaker's firm represents Mojo, and Petrified Petroleum comes calling.

The three kinds of conflicts (CB 557)

  • (1) Personal interest: the lawyer's own interests against a client's (MR 1.8; 1.7(a)(2)).
  • (2) Whom you know (loyalty): ongoing relationships with clients that tempt you to favor one over another, governed by the "concurrent client rule" (MR 1.7(a)).
  • (3) What you know (confidentiality): confidential information you might share with, or use for, another client to the original client's disadvantage, governed by the "successive client" or "former client rule" (MR 1.9, 1.18(c)).
  • Class quiz 22.A Q1: a "client's duty to the lawyer vs. the legal system" is not one of the categories.
note

Two observations to keep throughout Ch. 22

A continuum (CB 559): conflicting interests run from hardly any conflict to zero-sum opposition. Many are mild enough to need nothing beyond decency and common sense.

No harm required (CB 561): a serious conflict breaches the duty by its mere existence and potential to harm. The most common consequence is disqualification, an order to get out and stay out before harm occurs (class quiz 22.A Q2). Conflicts can also bring discipline (no harm needed), fee forfeiture and disgorgement of fees paid during the conflict, and damages for actual harm.

Two kinds of rules (CB 560): bright-line rules that forbid arrangements or require formal steps (most of MR 1.8), and general standards with a fuzzier threshold (MR 1.7(a)(2)'s "significant risk" of "material limitation").

Financial assistance to litigation clients: MR 1.8(e)

rule

MR 1.8(e)

A lawyer shall not provide financial assistance to a client in connection with pending or contemplated litigation, except that a lawyer may any one suffices:
(1) advance court costs and expenses of litigation, repayment of which may be contingent on the outcome;
(2) pay court costs and expenses of litigation for an indigent client; or
(3) when representing an indigent client pro bono (through the lawyer's firm, a nonprofit legal services or public interest organization, or a law school clinical or pro bono program), give modest gifts for food, rent, transportation, medicine, and other basic living expenses, if the lawyer all required: (i) doesn't promise, assure, or imply them before retention or as an inducement to continue; (ii) doesn't seek or accept reimbursement from the client, a relative, or anyone affiliated with the client; and (iii) doesn't publicize or advertise a willingness to give them to prospective clients.

  • The ban reaches almost any assistance: a loan, guarantee, or gift (CB 563).
  • Implicit exceptions (CB 566; any one suffices):
    (1) delaying or discounting your fee, or working pro bono;
    (2) a contingent fee; and
    (3) assistance to non-litigation clients or non-clients (the Rule covers only "pending or contemplated litigation").
  • Class quiz 22.A Q5: lending a litigation client money for living expenses is prohibited, whatever the collateral (a lien on the client's car doesn't help).

Gifts and bequests: MR 1.8(c)

  • A lawyer shall not (either is barred): (1) solicit any substantial gift from a client, including a testamentary gift; or (2) prepare an instrument giving the lawyer or a person related to the lawyer a substantial gift, unless the lawyer or other recipient is related to the client. "Related" means a spouse, child, grandchild, parent, grandparent, or other relative or individual with whom the lawyer or client maintains a close familial relationship (CB 569).
  • Another lawyer must draft it. Accepting a modest unsolicited gift (a $100 bottle of wine) is fine. Class quiz 22.A Q7: drafting a will leaving the lawyer expensive paintings is "maybe": it depends on whether lawyer and client are closely enough related.

Literary and media rights: MR 1.8(d)

  • Before the representation ends, a lawyer shall not make or negotiate an agreement giving the lawyer literary or media rights to a portrayal or account based in substantial part on information relating to the representation (CB 572). Class quiz 22.A Q3: taking all media and literary rights to the litigation violates MR 1.8(d); a discounted fee, a contingent fee, or the client's own bank loan doesn't.

Proprietary interests in the litigation: MR 1.8(i)

  • A lawyer shall not acquire a proprietary interest in the cause of action or subject matter of litigation the lawyer is conducting for a client, except any one suffices: (1) a lien authorized by law to secure the lawyer's fee or expenses; or (2) a reasonable contingent fee in a civil case (CB 572). Example: no ownership stake in the disputed parcel you're litigating over.

Business transactions with clients: MR 1.8(a)

rule

MR 1.8(a)

A lawyer shall not enter into a business transaction with a client or knowingly acquire an ownership, possessory, security, or other pecuniary interest adverse to a client unless all required:
(1) the transaction and terms are fair and reasonable to the client and are fully disclosed and transmitted in writing in a manner that can be reasonably understood by the client;
(2) the client is advised in writing of the desirability of seeking, and is given a reasonable opportunity to seek, the advice of independent legal counsel on the transaction; and
(3) the client gives informed consent, in a writing signed by the client, to the essential terms of the transaction and the lawyer's role in it, including whether the lawyer is representing the client in the transaction.

  • Scope is sweeping (CB 573): any business transaction with a client, or any acquisition of an interest adverse to a client (a lien on the client's property is the classic example). Loans, investing in the client's restaurant, taking stock as a fee.
  • Three recognized exclusions any one suffices:
    (1) an ordinary fee agreement paid in money (MR 1.5 governs instead);
    (2) an attorney's lien or security interest in the litigation claim or its proceeds, which many states exempt (some exempt it only to secure a contingent fee, and require MR 1.8(a) to secure an hourly or flat fee); and
    (3) standard commercial transactions for products or services the client generally markets to others (cmt [1]; class quiz 22.A Q8).
  • Class quiz 22.A Q6: a contractual lien on the client's home (property other than the recovery) to secure the fee is a business transaction under MR 1.8(a) (cmt [16]): allowed only if fair and reasonable, with written advice to seek counsel and informed consent in a signed writing.
  • Fairness alone is never enough. Miss any formal requirement and you face discipline, and the transaction is subject to rescission and restitution, even if it was completely fair (CB 580).
  • Class quiz 22.A Q4: a lawyer who takes the client's business opportunity for herself breaches her fiduciary duty and forfeits her fees (Meinhard v. Salmon, Part IV).

Personal-interest conflicts under MR 1.7(a)(2)

  • The test is predictive (CB 582): a "significant risk" that the representation will be "materially limited" by a personal interest of the lawyer, judged before anything bad happens and even if nothing ever does. Consent cures it if consentable (MR 1.7(b)).
  • Relationships with opposing counsel or parties (ABA Op. 494; CB 582):
    (1) intimate and close family relationships almost always create a conflict;
    (2) friendships may, depending on their nature and strength; and
    (3) acquaintances are unlikely to. Even when no conflict arises, the connection may matter enough to disclose under MR 1.4; err on the side of disclosure and consent (CB 583).
  • Lawyers related by blood or marriage on opposite sides (MR 1.7 cmt [11]): each needs the client's informed consent, and the conflict isn't imputed to their firms.
  • Other personal interests: a lawyer negotiating for a job with the opponent's firm (cmt [10]); a defense lawyer angling for a prosecutor job or running for DA who calls his client a "monster"; a lawyer's own exposure for the conduct at issue.
  • Class quiz 22.A Q9 and Q10: a mere acquaintance with the opposing party "generally require[s] no action," so accept without disclosing. An ex-spouse on the other side is a close personal relationship, a MR 1.7(a)(2) conflict: notify the client and proceed only with its informed consent, confirmed in writing (the ex-spouse isn't the one who consents).
  • Imputation: personal-interest conflicts under MR 1.7(a)(2) generally aren't imputed unless other firm lawyers are similarly affected (CB 583; MR 1.10(a)(1)).

24. Concurrent Clients: The Concurrent Client Rule (MR 1.7)

week 6
rule

MR 1.7 — conflict of interest: current clients

(a) A lawyer shall not represent a client if the representation involves a concurrent conflict of interest, which exists if any one suffices: (1) the representation of one client will be directly adverse to another client; or (2) there is a significant risk that the representation of one or more clients will be materially limited by the lawyer's responsibilities to another client, a former client, or a third person, or by a personal interest of the lawyer.

(b) Notwithstanding a concurrent conflict, a lawyer may represent a client if all required:
(1) the lawyer reasonably believes that the lawyer will be able to provide competent and diligent representation to each affected client;
(2) the representation is not prohibited by law;
(3) the representation does not involve the assertion of a claim by one client against another client represented by the lawyer in the same litigation or other proceeding before a tribunal; and
(4) each affected client gives informed consent, confirmed in writing.

Direct adversity: MR 1.7(a)(1)

  • The classic example: representing one current client in litigation or negotiation against another current client represented by other counsel. Forbidden without both clients' informed consent, confirmed in writing (CB 598).
  • Unrelated matters still count (CB 600). Because the rule rests on loyalty, the adversity need not be in the same or a related matter, and no confidential information need be at risk. Suing a current closing client in a tort case is a conflict.
  • "Directly adverse" means head-on, nose-to-nose adversity in the same litigation or transaction (CB 603). It also covers cross-examining a current client as an adverse witness and serving a third-party subpoena on a current client (class quiz 22.B Q4, Q6). Economic competitors aren't directly adverse (cmt [6]): representing Bullseye and Wal-Mark, each sued by its own employees, is fine (class quiz 22.B Q5).
  • Prospective clients: before taking a new matter, ask whether the new client would be directly adverse to a current client; if so, decline unless both properly consent (CB 599; class quiz 22.B Q1: decline the engagement unless both Opal and Nona waive).
  • Discovered midstream: if a concurrent conflict arises after both engagements are underway, withdraw as soon as possible, probably from both (CB 599).

Current or former client? The hot-potato rule

  • The line matters (CB 600): you may never be adverse to a current client, but you may be adverse to a former client in any matter where you can't use its confidential information against it. When an engagement's endpoint is unclear, the client may still be current (Part II).
  • The hot-potato rule (CB 601): you may not drop a current client "like a hot potato" to turn it into a former client so you can take a matter against it for a client you like better. Violating it isn't a MR 1.7 violation, so no discipline; the consequence is disqualification from the new engagement (class quiz 22.B Q1–Q2).

Material limitation: MR 1.7(a)(2)

Two rough categories, treated the same (CB 605):

  • (1) Currently indirect conflicts (CB 606): adversity isn't nose-to-nose in the same matter but is serious now. Weigh how the indirectly affected client will be affected and how adverse the effects are likely to be. Positional conflicts (inconsistent positions on an issue of law or fact for two clients in different cases) usually need no consent, unless there is "a significant risk that a lawyer's action on behalf of one client will materially limit the lawyer's effectiveness in representing another client in a different case; for example, when a decision favoring one client will create a precedent likely to seriously weaken the position taken on behalf of the other client" (MR 1.7 cmt [24]). Opposite positions on the same ordinance in the same court qualify (class quiz 22.B Q3, Q8). Even a positional conflict that needs no consent can hurt client relations.
  • (2) Possible future conflicts (CB 607): adversity is insignificant now, but there is a reasonable possibility it will become serious or direct later. If so, you must not proceed unless you inform all affected clients and get their informed consent, confirmed in writing, up front, or else represent only one.

Joint representation

  • The most common possible-future conflict (CB 608): joint clients' interests can almost always diverge, which requires disclosure and every joint client's informed consent, confirmed in writing, at the outset. If the conflict becomes actual later, more steps are needed (below).
  • Driver and passenger (class quiz 22.B Q11): the classic conflict is the passenger's possible claim against the driver; there could be a conflict if the evidence leads the passenger to want a cross-claim. Co-defendants (class quiz 22.B Q9): once the facts give one defendant reason to blame the other, the clients are actually adverse, and the lawyer should withdraw from both (MR 1.9 then bars staying on for either against the other). In criminal cases, ordinarily decline to represent more than one co-defendant (cmt [23]).
  • Forming a business for several clients is permitted with each client's informed consent, confirmed in writing, about the conflicts and the lawyer's inability to advocate for any one of them (cmt [28]; class quiz 22.B Q10). Scriveners (documenting a deal the clients already struck) and aggregate settlements (MR 1.8(g); class quiz 22.B Q7) raise their own issues (CB 610–611).
  • Divorcing spouses in a contested proceeding: representing both is a nonconsentable direct conflict (MR 1.7(b)(3); class quiz 22.B Q12). The clients' satisfaction is irrelevant.

Once you have a concurrent conflict (CB 614)

  • (1) You must either abstain or withdraw, or obtain all affected clients' informed consent, confirmed in writing (better, signed by each), if the conflict is consentable and the clients agree.
  • (2) Otherwise, discipline and disqualification can follow. The conflict exists as soon as interests conflict or a significant risk arises; no actual harm is required.
  • (3) If the conflict caused actual harm, tort damages are available.
  • (4) Many jurisdictions treat fees incurred under a serious unwaived conflict as unearned and forfeited.

25. Former and Prospective Clients: The Successive Client Rule (MR 1.9, 1.18(c))

week 7

Reading: MR 1.9; Ch. 22.C.

rule

MR 1.9 — duties to former clients

(a) A lawyer who has formerly represented a client in a matter shall not thereafter represent another person (all elements bar the representation):
(1) in the same or a substantially related matter;
(2) in which that person's interests are materially adverse to the interests of the former client;
(3) unless the former client gives informed consent, confirmed in writing.

(b) A lawyer shall not knowingly represent a person in the same or a substantially related matter in which a firm with which the lawyer formerly was associated had previously represented a client (1) whose interests are materially adverse to that person, and (2) about whom the lawyer had acquired information protected by MR 1.6 and 1.9(c) that is material to the matter, unless the former client gives informed consent, confirmed in writing.

(c) A lawyer who has formerly represented a client, or whose present or former firm has, shall not thereafter (1) use information relating to the representation to the former client's disadvantage except as the Rules permit or when the information has become generally known; or (2) reveal information relating to the representation except as the Rules permit.

  • The point (CB 637): the old engagement and the new one must not involve the same dispute or deal, or a substantively similar one, where the new client's interests are materially adverse to the former client's. Unrelated later matters are fine.
  • Prospective clients (MR 1.18(c); CB 629): similar language, but the bar applies only if the information received "could be significantly harmful" to the prospective client in the matter, which makes the protection a little narrower than for former clients.
  • Current clients (CB 629): the concurrent client rule already forbids adversity in any respect, so current clients' confidences get broader protection.
note

A practical paraphrase of the successive client rule (CB 630)

When a lawyer owes a duty of confidentiality to anyone (a prospective, current, or former client, or someone else), the lawyer must not take on or continue an engagement in which the lawyer might reasonably be able to use that confidential information against the person to whom the duty is owed.

The substantial relationship test (CB 630–635)

  • Where it's used: mostly in discipline and civil disqualification proceedings (CB 631).
  • Two methods, often mixed any one suffices: (1) the person seeking protection describes in general terms the confidential information actually confided, or discloses specific confidences under seal or in camera (CB 632); or (2) without revealing anything, it describes the earlier engagement from the outside, and the court infers what confidential information was likely imparted, compares it with what is likely to matter in the new engagement (also viewed from the outside), and asks whether the two are substantially related (CB 633). Don't confuse a substantial relationship between matters with how "substantial" the lawyer-client relationship was.
  • Playbook information (CB 635): general information about how a client operates (its "plays") is confidential, but ordinarily isn't enough to create a substantial relationship unless it is so voluminous, detailed, or specifically applicable that it creates a substantial unfair advantage.

Consequences of violating the successive client rule (CB 637)

  • (1) Discipline under MR 1.9(a) or 1.18, limited to information received from a former or prospective client.
  • (2) Disqualification reaches more broadly in most states: it is also available when a lawyer holds substantially related confidential information from anyone to whom the lawyer assumed a duty of confidentiality outside an actual or prospective client relationship. Morrison Knudsen v. Hancock Rothert & Bunshoft (Cal. Ct. App. 1999): disqualified lawyers had acquired a duty to a third party because their client in the old engagement had assumed one.
  • (3) Fee forfeiture for a substantial, serious conflict, with possible disgorgement of fees paid.
  • (4) Damages or restitution only if the conflict actually caused compensable harm or an ill-gotten gain (e.g., insider trading on the former client's information). No use or harm is required for disqualification.
note case · week 7

Westinghouse Elec. Corp. v. Kerr-McGee Corp., 580 F.2d 1311 (7th Cir. 1978) — confidentiality owed to non-clients

Facts: A law firm representing a petroleum industry trade association gathered confidential information from member oil companies, promising to keep it confidential, while another office of the firm represented Westinghouse in an antitrust suit against some of those same companies.

Held: The firm was disqualified: by promising confidentiality, it had assumed a duty to the member companies, which the court described as an implied attorney-client relationship.

Why it matters: A duty of confidentiality, and the disqualification that protects it, can arise without a full client relationship. The casebook agrees with the result but not the "implied representation" reasoning: only one duty was assumed (CB 256, 637).

26. Imputation and Screening (MR 1.10, 1.0(k))

week 8

Reading: MR 1.10; Ch. 22.D.

rule

MR 1.10 — imputation of conflicts: general rule

(a) While lawyers are associated in a firm, none of them shall knowingly represent a client when any one of them practicing alone would be prohibited by MR 1.7 or 1.9, unless any one suffices: (1) the prohibition is based on a personal interest of the disqualified lawyer and does not present a significant risk of materially limiting the representation by the remaining lawyers; or (2) the prohibition is based on MR 1.9(a) or (b), arises out of the disqualified lawyer's association with a prior firm, and all required: (i) the disqualified lawyer is timely screened from any participation in the matter and is apportioned no part of the fee; (ii) written notice is promptly given to any affected former client, describing the screening procedures, stating the firm's and the screened lawyer's compliance, stating that review may be available before a tribunal, and agreeing to respond promptly to written inquiries or objections; and (iii) certifications of compliance are provided by the screened lawyer and a partner, at reasonable intervals on the former client's written request and when the screening ends.

(b) When a lawyer has left a firm, the firm may represent a person materially adverse to a client the departed lawyer represented and the firm no longer represents, unless (both required for the bar): (1) the matter is the same or substantially related; and (2) a lawyer remaining in the firm has material information protected by MR 1.6 and 1.9(c).

(c) An imputed disqualification may be waived by the affected client under the conditions of MR 1.7. (d) Government lawyers' firms are governed by MR 1.11 (Part VII).

  • The general rule (CB 647): if any lawyer in a practice organization has a conflict (or a relationship or knowledge that could create one), every lawyer and the organization itself is deemed to have it. In most situations the presumption is conclusive (CB 649). It applies across offices and states: Keisha, Chrissy, Victor in Garden, and Abigail in Keystone are all conflicted (class quiz 22.D Q1).
  • Rebuttable presumptions (CB 650): in seven defined situations, the firm may rebut imputation, and other lawyers may proceed without any client consent as long as the personally disqualified lawyer stays out. Most states apply the same exceptions to disqualification.

Rebutting imputation with situational facts

Exception

When it applies

Personal interest (MR 1.10(a)(1))

Narrower than it sounds (CB 652): most personal-interest conflicts are conclusively imputed by MR 1.8(k). The ones that aren't are sex with a client (MR 1.8(j)) and MR 1.7(a)(2) personal-interest conflicts. Class quiz 22.D Q2: Amir's religious objection to suing the church isn't imputed if he does no work on the matter and his beliefs create no significant risk of limiting Leona's representation (MR 1.10 cmt [3]).

The mobile lawyer's actual knowledge (MR 1.9(b))

A lawyer who changes jobs brings to, and imputes within, the new firm only confidential information the lawyer personally knew (CB 654). It has no effect on concurrent conflicts. Class quiz 22.D Q3: Lulu, whose conflict was purely imputed at her old firm and who acquired no material information about the adverse party, needs no screen (MR 1.9(b), cmt [5]).

Everyone who knew has left (MR 1.10(b))

Every lawyer exposed to the client's information must have left, and no trace of it (paper or digital) may remain (CB 658). It applies only to successive conflicts, never concurrent ones.

Rebutting imputation with an ethical screen

  • What a screen is (MR 1.0(k); CB 660): prophylactic measures restricting confidential information to a lawyer or small group and keeping it from everyone else in the firm, through the timely imposition of procedures reasonably adequate to protect the information. Most conflicts can't be cured by a screen, and a screen never cures a concurrent conflict.
  • Common features (MR 1.0 cmts [8]–[10]; all expected):
    (1) the screened lawyer's written undertaking not to communicate about the matter or touch its files;
    (2) written notice and instructions to all other firm personnel forbidding communication with the screened lawyer about it;
    (3) denial of access to files and information, paper and electronic;
    (4) periodic reminders to everyone;
    (5) no share of the fee where a Rule requires it (MR 1.10(a)(2), 1.11, 1.12, 1.18(d)); and
    (6) timeliness: in place as soon as practical once the firm knows or reasonably should know a screen is needed (cmt [10]).
  • Only four successive-conflict situations can be cured by a screen alone (CB 663–667; any one suffices):
    (1) nonlawyer staff exposed to confidential information (MR 1.10 cmt [4]);
    (2) a lawyer exposed to the information before becoming a lawyer, e.g., as a paralegal or law clerk (MR 1.10 cmt [4]);
    (3) a lateral lawyer's actual knowledge from a prior firm (MR 1.10(a)(2), with notice and certifications); and
    (4) a prospective client's information, if the lawyer took reasonable measures to avoid more exposure than needed to decide whether to take the matter, is timely screened with no part of the fee, and written notice is promptly given to the prospective client (MR 1.18(d)(2)).
  • Government lawyers (MR 1.11) and former judges and neutrals (MR 1.12) have their own screening rules (Part VII).

27. Addressing Conflicts: Abstention, Consent, and Conflict Checks

week 9

Reading: Ch. 22.E–F.

Abstain or withdraw (CB 678–679)

  • Abstaining: "No conflict; no consequences." But you can abstain only from an engagement you never took on. Once the prospective client reasonably believes you're his lawyer, you are, and a conflicted engagement then requires withdrawal, likely from every engagement creating the conflict, unless all affected clients give informed consent, confirmed in writing, to which engagements continue and on what terms.
  • Two ways to proceed (CB 680; any one suffices): (1) rebut imputation and staff the matter with lawyers who aren't personally disqualified (MR 1.10); or (2) if the conflict is consentable, obtain all affected clients' informed consent in the required form and substance.

When is a conflict consentable? (CB 681)

rule

The four nonconsentable conflicts

A conflict can't be cured by consent when any one suffices:
(1) the representation is prohibited by law (MR 1.7(b)(2));
(2) it is one of the personal-interest conflicts MR 1.8 forbids outright;
(3) it involves the assertion of a claim by one client against another client the lawyer represents in the same proceeding before a tribunal (MR 1.7(b)(3)); or
(4) the lawyer doesn't reasonably believe the lawyer can provide competent and diligent representation to each affected client (MR 1.7(b)(1)). The belief must be subjectively held and objectively reasonable; if no reasonable client would consent, the conflict is nonconsentable.

  • Class quiz 22.E–F Q1: co-defendants Maru and Neo blame each other and have incompatible defenses, so Oscar can't reasonably represent both (MR 1.7(b)(1), cmt [23]). Q2: a braking discrepancy and different injuries between two clients from one accident need evaluation but don't automatically bar Shiva from representing both; with informed consent, confirmed in writing, and MR 1.7(b) satisfied, she may (cmts [18], [23]).

What makes consent informed? (CB 684)

  • An enforceable waiver should, and arguably must, disclose all required:
    (1) "the facts and circumstances giving rise to the situation" (MR 1.0 cmt [6]);
    (2) "the material risks of" the proposed course (MR 1.0(e));
    (3) the "reasonably available alternatives," including the client's absolute right to refuse consent (MR 1.0(e)); and
    (4) the client's right to seek independent advice and take the time needed (MR 1.0 cmt [6]; the casebook recommends always advising independent counsel).
  • Joint representation disclosures (CB 686–687): jointly represented clients have no privilege against one another and, in most states, no confidentiality against one another. Say so up front, and say what happens if interests diverge (the lawyer may have to withdraw from both). Class quiz 22.E–F Q4: Amity can't give informed consent to a joint estate plan without learning of Buzz's child; Chuck must first get Buzz's authorization to disclose (MR 1.6(a)), and if Buzz insists on secrecy, withdraw from both rather than finish the wills while withholding material information (MR 1.7 cmts [18], [19], [31]).
  • Advance waivers (MR 1.7 cmt [22]; CB 691): "The effectiveness of such waivers is generally determined by the extent to which the client reasonably understands the material risks that the waiver entails." That usually requires describing the kind of conflict that later materializes. A specific, explained waiver is more likely to work than an open-ended one, and no advance waiver can authorize a future nonconsentable conflict (class quiz 22.E–F Q7).
  • When interests later diverge: the initial consent doesn't cover new antagonism. Continuing for one of two doctors requires both doctors' informed consent, confirmed in writing, plus compliance with duties to the departing client (MR 1.7 cmt [29]; MR 1.9; class quiz 22.E–F Q6). Cleo, representing her own client's litigation opponent on conflicting positions about the same trade dress, should withdraw from both (class quiz 22.E–F Q3).
  • Every affected client consents. Nicole may represent Orin against current firm client Morris, even in an unrelated transaction, only if both Morris and Orin consent, confirmed in writing (MR 1.7(a)(1), cmt [7]; MR 1.10(a); class quiz 22.E–F Q5).

The form of consent (CB 692)

  • Most states require informed consent confirmed in writing; a few require a writing signed by the client, which is the better practice everywhere.
  • Silence is not consent: "Obtaining informed consent will usually require an affirmative response... In general, a lawyer may not assume consent from a client's or other person's silence" (MR 1.0 cmt [7]).
  • Disclosure comes first. Consent given before the disclosure and explanation "is no consent at all."
note case · week 9

Sheppard Mullin v. J-M Manufacturing Co., 6 Cal. 5th 59 (2018) — advance waivers and undisclosed conflicts

Facts: Sheppard, Mullin, Richter & Hampton took on J-M in a qui tam suit under an engagement letter with a broad advance waiver, without telling J-M that the firm was then also representing one of the public entities suing J-M, in unrelated matters.

Held: The advance waiver didn't cover a known, existing conflict the firm failed to disclose, so J-M's consent wasn't informed and the engagement agreement was unenforceable; the firm could at most seek quantum meruit fees, depending on the seriousness of the violation.

Why it matters: Informed consent requires disclosing conflicts you know about; boilerplate can't waive what wasn't explained (MR 1.7 cmt [22]). The casebook notes the amicus brief arguing that big firms demand such waivers on a take-it-or-leave-it basis.

Seeing conflicts coming (Ch. 22.F; CB 694)

  • Practice organizations must maintain policies, procedures, and systems to recognize and respond to conflicts before agreeing to a new engagement.
  • A conflicts database tracks every interaction that may have created duties to anyone: clients, prospective clients, adverse parties, co-parties, and people to whom confidentiality was promised. Check each potential engagement, and everyone involved, against it.
  • Run the check before receiving more than the minimum information (MR 1.18(d)(2)), and update it when parties are added, firms merge, or lawyers join (MR 1.6(b)(7)). MR 5.1(a) makes partners responsible for having such a system (Part IX).
roadmap

Conflicts analysis in order

1. Identify every person to whom duties are owed: current, former, and prospective clients, and non-clients promised confidentiality.

2. Classify the conflict: personal interest (MR 1.8, 1.7(a)(2)); concurrent (MR 1.7(a)(1)–(2)); successive (MR 1.9, 1.18(c)).

3. Impute it across the firm (MR 1.10(a), 1.8(k)), then ask whether imputation can be rebutted by situational facts or a screen.

4. Ask whether it is consentable (MR 1.7(b)(1)–(3); MR 1.8's outright bans).

5. Get informed consent from every affected client in the required form, or abstain or withdraw (MR 1.16(a)(1)).

6. Name the consequences: disqualification, discipline, fee forfeiture, damages.

Part VII — Government Lawyers

28. The Government Lawyer's Client, Conflicts, and Extra Duties

week 10

Reading: MR 1.11, 1.12, 3.8; Ch. 25. Week 10 also brought the 15-question multiple-choice exam (Oct. 26).

  • Same rules, with a few twists (CB 816): government lawyers follow the same Rules as everyone else, plus
    (1) obligations outside the Rules that apply because they are government employees;
    (2) recurring situations that make the general Rules hard to apply; and
    (3) two Rules that apply only to them: MR 1.11 (conflicts for current and former government lawyers) and MR 3.8 (prosecutors).
  • Where they may practice (CB 817): state and local government lawyers need a license where they practice, like anyone else. The federal government requires a license in at least one state or D.C., and by statute (28 U.S.C. § 517) a federal lawyer may appear in any state or federal court nationwide (class quiz Ch. 25 Q5). Federal lawyers still follow the conduct rules of the jurisdiction or court where they appear (the McDade Amendment, 28 U.S.C. § 530B); with no tribunal involved, the rules of the licensing jurisdiction generally govern.

Identifying the government client (CB 818–827)

  • Often clear: a specific state, county, city, or agency. Sometimes the lawyer represents an interest or function of government, nominally represents the agency head while really representing the agency, or personally represents an employee, but only to protect the employer's interests too.
  • The law that creates the entity defines it (CB 818): constitutions, statutes, and regulations identify the client and the official empowered to direct counsel and speak for it. The Rules won't tell you who calls the shots.
  • Prosecutors represent the sovereign: the State or "the People," not the DA personally, the victim, or the investigating officer, who are witnesses (class quiz Ch. 25 Q2, Q6). The victim's own private lawyer represents the victim (Q7).
  • Defending government actors (CB 822): governments act only through people, so suits often name employees. In an official-capacity suit the true client is the agency, and the named official is its procedural representative (Q3, Q8). When a government lawyer represents an employee personally (e.g., a Bivens damages claim against a federal officer), the employee is a full client owed every duty, yet the lawyer represents her only to serve the government's interests, so the agency's interests always count too. Conflicts can develop midstream, so disclose and get informed consents from everyone at the outset (CB 827).

Conflicts within government (CB 825)

  • Are two agencies one client? Sometimes substantive law says whether agencies are distinct enough to be different "clients," so one government law office can represent one against the other. Sometimes officials agree which lawyers handle each side; written informed consents resolve any lingering concern.
  • Absent agreement, infer the client from reasonable expectations, considering weighed together:
    (1) how the government client is legally defined and funded;
    (2) whether it has independent legal authority over the matter; and
    (3) the extent to which the matter has broader implications for the government. Resolve doubt in favor of detailed written disclosures and consents all around.
  • Even when two entities aren't the same client, MR 1.7(a)(2) still bars a significant risk of material limitation.

Duties outside the Rules (CB 829)

  • Leaders of government law offices and courts often expect a "higher standard" of government lawyers. Specific laws apply that don't bind private lawyers: conflict-of-interest statutes, "ethics in government" laws, codes of conduct, personnel rules, and gift rules that often bar even shared meals from people with business before the agency.
  • Class quiz Ch. 25 Q1: may Patty accept Derrick's offer? "Maybe": besides the Rules, she must check the gift laws and other government ethics rules.

29. The Revolving Door (MR 1.11, 1.12)

week 10
  • MR 1.11 covers service as any "public officer or employee," not only as a practicing lawyer (CB 831).
  • Concurrent conflicts are not imputed in government (CB 832). An individual government lawyer may not take part in a matter adverse to her own current client, but others in her office may. MR 1.10 doesn't apply (MR 1.10(d)); MR 1.11 does.
rule

MR 1.11(a)–(c) — former government lawyers

(a) A lawyer who formerly served as a public officer or employee (1) is subject to MR 1.9(c); and (2) shall not otherwise represent a client in connection with a matter in which the lawyer participated personally and substantially as a public officer or employee, unless the appropriate government agency gives its informed consent, confirmed in writing.

(b) When a lawyer is disqualified under (a), no lawyer in the lawyer's firm may knowingly undertake or continue the matter unless all required: (1) the disqualified lawyer is timely screened from any participation and is apportioned no part of the fee; and (2) written notice is promptly given to the appropriate government agency to enable it to ascertain compliance.

(c) A lawyer having information the lawyer knows is confidential government information about a person, acquired as a public officer or employee, may not represent a private client whose interests are adverse to that person in a matter in which the information could be used to the person's material disadvantage. The firm may proceed only if the disqualified lawyer is timely screened and apportioned no part of the fee.

(e) "Matter" includes (1) any judicial or other proceeding, application, request for a ruling or other determination, contract, claim, controversy, investigation, charge, accusation, arrest, or other particular matter involving a specific party or parties; and (2) any other matter covered by the agency's conflict rules.

rule

MR 1.11(d) — current government lawyers

A lawyer currently serving as a public officer or employee (1) is subject to MR 1.7 and 1.9; and (2) shall not (either is barred): (i) participate in a matter in which the lawyer participated personally and substantially while in private practice or nongovernmental employment, unless the agency gives informed consent, confirmed in writing; or (ii) negotiate for private employment with any person who is involved as a party or as lawyer for a party in a matter in which the lawyer is participating personally and substantially, except that a law clerk to a judge, adjudicative officer, or arbitrator may negotiate as MR 1.12(b) allows.

  • Why (d)(2)(i) bars even helping the former client (CB 839): MR 1.9(a) bars only adversity to a former client, but MR 1.11(d)(2)(i) bars participation altogether, to guard against using government power for the special benefit of the former client.

rule

MR 1.12 — former judges, arbitrators, mediators, other neutrals, and law clerks

(a) Except as stated in (d), a lawyer shall not represent anyone in connection with a matter in which the lawyer participated personally and substantially as a judge or other adjudicative officer, a law clerk to one, or an arbitrator, mediator, or other third-party neutral, unless all parties to the proceeding give informed consent, confirmed in writing.

(b) A judge, adjudicative officer, arbitrator, mediator, or other neutral shall not negotiate for employment with any party or lawyer for a party in a matter in which the lawyer is participating personally and substantially. A law clerk may, but only after notifying the judge or other adjudicative officer.

(c) If a lawyer is disqualified by (a), the firm may proceed only if all required: (1) the disqualified lawyer is timely screened and apportioned no part of the fee; and (2) written notice is promptly given to the parties and any appropriate tribunal.

(d) A partisan arbitrator on a multimember panel may later represent that party.

30. Special Duties of Prosecutors (MR 3.8)

week 10

Each paragraph of MR 3.8 marks a point in the criminal process where a prosecutor must favor justice over merely winning (CB 843). A prosecutor "may strike hard blows, [but] is not at liberty to strike foul ones" (Berger v. United States).

rule

MR 3.8 — the prosecutor in a criminal case shall:

(a) refrain from prosecuting a charge that the prosecutor knows is not supported by probable cause;

(b) make reasonable efforts to assure that the accused has been advised of the right to, and the procedure for obtaining, counsel and has been given reasonable opportunity to obtain counsel;

(c) not seek to obtain from an unrepresented accused a waiver of important pretrial rights, such as the right to a preliminary hearing;

(d) make timely disclosure to the defense of all evidence or information known to the prosecutor that tends to negate the guilt of the accused or mitigates the offense, and, in connection with sentencing, disclose to the defense and to the tribunal all unprivileged mitigating information known to the prosecutor, unless relieved by a protective order of the tribunal;

(e) not subpoena a lawyer in a grand jury or other criminal proceeding to present evidence about a past or present client unless the prosecutor reasonably believes all required:
(1) the information sought is not protected from disclosure by any applicable privilege;
(2) the evidence sought is essential to the successful completion of an ongoing investigation or prosecution; and
(3) there is no other feasible alternative to obtain the information;

(f) except for statements necessary to inform the public of the nature and extent of the prosecutor's action that serve a legitimate law enforcement purpose, refrain from extrajudicial comments that have a substantial likelihood of heightening public condemnation of the accused, and exercise reasonable care to prevent investigators, police, and others assisting the prosecutor from making statements the prosecutor couldn't make under MR 3.6 or this Rule;

(g) when the prosecutor knows of new, credible, and material evidence creating a reasonable likelihood that a convicted defendant did not commit an offense of which the defendant was convicted: (1) promptly disclose it to an appropriate court or authority; and (2) if the conviction was obtained in the prosecutor's jurisdiction, (i) promptly disclose it to the defendant unless a court authorizes delay, and (ii) undertake further investigation, or make reasonable efforts to cause an investigation, to determine whether the defendant was convicted of an offense the defendant did not commit; and

(h) when the prosecutor knows of clear and convincing evidence establishing that a defendant in the prosecutor's jurisdiction was convicted of an offense the defendant did not commit, seek to remedy the conviction.

  • (a) Probable cause (CB 844): a floor, not a target; the Rule is violated only when the prosecutor knows probable cause is lacking.
  • (b)–(c) Pretrial rights (CB 846): the prosecutor can't sit idly by if the court fails to advise the accused of the right to counsel. Class quiz Ch. 25 Q4: gently remind the judge that the defendant wasn't advised of the right to counsel and that her finances should be assessed for appointed counsel. MR 3.8(c) helps ensure that waivers of rights like a preliminary hearing are knowing and voluntary. It doesn't apply to an accused appearing pro se with the tribunal's approval, or forbid lawful questioning of an uncharged suspect who has knowingly waived counsel and silence (cmt [2]).
  • (d) Disclosure (CB 847): broader than the constitutional rule. Brady v. Maryland and Giglio v. United States require disclosing material exculpatory and impeachment evidence as due process, but the Constitution requires less for impeachment evidence before a plea (United States v. Ruiz) and limits mandated disclosure of impeachment material (Strickler v. Greene). MR 3.8(d) has no materiality limit: everything that tends to negate guilt or mitigate, timely.
  • (e) Subpoenas to lawyers (CB 849): the testimony must be
    (1) unprivileged,
    (2) essential, and
    (3) the only feasible source.
  • (f) Publicity (CB 850): on top of MR 3.6 (Part VIII); no "perp walk" commentary that heightens condemnation.
  • (g)–(h) Wrongful convictions (CB 851). "Credible" means a reasonable factfinder could believe it; "material" means it creates a reasonable likelihood the defendant didn't commit the offense, so it must bear on guilt (impeachment or mitigation evidence is covered by MR 3.8(d) instead, except impeachment so devastating that it meets the standard).

note case · week 10

Brady v. Maryland, 373 U.S. 83 (1963) — suppressing exculpatory evidence

Facts: The prosecution withheld a co-defendant's statement admitting that he, not Brady, did the actual killing.

Held: Suppressing evidence favorable to an accused that is material to guilt or punishment violates due process, whatever the prosecutor's good or bad faith.

Why it matters: The constitutional floor that MR 3.8(d) builds on. Giglio v. United States (1972) extends it to impeachment evidence (a promise of leniency to a key witness), and United States v. Bagley (1985) defines materiality as a reasonable probability of a different result. MR 3.8(d) has no materiality limit.

note case · week 10

Berger v. United States, 295 U.S. 78 (1935) — the prosecutor's role

Facts: A federal prosecutor misstated facts, put words in witnesses' mouths, and made improper insinuations in a counterfeiting trial.

Held: The misconduct required a new trial. The prosecutor represents a sovereign "whose interest... in a criminal prosecution is not that it shall win a case, but that justice shall be done"; he "may strike hard blows, [but] is not at liberty to strike foul ones."

Why it matters: The source of the "higher standard" courts expect of government lawyers (CB 829) and the theme of MR 3.8.

31. The Lawyer as Counselor and Neutral (MR 2.1, 2.3, 2.4, 3.9)

week 11

Reading: MR 2.1, 2.3, 2.4, 3.1–3.7, 3.9; Ch. 24.

rule

MR 2.1 — advisor

In representing a client, a lawyer shall exercise independent professional judgment and render candid advice. In rendering advice, a lawyer may refer not only to law but to other considerations such as moral, economic, social, and political factors that may be relevant to the client's situation.

  • Don't soften advice because the client won't like it (cmt [1]); purely technical legal advice can be inadequate (cmt [2]). When a client proposes something unlawful, MR 2.1 is the license for extra-legal advice about whether it's wise (Part XI). The client still decides objectives (MR 1.2(a)).
rule

MR 2.3 — evaluation for use by third persons


(a) A lawyer may evaluate a matter affecting a client for someone other than the client if the lawyer reasonably believes the evaluation is compatible with other aspects of the relationship.
(b) When the lawyer knows or reasonably should know the evaluation is likely to affect the client's interests materially and adversely, the lawyer shall not provide it unless the client gives informed consent.
(c) Except as disclosure is authorized in connection with the report, information relating to the evaluation is otherwise protected by MR 1.6.

  • Examples: a title opinion for a purchaser, an opinion letter for a lender, an audit-response letter (cmts [1], [6]). Whether the lawyer owes the third person a legal duty is a question of other law (cmt [3]); the casebook treats such reliance as one way to assume a duty of care to a non-client (Part II). Distinguish an evaluation from investigating a non-client, e.g., the vendor's title for the purchaser (cmt [2]).
rule

MR 2.4 — lawyer serving as third-party neutral

(a) A lawyer is a third-party neutral when helping two or more non-clients resolve a dispute or other matter (arbitrator, mediator, or similar). (b) The neutral shall inform unrepresented parties that the lawyer is not representing them, and when the lawyer knows or reasonably should know a party doesn't understand the role, shall explain the difference between a neutral and a lawyer who represents a client.

  • Later representing a party in the same matter is governed by MR 1.12 (Part VII). Lawyers representing clients in ADR follow the Rules: candor under MR 3.3 in binding arbitration (a "tribunal," MR 1.0(m)); otherwise MR 4.1 (cmt [5]).
rule

MR 3.9 — advocate in nonadjudicative proceedings

A lawyer representing a client before a legislative body or administrative agency in a nonadjudicative proceeding (lobbying, rulemaking, hearings) shall all required: (1) disclose that the appearance is in a representative capacity; and (2) conform to MR 3.3(a)–(c), 3.4(a)–(c), and 3.5.

  • It applies to official hearings or meetings where the lawyer presents evidence or argument, not to bilateral negotiations with the government (cmt [3]).

32. Gathering and Handling Evidence (MR 3.4, 4.4)

week 11

The officer of the court (Ch. 24.A; CB 744)

Being "an officer of the court" brings three ideas into play (all three):
(1) advocacy may draw extra scrutiny beyond other lawyer conduct;
(2) courts expect behavior beyond the minimum demands of the Rules and procedure; and
(3) the advocate's loyalty to the client is constrained by enforceable duties owed to the court and the legal system.

Investigate lawfully (Ch. 24.B.1; CB 746)

  • MR 4.4(a): in representing a client, a lawyer shall not use methods of obtaining evidence that violate the legal rights of a third person (trespass, wiretapping, intrusion into seclusion).
  • Class quiz Ch. 24 A–B Q1: an investigator videotaping the "housebound" plaintiff from a public street probably doesn't violate MR 4.4(a), unless local law forbids that surveillance. The lawyer answers for an investigator's unlawful methods (MR 5.3, 8.4(a)).
  • Q2: prosecutors may not lie to a suspect about the evidence ("the video shows you in the bank" when it's impossible to tell). Police may use some deception in interrogation (Frazier v. Cupp), but lawyers, including prosecutors, are bound by MR 4.1(a) and 8.4(c) (CB 746 n.4).
  • Q4: a prosecutor threatening to circulate humiliating photographs of a minor to coerce cooperation uses means with no substantial purpose other than to embarrass (MR 4.4(a)).

Discovery conduct (CB 748)

rule

MR 3.4(c)–(d)

A lawyer shall not (c) knowingly disobey an obligation under the rules of a tribunal, except for an open refusal based on an assertion that no valid obligation exists; or (d) in pretrial procedure, make a frivolous discovery request or fail to make reasonably diligent effort to comply with a legally proper discovery request by an opposing party.

  • Discovery misconduct is a disciplinary violation as well as sanctionable (FRCP 26(g), 37; Part I). Q5: openly refusing to produce the secret soda formula on the ground that no valid obligation exists isn't a MR 3.4(c) violation (though contempt or sanctions may follow so the issue can be reviewed). Ch. 24.C Q6: citing an unpublished opinion in violation of the court's rule violates MR 3.4(c), even though the brief candidly called it unpublished.

Retaining and handling evidence (Ch. 24.B.3; CB 748–752)

rule

MR 3.4(a)–(b), (f)

A lawyer shall not (a) unlawfully obstruct another party's access to evidence, or unlawfully alter, destroy, or conceal a document or other material having potential evidentiary value, or counsel or assist another to do so; (b) falsify evidence, counsel or assist a witness to testify falsely, or offer an inducement to a witness prohibited by law; or (f) request a person other than a client to refrain from voluntarily giving relevant information to another party, unless (both required) (1) the person is a relative, employee, or other agent of the client, and (2) the lawyer reasonably believes the person's interests won't be adversely affected.

  • "Unlawfully" matters (CB 748): lawyers lawfully withhold all the time: proper discovery objections, instructions not to answer privileged questions, advising a client not to talk to police.
  • "Potential" matters (CB 749): the material needn't be admissible or requested; it need only be relevant to or potentially useful in a pending or reasonably foreseeable proceeding. Most jurisdictions forbid spoliation, and electronically stored information has intensified the focus.
  • Consequences for the client (CB 750): monetary sanctions, then evidentiary or terminating sanctions after an order compelling discovery; for lost material that should have been preserved, remedial measures even without bad faith (e.g., failing to halt automatic deletion).
  • Counsel's own duty (CB 750): many courts impose personal obligations on lawyers for their clients' preservation and production. Become actively involved immediately on retention, and circulate a litigation hold (document preservation memo) to everyone who may have relevant material (Zubulake).
  • Evidence handed to you (CB 752): your possession can put you in a legal and ethical bind. You may not conceal, destroy, or alter it, or counsel anyone else to (MR 3.4(a)). Class quiz Q7: driving the client's bloody knife to another town and throwing it in a dumpster is unlawful concealment and destruction, a clear MR 3.4(a) violation and likely obstruction; no proceeding need be pending. Many courts hold that a lawyer who takes possession of physical evidence of a crime may keep it a reasonable time for examination but must then turn it over to the authorities.
  • MR 3.4(f) (CB 364): you may ask your own client, or the client's relatives, employees, or agents, not to talk voluntarily to the other side, but only if you reasonably believe they won't be hurt by it (Part XI).
note case · week 11

Zubulake v. UBS Warburg LLC, 229 F.R.D. 422 (S.D.N.Y. 2004) — litigation holds and counsel's duty to preserve

Facts: In an employment discrimination suit, UBS employees deleted relevant emails and failed to produce others despite counsel's instructions to preserve them.

Held: Counsel must issue a litigation hold, communicate with key players, and monitor compliance, and the court gave an adverse-inference instruction for the lost emails (Scheindlin, J.).

Inadvertently sent material: MR 4.4(b) (CB 753–757)

rule

MR 4.4(b)

A lawyer who receives a document or electronically stored information relating to the representation of the lawyer's client and knows or reasonably should know that it was inadvertently sent shall promptly notify the sender.

  • That's the whole Model Rule duty. Whether to return it, stop reading, or delete it is left to other law and professional judgment (cmts [2]–[3]). Metadata is covered (cmt [2]; class quiz Q3: track-changes comments in produced files trigger only the duty to notify).
  • FRCP 26(b)(5)(B) adds duties in federal civil cases: once notified of a privilege claim, the receiving party must promptly return, sequester, or destroy the material, not use or disclose it until the claim is resolved, and may present it to the court under seal (class quiz Q8: both may apply).
  • The ABA's views have evolved (from "return it unread" to "just notify"), and states vary widely; some stricter states disqualify counsel who exploit privileged material, and evidentiary sanctions may also be available (CB 755–757).
  • Q6: opposing counsel's email to his client, sent to you by mistake ("Dear Cynthia"): promptly notify the sender, and don't contact his represented client (MR 4.2).
  • Protect your own documents: know what metadata your software embeds, and scrub documents before they leave the firm unless there's a good reason not to (CB 754 n.9; MR 1.1, 1.6(c)).

Impartiality and decorum: MR 3.5 (CB 764)

rule

MR 3.5

A lawyer shall not (any one is a violation):
(a) seek to influence a judge, juror, prospective juror, or other official by means prohibited by law;
(b) communicate ex parte with such a person during the proceeding unless authorized by law or court order;
(c) communicate with a juror or prospective juror after discharge of the jury if (any one bars it)
(1) the communication is prohibited by law or court order,
(2) the juror has made known a desire not to communicate, or
(3) the communication involves misrepresentation, coercion, duress, or harassment; or (d) engage in conduct intended to disrupt a tribunal.

  • Officials include court reporters: offering a reporter $1,000 to "fix" the deposition transcript violates MR 3.5(a) even though she refused (class quiz Ch. 24.C Q1).

(5) a request for assistance in obtaining evidence;
(6) a warning of danger concerning a person's behavior when there's reason to believe there is a likelihood of substantial harm; and
(7) in a criminal case, also (i) the accused's identity, residence, occupation, and family status; (ii) information necessary to aid in apprehending an accused at large; (iii) the fact, time, and place of arrest; and (iv) the identity of the investigating and arresting officers or agencies and the length of the investigation.

(c) Right of reply: a statement a reasonable lawyer would believe is required to protect a client from the substantial undue prejudicial effect of recent publicity not initiated by the lawyer or client, limited to what's necessary to mitigate it.

(d) No lawyer in the same firm or government agency may make a statement prohibited by (a).

  • Presumptively prejudicial subjects (cmt [5]; any one raises the risk):
    (1) the character, credibility, reputation, or criminal record of a party, suspect, or witness, or the expected testimony of a party or witness;
    (2) in a criminal case, the possibility of a guilty plea, or the existence or contents of a confession, admission, or statement, or a refusal to make one;
    (3) the performance or results of any examination or test, or a refusal to submit to one;
    (4) any opinion as to the guilt or innocence of a defendant or suspect;
    (5) information the lawyer knows or reasonably should know is likely to be inadmissible and would create a substantial risk of prejudicing an impartial trial; and
    (6) the fact that a defendant has been charged with a crime, unless accompanied by a statement that the charge is merely an accusation.
  • Who's covered: only lawyers participating (or who participated) in the matter and their firm or agency colleagues. A blogging lawyer who isn't involved isn't covered (Q12).
  • Only extrajudicial statements: a closing argument in a packed courtroom isn't one (Q13). Telling a reporter when you'll finish calling witnesses is a safe harbor (MR 3.6(b)(4); Q14). An opinion on guilt is not a safe harbor (cmt [5](4); Q15). A sealed indictment isn't a public record (Q16). Revealing breathalyzer results on the eve of trial is presumptively prejudicial (cmt [5](3); Q17). Prosecutors have the extra limits of MR 3.8(f) (Part VII).
  • Overcorrecting destroys the right of reply: go only as far as reasonably necessary to mitigate (CB 772).
note case · week 11

Gentile v. State Bar of Nevada, 501 U.S. 1030 (1991) — trial publicity and the First Amendment

Facts: Hours after his client's indictment, a defense lawyer held a press conference saying the client was a scapegoat and that a police officer was the likely thief. The client was acquitted months later, and the bar disciplined the lawyer.

Held: The "substantial likelihood of material prejudice" standard is constitutional for lawyers in pending cases, but Nevada's safe-harbor provision (allowing a lawyer to state "the general nature of the defense") was void for vagueness as applied, so the discipline was reversed.

Why it matters: The constitutional backdrop for MR 3.6. It explains the Rule's structure: a prejudice standard, specific safe harbors, and the right of reply in MR 3.6(c).

Lawyer as witness: MR 3.7 (CB 774)

rule

MR 3.7

(a) A lawyer shall not act as advocate at a trial in which the lawyer is likely to be a necessary witness unless any one suffices:
(1) the testimony relates to an uncontested issue;
(2) the testimony relates to the nature and value of legal services rendered in the case; or
(3) disqualification would work substantial hardship on the client. (b) A lawyer may act as advocate at a trial in which another lawyer in the firm is likely to be called as a witness, unless MR 1.7 or 1.9 precludes it.

  • Not imputed (CB 774): a colleague may try the case while you testify. Client consent, admissibility, or being called by the other side are not exceptions (Q18).
  • (a)(2) means your own fees in this case: testifying as a damages expert on what the plaintiff-lawyer would have earned isn't covered (Q19); testifying in your own pro se fee suit is (Q22). MR 3.7 applies to bench trials too, though the confusion risk is lower.

Witness examination and argument: MR 3.4(e) (CB 776)

rule

MR 3.4(e)

In trial, a lawyer shall not (any one is a violation):
(1) allude to any matter the lawyer doesn't reasonably believe is relevant or that won't be supported by admissible evidence;
(2) assert personal knowledge of facts in issue except when testifying as a witness; or
(3) state a personal opinion as to the justness of a cause, the credibility of a witness, the culpability of a civil litigant, or the guilt or innocence of an accused.

  • Class quiz: referring in closing to a conviction the judge excluded violates (1), even with a certified copy in hand (Q10). Asking "Aren't you a wife-beating, pill-popping alcoholic?" with no factual basis violates (1); "Withdrawn," a curative instruction, or acquittal doesn't cure it (Q11). Reciting an unadmitted hearsay statement in closing violates (1), though it supplied a good-faith basis to ask on cross (Q23). "Thank you for your honest testimony" in front of the jury vouches for credibility, violating (3) (Q24).
  • Trickery: secretly seating a look-alike at counsel table to bait a misidentification misleads the tribunal (MR 3.3, 3.4, 3.5(d), 8.4(c)–(d); United States v. Thoreen; Q20). Ordinary cross and argument about weak identification are fine.

34. Candor to the Tribunal (MR 3.3)

week 11
rule

MR 3.3 — candor toward the tribunal

(a) A lawyer shall not knowingly:
(1) make a false statement of fact or law to a tribunal, or fail to correct a false statement of material fact or law previously made to the tribunal by the lawyer;
(2) fail to disclose legal authority in the controlling jurisdiction known to be directly adverse to the client's position and not disclosed by opposing counsel; or
(3) offer evidence that the lawyer knows to be false. If a lawyer, the lawyer's client, or a witness called by the lawyer has offered material evidence and the lawyer comes to know of its falsity, the lawyer shall take reasonable remedial measures, including, if necessary, disclosure to the tribunal. A lawyer may refuse to offer evidence, other than the testimony of a defendant in a criminal matter, that the lawyer reasonably believes is false.

(b) A lawyer representing a client in an adjudicative proceeding who knows that a person intends to engage, is engaging, or has engaged in criminal or fraudulent conduct related to the proceeding shall take reasonable remedial measures, including, if necessary, disclosure to the tribunal.

(c) The duties in (a) and (b) continue to the conclusion of the proceeding, and apply even if compliance requires disclosure of information otherwise protected by MR 1.6.

(d) In an ex parte proceeding, a lawyer shall inform the tribunal of all material facts known to the lawyer that will enable the tribunal to make an informed decision, whether or not the facts are adverse.

Don't lie, and don't present false evidence (CB 778–785)

  • Lying to a tribunal or presenting false evidence is a disciplinary violation and, when knowing, a crime (perjury, subornation, obstruction) and sanctionable. MR 3.3 covers ancillary proceedings like depositions and sworn interrogatory answers (cmt [1]): Dean can't serve interrogatory answers he knows are false, and the client's verification doesn't absolve him (Q21).
  • "False" evidence includes any falsity that frustrates truth-finding: false statements, or inauthentic exhibits. A photoshopped photo is false evidence even if an unsuspecting witness authenticates it (MR 3.3(a)(3), 3.4(b); Q30). You may offer false evidence to prove its falsity, e.g., a forged agreement offered to prove the forgery (cmt [5]; Q29).
  • MR 3.4(b) vs. MR 3.3(a)(3): 3.4(b) bars falsifying evidence or counseling false testimony; 3.3(a)(3) bars offering evidence you know is false, whoever falsified it.

Witness preparation (CB 779)

  • Nothing forbids properly preparing a witness, client or not, but communications with a non-client witness aren't privileged. Explaining procedure, demeanor ("speak up, make eye contact"), and privilege is fine; refreshing recollection with a document is fine if the memory is genuinely refreshed (Q26). Telling the client that truthful testimony would hurt and advising her not to testify isn't counseling perjury (Q27). Telling her to say she wore snow boots when she wore stilettos is (Problem 24C-8(g)).

Paying witnesses (CB 781–783)

  • Fact witnesses must not be paid to testify, but you may pay their expenses (travel, lodging, meals) and reasonable compensation for time lost from work testifying or preparing, or reviewing records germane to the testimony, if all required:
    (1) the amount is reasonable;
    (2) it is clearly not for the substance or fact of the testimony and is not conditioned on it; and
    (3) the governing jurisdiction permits it (ABA opinion; Q28: a train ticket and hotel for the busy architect are fine).
  • Experts may be paid for their time and testimony, but almost everywhere not a contingent fee, which no witness may receive.
  • A MR 3.4(b) violation means some other law was broken, e.g., the federal anti-gratuity statute (18 U.S.C. § 201); the testimony may be excluded, fees awarded, or a mistrial declared.

Evidence you only suspect is false (CB 785–786)

What you know

What you may or must do

You know it's false

Must refuse to offer it (MR 3.3(a)(3)).

You reasonably believe it's false (but don't know)

May refuse to offer it, even over the client's objection, or may offer it.

You reasonably believe a criminal defendant's own testimony is false (but don't know)

Must let the defendant testify: the decision is the defendant's (MR 1.2(a); Q31). Consult, advise of the risks (inconsistency, disbelief, damage to the rest of the defense), and try to persuade, but if the client insists, it's the client's call.

The criminal defendant who will lie (CB 786)

  • If you know your criminal client will testify falsely, MR 3.3(a)(3) and 1.2(d) say you must not offer it, but the defendant has a constitutional right to testify. No single solution exists; know your jurisdiction's method. The most common in the U.S. is the "narrative" approach: the defendant testifies in narrative form without counsel's questioning, and counsel doesn't argue the false testimony. The Model Rules note that "some jurisdictions" use it without endorsing it (MR 3.3 cmt [7]).
  • In Nix v. Whiteside (1986), the Supreme Court held that a lawyer who threatened to disclose and withdraw if the client testified falsely did not violate the Sixth Amendment.

The duty to correct (CB 789–791)

  • What triggers it any one suffices:
    (1) you made a false statement of material fact or law to the tribunal, knowing or not at the time (MR 3.3(a)(1));
    (2) you, your client, or your witness offered material evidence you later learn is false (MR 3.3(a)(3); only "material" evidence: Q32); or
    (3) you know any person intends, is engaging, or has engaged in criminal or fraudulent conduct related to the proceeding, such as bribing a juror (MR 3.3(b)).
  • Reasonable remedial measures, in order (cmt [10]):

1. Remonstrate. Privately urge the person who supplied the falsehood to correct it, with your help, noting that you must report it if she won't (Q33).

2. Withdraw if the client refuses and withdrawal will cure it (MR 1.16(a)(1)); sometimes you must.

3. Disclose to the tribunal what is reasonably necessary to remedy the situation, even if it reveals MR 1.6 information (MR 3.3(c)). Your own misstatement is usually corrected by a filing, served on opposing counsel (MR 3.5(b)).

  • Minimum disclosure: only what's needed to correct the wrong.
  • Duration: until the conclusion of the proceeding, meaning when a final judgment has been affirmed on appeal or the time for review has passed (cmt [13]). Other consequences (perjury prosecutions, relief from judgment under FRCP 60(b)) have their own time limits.

Ex parte proceedings: MR 3.3(d) (CB 793)

  • With no adversary to correct the record, disclose all material facts, adverse or not (Q7). A sealed qui tam conference on extending the seal is ex parte, so the AUSA must disclose the evidence suggesting an innocent error too (Q34). The duty applies whenever opposing counsel is simply absent: telling the judge a motion is "unopposed" without mentioning your agreement giving the other side 30 days to amend violates MR 3.3 (Malmin v. Oths; Q35). Half-truths can equal misrepresentations (cmt [3]).

35. Responsibility Within a Practice Organization (MR 5.1–5.3)

week 12

Reading: MR 1.13, 1.17, 5.1, 5.2, 5.3, 5.4, 5.6, 5.7; Chs. 19, 23.

  • Any organization offering legal services is a "law firm" (MR 1.0(c); CB 478): partnerships, PCs, solos, legal services organizations, and corporate or government legal departments. The casebook calls them "practice organizations."
  • The organization never shields you (CB 479). Each lawyer has a nondelegable, personal duty to comply with the Rules and other law. Agents are personally liable for their own torts within the scope of the agency, and no one goes to prison for you.
rule

MR 5.2 — the subordinate lawyer

(a) A lawyer is bound by the Rules notwithstanding that the lawyer acted at the direction of another person. (b) A subordinate lawyer does not violate the Rules by acting in accordance with a supervisory lawyer's reasonable resolution of an arguable question of professional duty.

  • The exception is extremely narrow (CB 480): it applies only when it was genuinely unclear how to comply and the supervisor's instruction was a reasonable way to comply. It excuses discipline, not necessarily tort liability. It never covers clear violations (Kodak-Berkey, Part I; Renaldo's buried adverse authority, Part VIII).

Managers and supervisors (CB 484–486)

  • Two kinds of oversight: (1) managerial authority is indirect, general authority over the firm's professional work, or a part of it (a practice-group leader; an office's managing partner), covering both lawyers and staff; and (2) supervisory authority is day-to-day responsibility for specific people's work. A lawyer may have either, both, or neither.

Duty

Over other lawyers (MR 5.1)

Over nonlawyers (MR 5.3)

Manage

(a) Partners and lawyers with comparable managerial authority shall make reasonable efforts to ensure the firm has measures giving reasonable assurance that all lawyers conform to the Rules (e.g., a conflicts-check system, calendaring, trust-account controls). No harm is needed for a violation.

(a) The same for measures giving reasonable assurance that nonlawyers' conduct is compatible with the lawyers' professional obligations: policies so staff don't do what lawyers can't.

Supervise

(b) A lawyer with direct supervisory authority over another lawyer shall make reasonable efforts to ensure that lawyer conforms to the Rules.

(b) A lawyer with direct supervisory authority over a nonlawyer shall make reasonable efforts to ensure the person's conduct is compatible with the lawyer's obligations. Includes outside vendors, investigators, outsourcing, cloud services, and AI tools: select, instruct, and oversee.

Vicarious discipline

(c) Responsible for another lawyer's violation if any one suffices: (1) the lawyer orders it or, with knowledge of the specific conduct, ratifies it; or (2) the lawyer is a partner, manager, or direct supervisor who knows of the conduct when its consequences can be avoided or mitigated and fails to take reasonable remedial action.

(c) The same, for a nonlawyer's conduct that would violate the Rules if a lawyer did it.

  • Not respondeat superior. Discipline requires your own failure: inadequate measures, inadequate supervision, ordering or ratifying, or failing to mitigate what you know. If reasonable measures existed and you neither ordered, ratified, nor could mitigate, you're not responsible (CB 486).
  • Other consequences of failing to manage or supervise (CB 482): civil liability (supervisors for torts they directed or reasonably could have prevented; the firm vicariously), court sanctions (FRCP 11(c)(1) holds the firm jointly responsible), and fee consequences.
  • Causing a subordinate to violate a Rule is the supervisor's own violation too (MR 8.4(a): violating the Rules through the acts of another).

The "duty to be supervised" (Ch. 19.D; CB 489)

  • Not a separate duty but a feature of competence and care: a subordinate must get the support needed to do the job competently. Ask, insist, and if necessary go elsewhere for help. "I asked and asked" is no defense; a looming deadline requires protective action (Part II).

36. Duties to the Firm, Changing Jobs, and Selling a Practice (MR 1.17, 5.7)

week 12

Loyalty to the practice organization (Ch. 19.E; CB 492)

  • Lawyers owe their firms a duty of loyalty much like the fiduciary duty owed to clients, and owe each other a duty to be forthright and honest. No exploiting your firm position for personal benefit beyond the firm's regular rewards, interfering with the firm's business, or appropriating practice opportunities that belong to the firm (Meinhard v. Salmon, Part IV).
  • Violations can bring discipline, civil liability, and, very likely, employment consequences.

Changing jobs (CB 494)

  • You keep your duties to every client you served. If you leave current clients behind, you have all the duties of a withdrawing lawyer: notice, a smooth transition, and tribunal permission where required (MR 1.16(c)–(d)). Once you leave, every client you served there is your former client for the rest of your career (MR 1.9), however many colleagues also worked for it.
  • Taking clients with you: both the departing lawyer and the firm must tell clients of material changes, including the departure of a lawyer with substantial responsibility for their matter (MR 1.4), and clients decide who will represent them. Despite the duty of loyalty to the firm, the departing lawyer may generally tell clients of the impending departure and their right to choose counsel. Timing and content vary by jurisdiction and are complex.
  • Conflicts follow the mobile lawyer only as far as actual knowledge goes (MR 1.9(b)), and screens may protect the new firm (MR 1.10(a)(2); Part VI). MR 1.6(b)(7) permits limited disclosures to detect conflicts.
  • Noncompetes are barred by MR 5.6(a), except retirement benefits (Part XI).
rule

MR 1.17 — sale of a law practice

A lawyer or firm may sell or buy a law practice, or an area of practice, including good will, if all required:
(a) the seller ceases to engage in the private practice of law, or in the area of practice sold, in the geographic area or the jurisdiction (a state chooses which) where the practice was conducted;
(b) the entire practice, or the entire area of practice, is sold to one or more lawyers or firms;
(c) the seller gives each client written notice of
(1) the proposed sale,
(2) the client's right to retain other counsel or take possession of the file, and
(3) the fact that consent to the transfer of the file will be presumed if the client does not act or object within 90 days of receiving notice (a client who can't be given notice may be transferred only by court order, with in camera disclosure limited to what's necessary); and (d) the fees charged clients are not increased by reason of the sale.

  • Why "entire": so less lucrative clients aren't left stranded while the profitable matters are sold (cmt [6]). Good-faith offering of the whole practice satisfies it even if some clients choose other counsel (cmt [2]).
  • Ceasing practice doesn't bar taking a government, legal services, or in-house job (cmt [3]). The purchaser must honor existing fee and scope arrangements (cmt [10]); client autonomy, including the right to discharge, survives the sale (cmt [9]).
  • Payments: the purchase price of a deceased, disabled, or disappeared lawyer's practice may be paid to the estate (MR 5.4(a)(2)), and paying for a practice is an exception to the ban on paying for referrals (MR 7.2(b)(3)). Restrictions in a practice-sale agreement don't violate MR 5.6 (cmt [3] to MR 5.6).
rule

MR 5.7 — law-related services

(a) A lawyer is subject to the Rules when providing law-related services any one suffices: (1) by the lawyer in circumstances not distinct from the lawyer's legal services to clients; or (2) by an entity the lawyer controls, alone or with others, if the lawyer fails to take reasonable measures to assure that a person obtaining the services knows they are not legal services and that the protections of the client-lawyer relationship do not exist. (b) "Law-related services" are services that might reasonably be performed in conjunction with, and in substance are related to, legal services, and that are not prohibited as UPL when provided by a nonlawyer.

  • Examples: title insurance, financial planning, accounting, lobbying, tax preparation, real estate consulting. The burden is on the lawyer to show reasonable measures were taken, and sophisticated users may need less explanation (cmts [6]–[7]). Referring a client to the lawyer's own law-related business is a MR 1.8(a) transaction (cmt [5]).

37. Representing Organizations and Their Constituents (MR 1.13)

week 12

Ch. 23. Ron Rainmaker's longtime client Ted Tech, a serial entrepreneur, illustrates how easily the lines blur between a founder and the companies he forms.

rule

MR 1.13(a), (f), (g)

(a) A lawyer employed or retained by an organization represents the organization acting through its duly authorized constituents. (f) In dealing with directors, officers, employees, members, shareholders, or other constituents, a lawyer shall explain the identity of the client when the lawyer knows or reasonably should know that the organization's interests are adverse to those of the constituents. (g) A lawyer representing an organization may also represent any of its constituents, subject to MR 1.7. If the organization's consent is required, it must be given by an appropriate official other than the individual to be represented, or by the shareholders.

Knowing who the client is (Ch. 23.A; CB 706–711)

  • Duties run exclusively to the organization, not to any constituent (CB 706).
  • Confidentiality and privilege belong to the organization (CB 708). Communications with constituents acting for it are confidential and can be privileged, but the organization alone holds and controls the privilege. You may disclose its information to a constituent (or anyone) only as the organization, through an authorized constituent, permits (MR 1.13 cmt [2]).
  • Dual representation is allowed (MR 1.13(g); CB 709), if you address any conflicts it creates, concurrent or successive. The organization's consent must come from an official other than the constituent being represented, or from the shareholders, so it is independent and informed (CB 711).
  • Organizational formation (CB 711): be clear, in your own mind and with everyone involved, about who your clients are and who they aren't. Founders often assume the lawyer represents them all (Problem 9A-2, Part II).
note case · week 12

Upjohn Co. v. United States, 449 U.S. 383 (1981) — the corporate privilege and the "Upjohn warning"

Facts: Upjohn's general counsel investigated possibly illegal payments to foreign officials by sending questionnaires to managers and interviewing employees. The IRS summoned the questionnaires and interview notes.

Held: The attorney-client privilege covered communications from employees at all levels, made at the direction of superiors so counsel could advise the corporation, about matters within their duties; the Court rejected the narrow "control group" test. The notes were also protected work product.

Why it matters: The privilege belongs to the corporation, not the employee, so counsel who interviews employees must warn them that counsel represents only the company and that the company may waive the privilege and share what they say. That warning bears the case's name.

Non-client constituents: the Upjohn warning (CB 717–722)

  • MR 4.3 applies to unrepresented constituents all required:
    (1) don't state or imply that you're disinterested;
    (2) correct any misunderstanding of your role that you know or reasonably should know of; and
    (3) when their interests conflict or may conflict with the organization's, give no legal advice except to get a lawyer.
  • MR 1.13(f) adds a duty to speak up whenever you know or reasonably should know the organization's interests are adverse to the constituent's, even if the constituent shows no confusion (CB 718).
  • Three typical divergences (CB 719; any one triggers concern): the constituent
    (1) is harming the company for personal benefit (embezzlement, theft);
    (2) is causing the company to violate the law (dangerous products, environmental harm, regulatory violations, fraud, antitrust); or
    (3) has personal difficulties (depression, addiction, illness) that interfere with the job.
  • What the warning says (CB 720): tailored to the circumstances, but always that you represent the company only, not the constituent, plus the implications of that role that may cut against the constituent: the conversation may be privileged, but the privilege is the company's, which may waive it and share what the constituent says, including with the government.
  • When (CB 721): at the start of any conversation where you expect the constituent may be engaged in conduct contrary to the organization's interests, because you then already reasonably should know of the adversity. If a surprise comes up mid-conversation, stop and clarify your role at that moment.
  • Form: no formal requirements, so an oral warning is allowed, but in an investigation give it in writing and let the constituent review it and ask questions first.
  • Consequences of a late or missing warning (CB 721): discipline (MR 1.13(f), 4.3); an implied duty of confidentiality to the constituent that conflicts with your duties to tell the company, forcing withdrawal or disqualification (potentially of the whole in-house department); damages if you disclose; exclusion of improperly obtained information in later litigation; and undoing decisions the constituent made on your forbidden advice.

Corporate civil wars (CB 723)

  • First ask whether you have a conflict. If you represent only the organization, competing instructions from warring factions don't create one: you have a single client. If you also represent a warring constituent (in anything), or have personal ties strong enough to create a significant risk of material limitation, you do.
  • Be transparent with every faction that has a colorable claim to direct the company, limit yourself to issues proper for the organization's lawyer (not business advisor or faction counsel), and keep asking whether the organization would be no worse off if you withdrew.

Reporting up and out (Ch. 23.B)

rule

MR 1.13(b) — reporting up

If a lawyer for an organization knows that an officer, employee, or other associated person is engaged in action, intends to act, or refuses to act in a matter related to the representation that is (1) a violation of a legal obligation to the organization, or a violation of law that reasonably might be imputed to it, and (2) likely to result in substantial injury to the organization, then the lawyer shall proceed as is reasonably necessary in the organization's best interest. Unless the lawyer reasonably believes it isn't necessary, the lawyer shall refer the matter to higher authority, including, if warranted, the highest authority that can act for the organization under applicable law.

  • Level by level (CB 726): report to the constituent's boss, then the next level, up to the board if warranted. A rogue officer's demand for silence doesn't control.
  • Failing to report up is disciplinable and, if it harms the organization, actionable as malpractice or breach of fiduciary duty, and the damages can be substantial (CB 729).
rule

MR 1.13(c)–(e) — reporting out

(c) Except as provided in (d), if all required: (1) despite the lawyer's efforts under (b), the highest authority that can act for the organization insists on or fails to address in a timely and appropriate manner an action or refusal to act that is clearly a violation of law; and (2) the lawyer reasonably believes the violation is reasonably certain to result in substantial injury to the organization, then the lawyer may reveal information relating to the representation, whether or not MR 1.6 permits it, but only if and to the extent the lawyer reasonably believes necessary to prevent substantial injury to the organization. (d) Paragraph (c) doesn't apply to information relating to the lawyer's representation of the organization (either is excluded) (1) to investigate an alleged violation of law, or (2) to defend the organization or a constituent against a claim arising out of an alleged violation of law. (e) A lawyer who reasonably believes she was discharged because of actions under (b) or (c), or who withdraws in circumstances requiring or permitting such action, shall proceed as reasonably necessary to assure that the highest authority is informed of the discharge or withdrawal.

  • Minimum disclosure (CB 732): report out as little, to as few, as will address the problem the organization refused to fix. Overreporting destroys the exception and invites discipline and liability.
  • Compared with MR 1.6(b)(2)–(3): MR 1.13(c) doesn't require that the lawyer's services were used, but it protects only against injury to the organization, and only after reporting up all the way.
  • In-house counsel who must withdraw may have to quit (Part II); MR 1.13(e) makes sure the board hears why.

Part X — Public Service

38. Pro Bono, Appointments, and Public-Service Organizations (MR 6.1–6.5)

week 13

Reading: MR 6.1–6.5 with their Comments. The casebook has no chapter on these Rules, so this section follows the Rules and Comments.

rule

MR 6.1 — voluntary pro bono publico service

Every lawyer has a professional responsibility to provide legal services to those unable to pay, and should aspire to render at least 50 hours of pro bono publico service a year. In doing so, the lawyer should: (a) provide a substantial majority of the 50 hours without fee or expectation of fee to (1) persons of limited means, or (2) charitable, religious, civic, community, governmental, and educational organizations in matters designed primarily to address the needs of persons of limited means; and (b) provide any additional services through
(1) free or substantially reduced-fee services to individuals, groups, or organizations seeking to secure or protect civil rights, civil liberties, or public rights, or to charitable, religious, civic, community, governmental, and educational organizations in matters furthering their organizational purposes, where standard fees would significantly deplete their resources or be otherwise inappropriate;
(2) substantially reduced-fee services to persons of limited means; or
(3) activities improving the law, the legal system, or the legal profession. A lawyer should also voluntarily contribute financial support to organizations that provide legal services to persons of limited means.

  • Aspirational, not enforceable: "should," and the responsibility "is not intended to be enforced through disciplinary process" (cmt [12]). It's an MPRE favorite precisely because it's the one Rule with no discipline behind it.
  • Intent matters (cmt [4]): services count only if rendered without fee or expectation of fee, so an uncollected fee isn't pro bono; but an award of statutory attorney's fees in a case originally accepted pro bono doesn't disqualify it (lawyers who receive such fees are encouraged to contribute a portion to organizations serving persons of limited means).
  • Who qualifies (cmt [3]): persons eligible for Legal Services Corporation programs, and those slightly above the guidelines who still can't afford counsel. (b)(1) examples (cmt [6]): First Amendment claims, Title VII claims, environmental protection claims. (b)(2) (cmt [7]): judicare programs and court appointments paid well below the lawyer's usual rate. (b)(3) (cmt [8]): bar committees, legal services boards, Law Day, CLE teaching, mediation, and arbitration.
  • When pro bono isn't feasible (cmt [9]), a lawyer may discharge the responsibility by financial support; firms should enable and encourage their lawyers' pro bono work (cmt [11]). Government and public-sector lawyers who are barred from outside practice may meet it through (b) activities (cmt [5]).
rule

MR 6.2 — accepting appointments

A lawyer shall not seek to avoid appointment by a tribunal to represent a person except for good cause, such as any one suffices:
(a) representing the client is likely to result in violation of the Rules or other law;
(b) representing the client is likely to result in an unreasonable financial burden on the lawyer; or
(c) the client or the cause is so repugnant to the lawyer as to be likely to impair the client-lawyer relationship or the lawyer's ability to represent the client.

  • Good cause, not distaste (cmts [1]–[2]): a lawyer is ordinarily free to decline a client whose character or cause is repugnant, but an appointment is different. Good cause exists if the lawyer couldn't handle the matter competently (MR 1.1), if an improper conflict would result, or if the case would be unjustly burdensome; repugnance must be strong enough to impair the representation.
  • Appointed counsel owes everything retained counsel owes (cmt [3]): loyalty, confidentiality, and the same limits (no assisting violations of the Rules). Appointment itself creates the relationship (Restatement § 14; Part II), and the court controls withdrawal and substitution (MR 1.16(c), cmt [5]).
rule

MR 6.3 — membership in a legal services organization

A lawyer may serve as a director, officer, or member of a legal services organization, apart from the lawyer's firm, even though it serves persons with interests adverse to the lawyer's clients. The lawyer shall not knowingly participate in a decision or action of the organization (either is barred): (a) if participating would be incompatible with the lawyer's obligations to a client under MR 1.7; or (b) where the decision or action could have a material adverse effect on the representation of a client of the organization whose interests are adverse to a client of the lawyer.

  • Board service doesn't make the organization's clients the lawyer's clients (cmt [1]), but the lawyer must recuse from decisions that would hurt her own clients or the organization's clients adverse to them.
rule

MR 6.4 — law reform activities affecting client interests

A lawyer may serve as a director, officer, or member of an organization involved in reform of the law or its administration even though the reform may affect a client's interests. When the lawyer knows that a client's interests may be materially benefitted by a decision in which the lawyer participates, the lawyer shall disclose that fact, but need not identify the client.

  • Lawyers in law reform organizations generally don't represent the organization (cmt [1]); otherwise an antitrust litigator might be disqualified from helping revise the antitrust rules. Compare MR 1.2(b): representing a client isn't an endorsement.
rule

MR 6.5 — nonprofit and court-annexed limited legal services programs

(a) A lawyer who, under the auspices of a program sponsored by a nonprofit organization or court, provides short-term limited legal services to a client without expectation by either that the lawyer will provide continuing representation in the matter: (1) is subject to MR 1.7 and 1.9(a) only if the lawyer knows the representation involves a conflict of interest; and (2) is subject to MR 1.10 only if the lawyer knows another lawyer in the lawyer's firm is disqualified by MR 1.7 or 1.9(a) in the matter. (b) Except as provided in (a)(2), MR 1.10 is inapplicable to a representation governed by this Rule.

  • Why relax conflicts (cmts [1], [3]–[4]): hotlines, advice-only clinics, and court help desks can't run systematic conflict checks, and the limited nature of the service reduces the risk. The other Rules, including MR 1.6 and 1.9(c), still apply.
  • Limited scope still needs informed consent (MR 1.2(c); cmt [2]). If a short-term limited representation wouldn't be reasonable, the lawyer may give advice but must also advise the client of the need for further assistance.
  • If the lawyer goes on to represent the client on an ongoing basis, MR 1.7, 1.9(a), and 1.10 apply in full (cmt [5]).
tip

Public-service rules on the exam

MR 6.3 and 6.4 permit board service with recusal (6.3) or disclosure without naming the client (6.4). 39. Truthfulness in Statements to Others (MR 4.1)

week 14

Reading: MR 4.1–4.4, 7.1–7.3, 7.6; Chs. 10–14, 16–18; Zauderer v. Office of Disciplinary Counsel (1985). The Thanksgiving break fell this week (Nov. 25–29).

rule

MR 4.1

In the course of representing a client, a lawyer shall not knowingly: (a) make a false statement of material fact or law to a third person; or (b) fail to disclose a material fact when disclosure is necessary to avoid assisting a criminal or fraudulent act by a client, unless disclosure is prohibited by MR 1.6.

  • Scope (CB 310): statements by a lawyer (or on a lawyer's behalf), to a third person (not a client; MR 1.4 protects clients), in the course of representing a client. Consequences beyond discipline: tort or criminal liability and rescission of any agreement the misrepresentation procured.

What is a "false statement of material fact" (Ch. 10.B; CB 312–316)

  • The elements all required:
    (1) a statement, by any means (oral, written, by context), including adopting another's statement you know is false;
    (2) material;
    (3) factual, meaning provably true or false, not opinion or position;
    (4) false: if a reasonable recipient would be misled into understanding it as a statement of a fact the speaker knows is false, it's a misrepresentation (CB 314); and
    (5) made knowingly.
  • Negotiation (CB 315; MR 4.1 cmt [2]): by convention, some statements aren't taken as statements of fact: estimates of price or value, a party's intentions as to an acceptable settlement ("my client won't take less than $500K"), and the existence of an undisclosed principal (unless nondisclosure would be fraud). This "puffing" or "posturing" is fine; "we have another offer at $500K," if false, isn't.
  • Knowledge (CB 316): negligent ("should have known") or innocent misstatements can't support discipline under MR 4.1(a), but knowing or negligent misrepresentation can support tort liability, and even an innocent one can support rescission or a contract defense.
  • Half-truths and strategic ambiguity violate MR 4.1(a) (CB 316): once you speak, don't leave a misleading impression.

Failure to disclose: MR 4.1(b) (Ch. 10.C; CB 316–320)

  • You must disclose an undisclosed material fact in only two situations any one suffices: (1) you said something that makes the omission misleading (MR 4.1(a)); or (2) disclosure is necessary to avoid assisting a client's crime or fraud (MR 4.1(b)).
  • The 4.1(b) twist (CB 320): if the client won't allow the disclosure, withdraw (with leave where required), and you must make it unless MR 1.6 prohibits it. Because a permissive exception (MR 1.6(b)(2)–(3)) means MR 1.6 doesn't prohibit disclosure, MR 4.1(b) turns that "may" into a "must."
  • Problem 10 setup: Maya, assisting partner Ron Rainmaker on an acquisition, learns facts the other side doesn't know. Silence is generally fine; affirmative misstatements, misleading half-truths, and silence that assists the client's fraud are not.

40. Represented Persons: The No-Contact Rule (MR 4.2)

week 14
rule

MR 4.2

In representing a client, a lawyer shall not communicate about the subject of the representation with a person the lawyer knows to be represented by another lawyer in the matter, unless the lawyer has the consent of the other lawyer or is authorized to do so by law or a court order.

  • Know which world you're in (CB 327): represented (MR 4.2) or unrepresented (MR 4.3).
  • Who must follow it (CB 329): "a lawyer," including people acting at the lawyer's direction or on the lawyer's behalf (investigators, paralegals; MR 8.4(a), 5.3). It applies even if the represented person starts the conversation or consents (cmt [3]); only the lawyer can consent.
  • Who is protected (CB 331–336): a person who has retained counsel in the matter, and, when an organization is represented in the matter, three categories of constituents (MR 4.2 cmt [7]; any one suffices):
    (1) a constituent who supervises, directs, or regularly consults with the organization's lawyer about the matter ("Upjohn management");
    (2) one who has authority to obligate the organization about the matter; or
    (3) one whose act or omission in the matter may be imputed to the organization for civil or criminal liability. Former constituents aren't protected, and a constituent with personal counsel needs only that counsel's consent. Older versions and state variations differ, so check your jurisdiction (CB 334).
  • "In the matter": an organization with an in-house legal department isn't necessarily "represented in the matter" you're investigating (Problem 11B; see Jorgenson v. Taco Bell). United States v. Talao (9th Cir. 2000): a protected employee who wants to talk without company counsel present, to report the employer's wrongdoing, presents a different case.
  • Knowledge (CB 338): actual knowledge, but willful blindness counts the same (Global-Tech Appliances v. SEB; cmt [8]). If you're unsure, ask; the better practice is to ask a pro se litigant who may be ghost-advised (ABA Op. 15-472). A witness who honestly says "no lawyers" when an in-house lawyer has secretly been involved doesn't put you in violation.
  • Communication (CB 339): investigation without communicating (watching in public, reading public posts) doesn't violate the Rule (Problems 11D-2, 11D-3). Getting behind privacy settings with a "friend" request, or through someone else's account, raises the communication question (see In re Robertelli, N.J. 2020).

Permitted communications (CB 341–345; any one suffices)

1. With opposing counsel's consent (CB 341). Get it clearly, preferably in writing.

2. Not about the subject of the representation: chatting about the weather or an unrelated matter (CB 342).

3. As a party: a lawyer who is a party may talk to the other party, and parties may always talk to each other directly; the lawyer may advise the client about such talks (cmt [4]).

4. With someone you also represent or are asked to represent, e.g., a person seeking a second opinion (CB 343; cmt [4]).

5. Authorized by law or court order (CB 344): statutory pre-filing investigations by government lawyers (subject to the McDade Amendment), communications with represented government agencies or officials on policy issues, and court-approved notices in class actions.

Consequences (Ch. 11.G; CB 346)

  • Discipline (see In re Howes, N.M. 1997). Information-protective remedies: disqualification, exclusion of the improperly obtained evidence, and monetary sanctions. Decision-protective remedies: rescission of agreements and relief from judgments or orders obtained through the forbidden contact.

41. Unrepresented Persons (MR 4.3, 3.4(f))

week 14
rule

MR 4.3 and 3.4(f) — four things you must or must not do (CB 355)


(1) You must not state or imply that you are disinterested.
(2) When you know or reasonably should know the unrepresented person misunderstands your role, for whatever reason, you must make reasonable efforts to correct it.
(3) If the person's interests are or have a reasonable possibility of being in conflict with your client's, you must not give legal advice other than the advice to secure counsel.
(4) You must not ask a non-client to refrain from voluntarily giving relevant information to another party, unless the person is the client's relative, employee, or agent and you reasonably believe the person's interests won't be adversely affected (MR 3.4(f)).

  • What you may do: answer factual questions, give information, negotiate, and advocate for your client, truthfully, as long as none of it is dressed up as advice to the unrepresented person (CB 363). The line between "legal advice" and a statement of fact or position often turns on context and word choice: "the law requires you to sign this" is advice; "my client won't settle unless you sign this" is position.
  • "Reasonable possibility" of conflict and "knows or reasonably should know" set a low threshold (CB 598–599); when in doubt, assume the interests conflict.
  • Organizational constituents: company counsel must remind constituents that counsel represents only the company whenever counsel knows the company's interests are adverse to theirs (MR 1.13(f); Upjohn warning, Part IX).
  • Consequences (CB 365): discipline, and the same civil remedies as for no-contact violations: disqualification, evidentiary and monetary sanctions, rescission of agreements unfairly induced, and FRCP 60(b) relief.

42. Involvement in a Client's Wrongdoing (MR 1.2(d))

week 14
rule

MR 1.2(d)

A lawyer shall not counsel a client to engage, or assist a client, in conduct the lawyer knows is criminal or fraudulent, but may discuss the legal consequences of any proposed course of conduct and may counsel or assist a client to make a good-faith effort to determine the validity, scope, meaning, or application of the law.

  • Knowledge (CB 372): actual knowledge, which may be inferred from circumstances, including willful blindness (conscious avoidance): failing to learn what you consciously suspected and could have discovered with reasonable effort.
  • Compliance advice vs. evasion advice: advising how close a client can get to a legal line without crossing it is proper; advising how not to get caught, or how likely enforcement is, is counseling the client to break the law.
  • Testing the law (CB 374): advice about testing a statute or regulation by disobeying it can be proper (MR 1.2 cmt [12]). Never counsel disobeying a court order, even one you think is unconstitutional: that isn't a defense to contempt (Walker v. City of Birmingham).

A client proposes, persists in, or may be doing something unlawful (CB 374–379)

Situation

What you must, may, and should do

Client proposes something unlawful

Must not counsel or assist; may, should, and likely must explain the consequences (MR 1.2(d), 1.4(a)(5)); may give extra-legal advice about whether it's wise (MR 2.1). Never keep the client "happy" by blessing unlawful conduct.

Client persists without telling you, or despite your advice

Silence doesn't absolve you: the Comments suggest you must speak up. If the client keeps using your services in the unlawful conduct after the MR 1.4(a)(5) talk, you must decline or withdraw (MR 1.16(a)(4), 2023).

Client persists but isn't using your services

You may, and generally should, withdraw (MR 1.16(b)(1), (4), cmt [7]), subject to tribunal permission.

Client used your services in past unlawful conduct

You may, and generally should, withdraw (MR 1.16(b)(2)–(3)); you must if the client intends to keep using your services or your work implicates you (MR 1.16(a)(1), (4); MR 1.2 cmt [10]). Consider a noisy withdrawal (Part II).

You suspect but don't know

The 2023 MR 1.16(a) amendment creates a duty to inquire into and assess the facts before and during every representation, scaled to the circumstances (not yet adopted anywhere as of the casebook). Whether or not required, you should investigate to confirm or allay suspicions and manage your own risk.

  • Vicarious liability (CB 380): willful blindness or assistance can make you criminally liable as an aider and abettor (18 U.S.C. § 2) or conspirator (MPC § 5.03), and civilly liable to third parties under standards that vary state to state.

43. Abusive Tactics, Threats, and Reporting Misconduct (MR 4.4(a), 8.3)

week 14

Embarrassing, delaying, or burdening others: MR 4.4(a) (Ch. 14; CB 393)

rule

MR 4.4(a)

In representing a client, a lawyer shall not use means that have no substantial purpose other than to embarrass, delay, or burden a third person, or use methods of obtaining evidence that violate the legal rights of such a person.

  • The elements all required:
    (1) "in representing a client" (not grocery shopping);
    (2) a "third person," meaning anyone but your client (fiduciary duties already require better behavior toward clients);
    (3) "means," actual speech or conduct, which violate the Rule when undertaken with the bad purpose, even if they don't succeed; and
    (4) "no substantial purpose other than" to embarrass, delay, or burden. Some embarrassment and burden are unavoidable; the Rule reaches only what isn't reasonably necessary.
  • Contrast MR 8.4(g), which reaches harassment or discrimination on a protected basis in any conduct related to the practice of law, whether or not you're representing a client (Part XII).
  • Overlap (CB 396): most conduct violating MR 4.4(a) violates other Rules too, and supports court sanctions (FRCP 11, 37; § 1927) and sometimes tort liability.
  • The First Amendment (CB 396): no formal consequences may rest on protected speech, but the case law is confused and courts read lawyers' speech rights narrowly (Gentile; Part VIII). Example: United States v. Wunsch (9th Cir. 1996) held a local rule against "offensive personality" too vague to punish a lawyer's sexist letter to a prosecutor.

Threats (Ch. 16.A; CB 415–420)

  • The old Model Code rule (DR 7-105(A)), still followed in a significant minority of states, barred threatening criminal charges solely to gain advantage in a civil matter. The Model Rules dropped it as redundant of more general prohibitions.
  • ABA Op. 92-363 (CB 417): under the Model Rules, a lawyer may threaten to bring, or agree not to bring, criminal or quasi-criminal charges to gain advantage in a civil dispute if all required:
    (1) the charge is related to the civil claim;
    (2) the lawyer has a well-founded belief that both the civil claim and the criminal charges are warranted by law and fact; and
    (3) the lawyer doesn't attempt to exert or suggest improper influence over the criminal process.
  • Never okay, anywhere (CB 418; any one is extortionate): threats
    (1) to make charges unrelated to, or to disclose embarrassing facts not genuinely relevant to, the civil dispute;
    (2) to make charges with no substantial basis in fact or law (MR 3.1, 8.4(d));
    (3) to engage in lawful conduct you don't actually intend to pursue (MR 4.1(a));
    (4) based on improperly influencing officials, or suggesting you can (MR 8.4(e)); or
    (5) to commit violence or other illegal acts. These are torts and potentially crimes even when the threatened disclosures are true, and violate MR 4.4(a) and often 8.4(b).
  • FRE 408 is no shield: an extortionate threat couched as a settlement demand is admissible to prove the threat itself (FRE 408(b)), and the victim can always report it.
  • Practical tiers (CB 419):
    (1) threatening civil claims your client colorably has is the essence of a demand letter;
    (2) threatening to report opposing counsel's ethics violation to gain an advantage: don't, since in MR 8.3 states you must report it anyway, so it can't be a bargaining chip; and
    (3) anything else, think twice: the line between a proper threat and extortion can be razor-thin.
  • Presenting charges without threatening first (CB 420): under the majority view, you may present a charge you could properly threaten, but not one you couldn't.
note case · week 14

Flatley v. Mauro, 39 Cal. 4th 299 (2006) — extortion dressed as a settlement demand

Facts: A lawyer demanded a seven-figure "settlement" from Michael Flatley (of Riverdance) to resolve a sexual assault claim, warning that otherwise an investigation would expose immigration, Social Security, and tax matters to "any and all appropriate authorities," and that press releases would go to media worldwide.

Held: The demand was criminal extortion as a matter of law. Flatley sued for civil extortion and defamation; Mauro was suspended for a year in Illinois and paid Flatley $400,000.

Why it matters: The model of a forbidden threat: unrelated charges and disclosures used as leverage (CB 415, 421).

Reporting another lawyer's misconduct: MR 8.3 (Ch. 16.D; CB 422)

  • The elements (all required for the duty):
    (1) you know (actual knowledge or willful blindness);
    (2) another lawyer (any other lawyer, including opposing counsel);
    (3) committed a violation of the Rules that raises a substantial question as to that lawyer's honesty, trustworthiness, or fitness as a lawyer in other respects; then
    (4) you shall inform the appropriate professional authority (the disciplinary agency, not the lawyer's boss).
  • Exceptions any one suffices: (1) information protected by MR 1.6 (confidentiality trumps reporting unless an exception or consent applies); or (2) information gained while participating in an approved lawyers assistance program.
  • MR 8.3(b) imposes the same duty for judges' serious violations of the judicial conduct rules.
  • Chapter 16 review Q3–Q4: the client's no-objection removes the confidentiality bar, so knowing of opposing counsel's extortionate threat that raises a substantial question about his honesty and doing nothing violates MR 8.3. In re Riehlmann (La. 2005): a lawyer who waited years to report a friend's confession that, as a prosecutor, he had suppressed exculpatory evidence was disciplined.

44. Restricting Future Practice (MR 5.6)

week 14

Ch. 17 (MR 5.6 was assigned in Week 12).

rule

MR 5.6

A lawyer shall not participate in offering or making (either is barred): (a) a partnership, shareholders, operating, employment, or other similar agreement that restricts a lawyer's right to practice after the relationship ends, except an agreement concerning benefits upon retirement; or (b) an agreement in which a restriction on the lawyer's right to practice is part of the settlement of a client controversy.

Settlement restrictions: MR 5.6(b) (Ch. 17.A; CB 703–708)

  • Elements:
    (1) "shall not" (mandatory);
    (2) "participate in offering or making," so defense counsel who proposes it and plaintiff's counsel who accepts it both violate;
    (3) an agreement that is part of the settlement of a client controversy;
    (4) containing a restriction on the lawyer's right to practice (e.g., "plaintiff's counsel will never sue H2Whoa again," or won't use what was learned in discovery against it in future cases).
  • Why it's forbidden (CB 705): it pits the lawyer's interests against the client's (a premium for the lawyer's promise), deprives future clients of the most knowledgeable counsel, and lets defendants buy off the lawyers who know where the bodies are. Frank and Happy's H2Whoa contamination case (Ch. 17 intro) is the setup.
  • Consequences (CB 708): discipline; breach of fiduciary duty and restitution of the lawyer's ill-gotten gains to the client; and the restriction is usually unenforceable as against public policy. Confidentiality terms that don't restrict practice are generally allowed, though increasingly controversial.

Employment and partnership restrictions: MR 5.6(a) (Ch. 17.B; CB 710–713)

  • Bars covenants not to compete for clients after leaving a firm, often limited by time, place, or practice area, because clients must be free to choose counsel (cmt [1]).
  • Exceptions any one suffices: (1) restrictions tied to retirement benefits; and (2) restrictions in the sale of a law practice under MR 1.17 (MR 5.6 cmt [3]).
  • Consequences: discipline, and the restriction is voidable as against public policy, with amounts paid or withheld recoverable as unjust enrichment. Courts split on financial disincentives: compare Howard v. Babcock (Cal. 1993) (a reasonable cost imposed on departing partners who compete may be enforced) with Moskowitz v. Jacobson Holman (E.D. Va. 2015). Clawbacks of bar-exam or moving expenses from short-tenure hires are a different, rarer issue.

45. Advertising and Solicitation (MR 7.1–7.3, 7.6)

week 14

The constitutional frame (Ch. 18.A; CB 448)

  • Bates v. State Bar of Arizona (1977): truthful lawyer advertising is commercial speech protected by the First Amendment. Regulation gets intermediate scrutiny: the state must prove the rule all required
    (1) serves a substantial state interest;
    (2) directly advances it; and
    (3) is no more extensive than necessary (Central Hudson).
  • Antitrust may also limit a self-regulating bar's anticompetitive rules (North Carolina State Board of Dental Examiners v. FTC; Goldfarb, Part V).
  • States still regulate aggressively, and some rules are unconstitutional in part. Florida disciplined Sylvia Ibanez for truthfully listing her CPA and CFP credentials until the Supreme Court reversed (Ibanez). Know your state's rules before you post anything, including a website, and decide whether a fight over an overbroad rule is worth it. Florida found a pit bull and "1-800-PIT-BULL" misleading (Florida Bar v. Pape).
note case · week 14

Bates v. State Bar of Arizona, 433 U.S. 350 (1977) — lawyer advertising is protected speech

Facts: Two lawyers running a low-cost "legal clinic" advertised routine services, such as uncontested divorces and simple bankruptcies, at stated fees, in violation of Arizona's ban on lawyer advertising.

Held: A blanket ban on truthful advertising of routine legal services and prices violates the First Amendment; states may still regulate false, deceptive, or misleading advertising and in-person solicitation.

Why it matters: The start of the modern regime: MR 7.1 targets only false or misleading communications.

main case

Zauderer v. Office of Disciplinary Counsel, 471 U.S. 626 (1985) — illustrated ads, legal advice in ads, and required disclosures

Source: assigned reading, Week 14 syllabus; casebook Ch. 18.B (CB 455 n.13, 458).

Facts: An Ohio lawyer ran a newspaper ad aimed at women injured by the Dalkon Shield IUD. It included a drawing of the device, said the lawyer was handling such cases, and stated that cases were taken on a contingent-fee basis and "if there is no recovery, no legal fees are owed by our clients." Ohio disciplined him for soliciting through the ad, giving legal advice in it, using an illustration, and omitting that clients might owe costs.

Held: (1) A state may not discipline a lawyer for truthful, nondeceptive printed advertising giving accurate information about potential clients' legal rights. (2) The ban on illustrations (Ohio's "dignity" rule) was unconstitutional: an accurate drawing isn't misleading and the state showed no substantial interest it advanced. (3) But the state may require disclosure that clients may be liable for significant litigation costs even if they lose; a disclosure requirement is valid if reasonably related to preventing deception and not unjustified or unduly burdensome, so the "no legal fees" ad without that disclosure was misleading.

Why it matters: The line between protected targeted advertising and regulable deception, and the deferential standard for compelled factual disclosures. It's why "no fee unless we win" ads must disclose costs (MR 7.1; Part V).

False or misleading communications: MR 7.1 (Ch. 18.B; CB 450–457)

rule

MR 7.1

A lawyer shall not make a false or misleading communication about the lawyer or the lawyer's services. A communication is false or misleading if it any one suffices: (1) contains a material misrepresentation of fact or law; or (2) omits a fact necessary to make the statement considered as a whole not materially misleading.

  • Every communication counts (CB 450): ads, websites, social media, business cards, letterhead, and firm names, made by or for the lawyer.
  • Categories states condemn, rightly or not (MR 7.1 cmt [3]; CB 733–737):
    (1) truthful reports of past results or testimonials that create unjustified expectations of similar results without reference to each case's facts ("he got me ONE MILLION DOLLARS, and he can do that for you, too!");
    (2) unsubstantiated comparisons with other lawyers' services or fees, if specific enough to suggest they can be substantiated ("the lowest cost for a will in Yoknapatawpha County"), as opposed to puffery ("don't settle for less than the best"); and
    (3) claims of expertise or specialization (MR 7.2(c)). Some states ban testimonials, dramatizations, or superlatives outright; such categorical bans are constitutionally vulnerable. Paid spokespersons usually need a visible disclaimer (CB 466).
  • Misleading by implication: a firm name implying a partnership that doesn't exist, or ads implying you can achieve results by means that violate the law (MR 8.4(e)).

Paying for recommendations: MR 7.2 (Ch. 18.C; CB 458–460)

  • MR 7.2(a): a lawyer may communicate information about the lawyer's services through any media.
  • MR 7.2(b) forbids giving anything of value for recommending the lawyer's services, with the five exceptions in Part V (advertising costs; legal service plans and not-for-profit or qualified referral services; buying a practice; non-exclusive, disclosed reciprocal referral agreements; nominal thank-you gifts). It bars "capping" and "running": paying EMTs, hospital staff, tow-truck drivers, funeral directors, or anyone else to steer clients, whether by cash, kickbacks per client, or payments to hand out cards.
  • MR 7.2(c) specialists: don't state or imply certification as a specialist unless all required (1) the lawyer is certified by an organization approved by a state authority or accredited by the ABA; and (2) the certifying organization is clearly named. Saying you "concentrate in" or "focus on" an area is fine if true.
  • MR 7.2(d): every communication must include the name and contact information of at least one lawyer or firm responsible for its content. Many states add filing, labeling, and record-keeping requirements.

Solicitation: MR 7.3 (Ch. 18.D; CB 461–464)

rule

MR 7.3

(a) "Solicitation" is a communication initiated by or for a lawyer, directed to a specific person the lawyer knows or reasonably should know needs legal services in a particular matter, that offers, or reasonably can be understood as offering, to provide legal services for that matter. (b) A lawyer shall not solicit professional employment by live person-to-person contact when a significant motive is the lawyer's or firm's pecuniary gain, unless the contact is with any one suffices:
(1) a lawyer;
(2) a person with a family, close personal, or prior business or professional relationship with the lawyer or firm; or
(3) a person who routinely uses for business purposes the type of legal services offered. (c) Even when (b) doesn't bar it, a lawyer shall not solicit if (any one bars it): (1) the target has made known a desire not to be solicited; or (2) the solicitation involves coercion, duress, or harassment. (d) The Rule doesn't prohibit communications authorized by law or ordered by a court. (e) A lawyer may participate in a prepaid or group legal service plan, operated by an organization the lawyer doesn't own or direct, that uses live contact to enroll members who aren't known to need legal services in a particular matter.

  • Why live contact is different (CB 461): a person who just learned of a criminal charge or a loved one's death or injury is distressed and vulnerable, less able to exercise independent judgment, and more subject to a trained advocate's undue influence. Live person-to-person means in person, face-to-face, live telephone, and real-time visual or auditory contact; not letters, email, texts, or chat-room messages the recipient can ignore (MR 7.3 cmt [2]).
  • Targeted letters and emails are generally allowed under MR 7.3, but remain subject to MR 7.1 and to MR 7.3(c). The Supreme Court protected targeted letters (Shapero v. Kentucky Bar Ass'n, 1988) but upheld a 30-day ban on letters to accident victims (Florida Bar v. Went For It, 1995); it upheld discipline for in-person solicitation for profit (Ohralik v. Ohio State Bar, 1978) but protected nonprofit civil-rights solicitation (In re Primus, 1978).
  • No pecuniary motive, no (b) problem: pro bono or nonprofit outreach isn't barred by MR 7.3(b), though (c) still applies.
  • A permissible solicitation can still be false or misleading (MR 7.1; CB 464).
  • Consequences (CB 464): discipline, and in some states unenforceable fee agreements, fee forfeiture, or criminal "barratry" or "runner" statutes.
rule

MR 7.6 — political contributions to obtain government work ("pay to play")

A lawyer or law firm shall not accept a government legal engagement or an appointment by a judge if the lawyer or firm makes a political contribution, or solicits political contributions, for the purpose of obtaining or being considered for that type of engagement or appointment.

  • Lawyers may participate fully in politics (cmt [1]). The Rule reaches only contributions made for the purpose of getting the work: contributions that wouldn't have been made but for the desire to be considered (cmt [5]). "Government legal engagement" means work a public official can award; "appointment by a judge" includes referees, special masters, receivers, guardians, and similar posts, but not substantially uncompensated services, engagements made on the merits after a request for proposals or other process free of political influence, or appointments made on a rotational basis from a list compiled without regard to contributions (cmt [3]). The ban covers PACs and entities the lawyer or firm controls (cmt [4]).

Part XII — Harassment, Discrimination, and Bias

46. Avoiding Invidious Discrimination (MR 8.4(g))

week 15

Reading: MR 8.4(g); Ch. 15. Taught during the make-up week (Nov. 30–Dec. 3).

  • Why the Rule exists (CB 404): good intentions get us only part of the way; people believe they are free of bias when they aren't. The ABA adopted MR 8.4(g) in 2016 to educate the bar and act against the invidious discrimination some colleagues still face. Goal B of the syllabus: a justice system that provides equal access and eliminates harassment, bias, discrimination, and racism in the law.
rule

MR 8.4(g)

It is professional misconduct to engage in conduct that the lawyer knows or reasonably should know is harassment or discrimination on the basis of race, sex, religion, national origin, ethnicity, disability, age, sexual orientation, gender identity, marital status, or socioeconomic status in conduct related to the practice of law. This paragraph does not limit the ability of a lawyer to accept, decline, or withdraw from a representation in accordance with MR 1.16. This paragraph does not preclude legitimate advice or advocacy consistent with these Rules.

The elements (Ch. 15.A; all required)

1. Harassment or discrimination (CB 405): defined by general anti-discrimination law, and by MR 8.4 cmt [3]: discrimination includes harmful verbal or physical conduct that manifests bias or prejudice; harassment includes sexual harassment and derogatory or demeaning verbal or physical conduct; sexual harassment includes unwelcome sexual advances, requests for sexual favors, and other unwelcome verbal or physical conduct of a sexual nature.

2. On the basis of a protected category (CB 405): only the eleven listed categories. Harassment that isn't based on one (pure rudeness) may still violate MR 4.4(a) or 8.4(d), but not (g).

3. Knows or reasonably should know (CB 406): an objective standard, not just actual knowledge. Not knowing your joke was demeaning is no defense if a reasonable lawyer would have known.

4. In conduct related to the practice of law (CB 406; cmt [4]): much wider than client work: representing clients; interacting with witnesses, coworkers, court personnel, lawyers, and others while engaged in the practice of law; operating or managing a firm; and participating in bar association, business, or social activities connected with the practice of law.

Limits (cmts [4]–[5]; CB 406)

  • (1) Accepting, declining, or withdrawing under MR 1.16 isn't limited: if MR 1.16 allows it, MR 8.4(g) probably doesn't forbid it (but other anti-discrimination law may; see Stropnicky).
  • (2) Legitimate advice or advocacy consistent with the Rules and the First Amendment rights of expression and petition is protected, even if others find the ideas discriminatory or hateful.
  • (3) Promoting diversity and inclusion: conduct to promote diversity, equity, and inclusion in the workplace or the bar, permitted by other law, doesn't violate the Rule.
  • (4) Other carve-outs: a trial judge's finding that peremptory challenges were exercised on a discriminatory basis (a Batson violation) doesn't alone establish a violation, though it has other consequences; limiting your practice by subject matter or to underserved populations is allowed; and charging reasonable, lawful fees isn't discrimination.

Why MR 8.4(g) is controversial (Ch. 15.B; CB 409)

  • Adoption is thin: only a few states adopted it verbatim, though about half have some prohibition on discriminatory conduct, usually from a Comment treating discrimination as conduct "prejudicial to the administration of justice" (MR 8.4(d)). Delaware's MR 8.4 has no paragraph (g) (Part I). Virtually every jurisdiction has general anti-discrimination and anti-harassment laws that also reach lawyers.
  • Free speech and vagueness challenges: a federal court enjoined Pennsylvania's version as vague and overbroad (Greenberg v. Haggerty, E.D. Pa. 2020), but the Third Circuit reversed for lack of standing without reaching the merits (Greenberg v. Lehocky, 3d Cir. 2023).
  • Other law already applies: Title VII covers professional partnerships (Price Waterhouse v. Hopkins), and a firm and its partner paid compensatory and punitive damages for the partner's serial harassment of subordinates (Weeks v. Baker & McKenzie, Cal. Ct. App. 1998). The Supreme Court's limits on race-conscious admissions (Students for Fair Admissions, 2023) bear on what "permitted by other law" means for diversity programs.
note case · week 15

Stropnicky v. Nathanson (Mass. Comm'n Against Discrimination 1997) — refusing clients because of sex

Facts: A divorce lawyer who represented only wives, as a matter of principle, refused to take a husband as a client.

Held: The Massachusetts Commission Against Discrimination found that refusing him because of his sex violated the state public-accommodations law.

Why it matters: MR 8.4(g) doesn't limit declining under MR 1.16, but general anti-discrimination law may still forbid refusing clients because of a protected trait. Contrast limiting a practice to underserved populations or a subject matter, which cmt [5] allows (the basis for a Ch. 15 problem).

trap

MR 8.4(g) vs. MR 4.4(a)

MR 4.4(a) needs a client representation and a "no substantial purpose other than to embarrass, delay, or burden" means, aimed at a third person. MR 8.4(g) needs a protected basis but reaches any conduct related to the practice of law (a firm party, a bar dinner, how you treat staff), and requires only that you reasonably should have known the conduct was harassment or discrimination.

Last-minute traps

  • Discipline needs a Rule; disqualification doesn't. Hot-potato violations, conflicts with non-clients owed confidentiality, and playbook disputes are fought through disqualification, not discipline.
  • "May" isn't "must." Every MR 1.6(b) exception is permissive, but MR 4.1(b), 3.3(c), and court orders can make disclosure mandatory.
