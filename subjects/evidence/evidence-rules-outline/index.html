Part I — Introduction And General Relevance

1. The Federal Rules and Jury Secrecy

  • What evidence law does (slides): it limits what information juries hear and how they may use it, for reasons of time, constitutional protection, truthful outcomes, public policy (privileges), and relevance and reliability.
  • The FRE are a statute, effective July 1, 1975, restyled in 2011 with no change in meaning. Read the text first, in light of legislative history, which "on occasion may even override an apparently plain and unmistakable meaning." Most courts give the Advisory Committee Notes weight (Cleary); Justice Scalia said they "bear no special authoritativeness" (Tome, concurring).
  • Rule 105: when evidence is admissible for one purpose or party but not another, the court, on timely request, must restrict it and instruct the jury.
rule

FRE 606(b)(1): in an inquiry into the validity of a verdict, a juror may not testify about
(1) any statement made or incident that occurred during deliberations;
(2) the effect of anything on any juror's vote; or
(3) any juror's mental processes concerning the verdict. No affidavit or evidence of a juror's statement on these matters may be received. 606(b)(2): a juror may testify about whether (A) extraneous prejudicial information was improperly brought to the jury's attention; (B) an outside influence was improperly brought to bear on any juror; or (C) a mistake was made in entering the verdict on the form.

  • Internal vs. external turns on the nature of the matter, not where it happened. Juror incompetence, intoxication, and bias are internal.
  • Recording or observing federal jury deliberations is a crime under 18 U.S.C. § 1508 (the Class 1 slide's "§ 1580" is a typo).
  • New trial for voir dire dishonesty (Warger, 574 U.S. 40 (2014)): show (1) the juror failed to answer honestly a material voir dire question and (2) a correct answer would have supported a challenge for cause. 606(b) still bars juror testimony about deliberations to prove it.
  • 606(b) and the jury right: drugs and alcohol are no more an "outside influence" than a virus, poorly prepared food, or a lack of sleep, and barring juror testimony about them does not violate the Sixth Amendment right to a competent jury. Voir dire, observation during trial, juror reports before the verdict, and non-juror evidence protect the right (Tanner, 1987). The rule serves four ends: protecting jurors from harassment, frank deliberation, finality, and public trust.
  • Racial-bias exception all required:
    (1) a juror made a clear statement showing reliance on racial stereotypes or animus;
    (2) in a criminal case; and
    (3) the animus was a significant motivating factor in the vote to convict. The Sixth Amendment then requires the no-impeachment rule to give way (Peña-Rodriguez v. Colorado, 2017). It is the only constitutional exception to 606(b); coded remarks in a civil case are a hard fit.
  • Juror experiences: a juror's own experiences are not "extraneous prejudicial information" under 606(b)(2)(A), and 606(b) bars juror testimony about deliberations even when offered to show that a juror lied during voir dire (Warger).

2. Relevance (FRE 401, 402, 104(b))

rule

FRE 401: evidence is relevant if (a) it has any tendency to make a fact more or less probable than it would be without the evidence (probativeness) and (b) the fact is of consequence in determining the action (materiality). FRE 402: relevant evidence is admissible unless the Constitution, a federal statute, the FRE, or other Supreme Court rules provide otherwise; irrelevant evidence is not admissible.

  • Minimal threshold, far below a preponderance: "A brick is not a wall" (ACN to 401). The fact need not be disputed (ACN to 401; relied on in Old Chief).
  • Materiality comes from the substantive law. A statute can make a probative fact immaterial (Montana v. Egelhoff: voluntary intoxication on mens rea). Ignorance is immaterial to a strict-liability offense (Wingate).
  • State the chain of inferences and test each link ("Show me the body"; the passports drawer; consenting to a polygraph). At trial, "irrelevant" covers both probativeness and materiality.
rule

FRE 104(b): when the relevance of evidence depends on whether a fact exists, proof must be introduced sufficient to support a finding that the fact exists. The court may admit the evidence on the condition that the proof be introduced later. Standard (Huddleston): whether a jury could reasonably find the conditional fact by a preponderance; the judge does not weigh credibility. Contrast 104(a): the judge herself decides admissibility questions (hearsay foundations, privilege, qualification) by a preponderance and is not bound by the evidence rules except privilege.

  • Nearly all relevance is conditional on something (slides: the weapon bought the day before is relevant unless it was a candlestick during a citywide blackout, or one of hundreds of hunting rifles sold in hunting season). "Sharp lawyers will identify the missing links."
tip

Who decides? 104(a): Preponderance, proponent's burden: the judge finds the fact by a preponderance and is not bound by the evidence rules, except privilege: a witness's qualification, privilege, hearsay foundations such as unavailability and the 801(d)(2)(E) conspiracy (Bourjaily), the 702 requirements, and the 1004 conditions. 104(b): Sufficient to support a finding, lower than a preponderance finding: the judge asks only whether a jury could reasonably find the fact by a preponderance, and the jury decides: other acts (Huddleston), personal knowledge (602), authentication (901), and the 1008 jury questions.

  • Probativeness through credibility: evidence the defendant never saw can still be relevant if it corroborates her testimony about what she was told, which bears on the reasonableness of her belief (United States v. James, 9th Cir. 1999 (en banc): records of the victim's past violence). Materiality turns on the elements of the claim or defense.
  • Knowledge as the conditional fact: when evidence is relevant only if the defendant knew something, 104(b) asks whether a jury could reasonably find that he knew; regular presence where the information circulated can suffice (Cox v. State, Ind. 1998).

3. Unfair Prejudice (FRE 403)

rule

FRE 403: the court may exclude relevant evidence if its probative value is substantially outweighed by a danger of unfair prejudice, confusing the issues, misleading the jury, undue delay, wasting time, or needlessly presenting cumulative evidence.

  • Pro-admission: exclusion only when the imbalance is substantial. Reviewed for abuse of discretion, then harmlessness.
  • Unfair prejudice is "an undue tendency to suggest decision on an improper basis, commonly, though not necessarily, an emotional one" (ACN), including the propensity inference (Old Chief) and overvaluation (Collins).
  • Factors weighed together:
    (1) whether the fact is actually disputed;
    (2) the availability of other means of proof (ACN);
    (3) the likely effectiveness of a limiting instruction.
  • Only exemption: crimen falsi convictions under 609(a)(2) must be admitted with no balancing.
  • Flight (slides; the Myers four-link chain):
    (1) behavior → flight;
    (2) flight → consciousness of guilt;
    (3) → consciousness of guilt of the crime charged;
    (4) → actual guilt. Each link can fail. Race may weaken link 2 (Gonzales Rose; Commonwealth v. Warren, Mass. 2016: flight by Black men in Boston "is not necessarily probative" of guilt). Explaining flight may force a defendant to reveal a prior record (Cutchall) or an uncharged crime (Johnson's marijuana).
  • Slides' 403 exercises: the Godfather Part II "Pentangeli" clip (mob associations versus Stone's deliberate allusion); Chauvin's computer animation (lifelike persuasion versus less dramatic than the real video; authentication may be the better objection); "spark-of-life" photos of a homicide victim (Minnesota allows them to humanize victims).
  • Undisputed facts: an undisputed fact lowers probative value on the 403 scale without making the evidence irrelevant. Gruesome photos are admissible when they bear on a disputed issue, but not when they have little tendency to prove one and are "introduced primarily to inflame the jury" (State v. Bocharski, Ariz. 2001).
  • Probability evidence: admissible only with a foundation in real data (as with DNA) and independent factors. Made-up numbers and interrelated variables, presented by an impressive expert the defense cannot refute, mislead the jury, and even an accurate figure would not show that this couple was guilty (People v. Collins, Cal. 1968).
  • Stipulations to status: when the only issue is a defendant's legal status (a prior felony), the name and nature of the offense add nothing but a propensity risk, so the court must accept the defendant's offered concession (Old Chief, 1997). Otherwise the prosecution may prove its case its own way, for narrative richness, a morally reasonable verdict, and jurors' expectations. The holding is confined to status elements.
tip

403 analysis:
(1) probative value on a disputed fact, discounted for concessions and alternatives;
(2) the specific danger;
(3) is it substantially outweighed?
(4) less prejudicial alternatives and a 105 instruction;
(5) abuse of discretion and harmlessness.

tip

Which scale? 403: Standard balancing, opponent's burden: excluded only if probative value is substantially outweighed, so it tilts toward admission. 609(a)(1)(B): Straight balancing, proponent's burden, ties exclude: a testifying criminal defendant's felony comes in only if probative value outweighs prejudicial effect to that defendant. Reverse 403, tilted against admission: the proponent's heavy burden: 609(b) (convictions over 10 years old); 412(b)(2) (civil rape-shield evidence: probative value must substantially outweigh harm to any victim and unfair prejudice to any party); 703 (disclosing otherwise-inadmissible bases to the jury). No balancing: 609(a)(2) (crimen falsi). 107(a): an illustrative aid gets the 403 scale, weighing its utility against the same dangers. Comparative necessity, not a prejudice balance: 807(a)(2) (more probative on the point than any other evidence reasonably obtainable).

Part II — The Specialized Relevance Rules (FRE 407–411)

4. Structure and Rationale

  • Each rule reflects a judgment that the evidence fails 403 as a matter of law and serves a public policy. 407, 408, 409, and 411 bar only certain purposes. 410 bars the evidence for all purposes against the defendant, with two narrow exceptions.

5. The Rules

rule

FRE 407: when measures are taken that would have made an earlier injury or harm less likely, evidence of the measures is not admissible to prove negligence, culpable conduct, a defect in a product or its design, or a need for a warning or instruction. The court may admit it for another purpose, such as impeachment or, if disputed, ownership, control, or the feasibility of precautions.

  • Timing is measured against the injury sued on (Wolf Attack: chaining the wolf after it attacked a beagle is barred in the beagle owner's suit but not in the suit by the child it later mauled).
  • Third-party repairs are generally admissible over 407 (the policy does not apply), though often weak under 403. "If disputed" modifies all three permitted purposes. Strict-liability cases are covered (1997).
  • Opening the door: a defendant who says the old design is still fine or a fix was impossible invites impeachment (Grotto Pizza's $55.26 repair; the wood-chipper defendant who touted identical machines in use).
rule

FRE 408(a): evidence of (1) furnishing, promising, or offering, or accepting or offering to accept, valuable consideration to compromise a claim, and (2) conduct or statements made during compromise negotiations about the claim, is not admissible on behalf of any party to prove or disprove the validity or amount of a disputed claim or to impeach by a prior inconsistent statement or contradiction. Exception: (a)(2) statements are admissible in a criminal case when the negotiations related to a claim by a public office exercising regulatory, investigative, or enforcement authority. 408(b): the court may admit the evidence for another purpose, such as a witness's bias or prejudice, negating a contention of undue delay, or proving obstruction.

  • Requires a claim that is disputed as to validity or amount. A report commissioned to support settlement talks counts as made "during" negotiations (Ramada v. Rauch: the Goldsmith report). Courts may apply 408 to settlements with third parties.
rule

FRE 409: furnishing, promising, or offering to pay medical, hospital, or similar expenses resulting from an injury is not admissible to prove liability. FRE 411: that a person was or was not insured against liability is not admissible to prove negligence or other wrongful conduct, but may be admitted for another purpose, such as bias or prejudice, or agency, ownership, or control.

  • 409 vs. 408 (slides): "Let me give you a bandaid" is barred; "I'm so sorry" is an admission that 409 does not cover. The same apology at a settlement meeting is barred by 408. Why the difference: an accident-scene apology is not deterred by an evidence rule the speaker has never heard of, while negotiations after a claim need broad protection.
rule

FRE 410(a): not admissible against the defendant who pleaded or negotiated:
(1) a guilty plea later withdrawn;
(2) a nolo plea;
(3) statements in a Rule 11 (or comparable state) proceeding on either;
(4) statements in plea discussions with an attorney for the prosecuting authority that did not result in a guilty plea or resulted in a withdrawn one. 410(b): statements under (a)(3) or (4) may be admitted (1) when another statement from the same plea or discussions has been introduced and in fairness they should be considered together, or (2) in a perjury or false-statement prosecution if made under oath, on the record, with counsel present.

  • No impeachment use (Congress removed it in 1975). Waivable (United States v. Mezzanatto, 1995: impeachment waivers valid; several circuits uphold case-in-chief waivers).
  • Covers discussions with prosecutors (or their designated agents), not police, though some courts protect a defendant's actual, reasonable expectation of negotiating (Robertson, 5th Cir. 1978).
  • The text bars only use "against the defendant," but courts generally also bar a defendant's use of the prosecutor's offer (Problem 2.8), since admitting it would chill offers.
  • A defendant's rejected offer: 410 protects the defendant, not the government, so a defendant may offer his refusal of immunity as evidence of innocence. Probative value depends on what was turned down: rejecting immunity strongly suggests innocence, while rejecting a plea to a lesser charge may be only a gamble (United States v. Biaggi, 2d Cir. 1990).
  • Problem 2.5, Claims Adjuster (Lacher v. Anderson): the insurer's adjuster who took the plaintiff's statement did not testify. Bias of a non-witness is a stretch. Slides: exclusion was right under 411 or, failing that, 403.
tip

Policy-rule steps:
(1) match the evidence to its rule (a repair, a compromise, payment of expenses, plea material, insurance);
(2) check the trigger (407: after the injury; 408: a claim disputed as to validity or amount; 410: a plea, or talks with a prosecutor);
(3) name the purpose: the barred use, or a permitted one actually in issue (407 ownership, control, and feasibility only if disputed; bias under 408 and 411);
(4) check who it is offered against (410 protects only the defendant who pleaded or negotiated; 408 applies on behalf of any party);
(5) then 403 and a 105 instruction.

Part III — Character Evidence (FRE 404–406, 413–415)

6. The Propensity Ban and Its Routes Around (FRE 404)

rule

FRE 404(a)(1): evidence of a person's character or character trait is not admissible to prove that on a particular occasion the person acted in accordance with it. 404(b)(1): evidence of any other crime, wrong, or act is not admissible to prove a person's character to show action in accordance with it. 404(b)(2): it may be admissible for another purpose, such as motive, opportunity, intent, preparation, plan, knowledge, identity, absence of mistake, or lack of accident. 404(b)(3): in a criminal case the prosecutor must give reasonable written notice before trial (or during trial for good cause), articulating the permitted purpose and the reasoning that supports it.

  • Applies in civil and criminal cases. Propensity evidence is relevant but barred for policy reasons: jurors overweight it and may punish a bad person regardless of guilt (slides).
  • The 404(b) purposes are not exceptions (slides; State v. Kirsch): they are non-character routes. The true exceptions are 404(a)(2), 404(a)(3), and 413–415. 404(b)(2) never compels admission; 403 still applies.
  • Huddleston four steps:
    (1) a proper purpose;
    (2) relevance, with the other act proved under 104(b) (a jury could reasonably find it by a preponderance);
    (3) 403;
    (4) a 105 instruction on request. An acquittal of the other act does not bar its use (Dowling).
  • Identity: the other crime must share characteristics "sufficiently idiosyncratic to permit an inference of pattern"; "prosaic commonalities" are not enough (Trenkler). Reverse 404(b) offered by a defendant needs only ordinary 401/403 similarity (Stevens).
  • Plan requires that the acts be parts of one overall scheme, not a mere pattern (Kirsch). Motive that is just a desire to commit this kind of crime is propensity (Kirsch).
  • Doctrine of chances (Brides in the Bath): many "accidents" are objectively improbable. One view says it avoids character; the other says it still runs through propensity (slides).
  • Rap lyrics: without a specific link to the crime, lyrics invite a violent-character inference (State v. Skinner, N.J. 2014). California, Louisiana, and Maryland have statutes restricting creative-expression evidence; Bey-Cousin v. Powell (E.D. Pa. 2021) presumes "artists tell stories."
  • Weapons and propensity: weapons that prove only that the defendant is a dangerous man are barred. Weapons bought after the threat (preparation) or found at the scene (placing him there) come in for that purpose, with a 105 instruction (People v. Zackowitz, N.Y. 1930).

7. Sexual-Assault Propensity (FRE 413–415)

rule

FRE 413(a): in a criminal case in which a defendant is accused of a sexual assault, the court may admit evidence that the defendant committed any other sexual assault, and it may be considered on any matter to which it is relevant. 414(a): the same for child molestation. 415(a): the same in civil cases based on sexual assault or child molestation. The proponent must disclose the evidence at least 15 days before trial (or later for good cause).

  • True exceptions allowing propensity reasoning, enacted by Congress in 1994 over the Judicial Conference's opposition. No conviction is required; the standard is presumably Huddleston's (Rep. Molinari). 403 still applies with its normal balance (Guardia). Due-process challenges have failed (Mound).
  • Critiques (Lannan; Baker): sex offenders are not shown to reoffend more (BJS: 7.7% rearrested for sexual assault within three years, versus about 60% for property and drug offenders); juries overweight priors; police "round up the usual suspects."
  • 413 threshold all required:
    (1) the defendant is accused of sexual assault;
    (2) the evidence is of another sexual assault; and
    (3) it is relevant. Then 403 applies with its normal balance, and courts may not treat propensity as inherently too prejudicial; disputes over whether the other acts were improper can still justify exclusion when they would turn a trial of two incidents into six (United States v. Guardia, 10th Cir. 1998).

8. Character of the Defendant and the Victim; Habit (FRE 404(a)(2), 405, 406)

rule

FRE 404(a)(2) (criminal cases): (A) a defendant may offer evidence of the defendant's pertinent trait, and if admitted the prosecutor may rebut it; (B) subject to 412, a defendant may offer evidence of an alleged victim's pertinent trait, and if admitted the prosecutor may rebut it and offer evidence of the defendant's same trait; (C) in a homicide case, the prosecutor may offer the victim's trait of peacefulness to rebut evidence that the victim was the first aggressor. 404(a)(3): a witness's character may be admitted under 607, 608, and 609.

FRE 405(a): character may be proved by reputation or opinion; on cross-examination of the character witness, the court may allow inquiry into relevant specific instances. 405(b): when character is an essential element of a charge, claim, or defense, it may also be proved by specific instances.

  • Only the accused opens the door, and only in criminal cases. On direct, reputation or opinion only. On cross, "have you heard" or "do you know" questions about specific acts test the witness's basis; they need a good-faith basis and the examiner is stuck with the answer (Michelson).
  • 405(b) is narrow. Character is an element in entrapment (predisposition), truth as a defense to defamation, negligent entrustment, custody, and sexually-violent-person commitment. It is not an element of self-defense (the issue is the defendant's reasonable fear), so a victim's specific violent acts are not admissible under 405(b) to prove his character.
  • A defendant who testifies "I wouldn't shoot anybody" makes herself a character witness and may be crossed on past shootings (Problem 3.16).
rule

FRE 406: evidence of a person's habit or an organization's routine practice may be admitted to prove that on a particular occasion the person or organization acted in accordance with it, regardless of corroboration or an eyewitness.

  • Habit is a regular, specific, semi-automatic response to a specific recurring situation (buckling a seatbelt), "predictable and predictive." Occasional conduct is not enough (Halloran: regularly heating Freon cans with a coil). Deliberate, morally charged conduct (a doctor's deceptions in Weil v. Seltzer) is usually character.
tip

Character steps:
(1) is it offered to show action in character? If not, 404 does not apply, though 403 does.
(2) A propensity route exists only through 404(a)(2) in a criminal case, once the accused opens the door, or 413–415 in sex cases.
(3) Form: reputation or opinion under 405(a); specific acts only on cross of a character witness, or when character is an essential element (405(b)).
(4) 404(b)(2): state the non-propensity chain inference by inference. MIMIC (motive, intent, absence of mistake, identity, common plan) is a memory aid; the rule's list is longer and not exhaustive. Then Huddleston: proper purpose, 104(b) sufficiency, 403, a 105 instruction on request. In a criminal case the prosecutor owes written notice under 404(b)(3).
(5) A witness's truthfulness goes to 607–609, not 404.
(6) Habit under 406 is not character: is the response specific, regular, and semi-automatic?

Part IV — Impeachment And The Rape Shield (FRE 607–610, 412)

9. The Modes of Impeachment

rule

FRE 607: any party, including the party that called the witness, may attack the witness's credibility.

  • Two families (slides): "the witness is lying now" (contradiction, prior inconsistent statement, bias, capacity) and "the witness is a liar" (608(a), 608(b), 609). Bias is never collateral and may be proved extrinsically (Abel). Religious beliefs may not be used to attack or support credibility (610).
  • Extrinsic evidence by mode: bias and capacity may be proved extrinsically; 608(b) acts may not (cross only); a 609 conviction may. A contradiction or inconsistent statement on a collateral matter, one relevant only to contradict the witness, usually may not. Under the common-law collateral-matter principle a witness's answer on such a point stands. 608(b)'s bar is absolute only when the sole purpose is to attack character for truthfulness (ACN; Abel).

10. Character for Truthfulness (FRE 608)

rule

FRE 608(a): a witness's credibility may be attacked or supported by reputation or opinion testimony about the witness's character for truthfulness or untruthfulness, but evidence of truthful character is admissible only after the witness's character for truthfulness has been attacked. 608(b): except for 609 convictions, extrinsic evidence is not admissible to prove specific instances of a witness's conduct to attack or support character for truthfulness; the court may, on cross-examination, allow inquiry into them if probative of the character for truthfulness of (1) the witness or (2) another witness about whose character the witness being cross-examined has testified.

  • 608 applies in civil and criminal cases, to every witness, and either side may start the attack.
  • Limits on 608(b) all required:
    (1) the act must be probative of truthfulness (the lie, not the underlying misconduct);
    (2) no extrinsic proof (take the answer);
    (3) 403 discretion;
    (4) a good-faith basis (Whitmore); and
    (5) protection from harassment (611(a)). Extrinsic evidence offered for another purpose, such as bias, is governed by 401–403.
  • Rehabilitation requires an attack on character for truthfulness any one suffices:
    (1) 608 opinion or reputation,
    (2) 608(b) acts, or
    (3) 609 convictions. Bias is not such an attack (ACN), and a single contradiction usually is not (Problems 4.6–4.7).
  • Questions, not proof: 608(b) bars extrinsic proof, not questions. Barring a good-faith cross about a lie probative of untruthfulness can be an abuse of discretion, while 608(a) witnesses whose information is too remote or too narrow may be excluded (United States v. Whitmore, D.C. Cir. 2004).

11. Impeachment by Conviction (FRE 609)

rule

FRE 609(a): to attack a witness's character for truthfulness by a criminal conviction: (1) for a crime punishable by death or more than one year in the convicting jurisdiction, the evidence (A) must be admitted, subject to 403, in a civil case or for a witness who is not a criminal defendant, and (B) must be admitted against a testifying criminal defendant if its probative value outweighs its prejudicial effect to that defendant; (2) for any crime, the evidence must be admitted if the court can readily determine that establishing its elements required proving, or the witness's admitting, a dishonest act or false statement.

609(b): if more than 10 years have passed since the conviction or release from confinement for it, whichever is later, the conviction is admissible only if its probative value, supported by specific facts and circumstances, substantially outweighs its prejudicial effect, and the proponent gives reasonable written notice. 609(c): pardons and the like based on rehabilitation (with no later felony) or innocence bar use. 609(d): juvenile adjudications only in a criminal case, only against a witness other than the defendant, only if an adult conviction would be admissible, and only if necessary to fairly determine guilt or innocence. 609(e): a conviction is admissible even if an appeal is pending, and the pendency is also admissible.

  • What counts is the maximum punishment, not the sentence imposed. Larceny, robbery, and drug crimes are generally not crimen falsi; the court looks to the elements and, if needed, the indictment, admitted facts, or jury instructions (ACN 2006). 609(b) applies to crimen falsi too.

  • Gordon factors for 609(a)(1)(B) weighed together:
    (1) the nature of the crime (violence says little about veracity);
    (2) its recency and the witness's later history;
    (3) similarity to the charged crime (weighs against admission);
    (4) the importance of the defendant's testimony (weighs against admission);
    (5) the centrality of credibility (weighs for admission). A conviction identical to the charged crime is usually excluded (United States v. Brewer, E.D. Tenn. 1978). The 609(b) ten years run from the later of conviction or release, including release from reconfinement for a parole violation.

12. The Rape Shield (FRE 412)

rule

FRE 412(a): in a civil or criminal proceeding involving alleged sexual misconduct, evidence offered to prove that a victim engaged in other sexual behavior, or to prove a victim's sexual predisposition, is not admissible. 412(b)(1) (criminal): the court may admit (A) specific instances of the victim's sexual behavior offered to prove someone other than the defendant was the source of semen, injury, or other physical evidence; (B) specific instances with the accused, offered by the defendant to prove consent or by the prosecutor; (C) evidence whose exclusion would violate the defendant's constitutional rights. 412(b)(2) (civil): admissible if its probative value substantially outweighs the danger of harm to any victim and unfair prejudice to any party; reputation only if the victim placed it in controversy. 412(c): a motion at least 14 days before trial, notice to the victim, and a sealed in camera hearing.

  • Why (slides): the common law let defendants argue that past consent meant present consent (via the 404(a)(2)(B) analogue) and that unchastity showed untruthfulness (via 608(b)); Wigmore urged suspicion of complainants. Colb: once a woman swears she did not consent, her history has no real predictive force.
  • A prior false accusation is not sexual behavior, so 412 does not reach it; the threshold is whether a jury could find it false (State v. Smith, La. 1999). Ordinary 403 and 608(b) limits still apply.
  • Asymmetry with 413–415 (slides): the shield protects reporting and privacy, interests a past offender lacks, and a victim's history cuts both ways while a defendant's prior rapes cut one way.
  • Bias and the Constitution: excluding cross-examination that shows a complainant's motive to fabricate, "a prototypical form of bias," violates the Confrontation Clause, the model for 412(b)(1)(C) (Olden v. Kentucky, 1988).
tip

Impeachment problem:
(1) name the theory (bias, contradiction, inconsistency, 608(a), 608(b), 609);
(2) apply its form-of-proof limits (opinion or reputation; cross only; record of conviction);
(3) is extrinsic evidence allowed? (bias and non-collateral matters, yes; 608(b) character, no);
(4) has character been attacked before rehabilitation?
(5) 403, 611(a), and in sex cases, 412.

Part V — Competency And Hearsay

13. Competency (FRE 601–603)

  • 601: every person is competent unless the rules provide otherwise (state law governs in civil cases where state law supplies the rule of decision). 602: a witness must have personal knowledge, shown by evidence sufficient to support a finding, a 104(b) question (not required for expert opinion under 703). 603: an oath or affirmation. The presiding judge (605) and jurors at the trial (606(a)) may not testify.
  • Old rules barred parties, spouses, interested persons, felons, atheists, and non-white persons. Today competency mostly concerns young children: the ability to perceive and remember, communicate, understand the duty to tell the truth, and answer on cross. 18 U.S.C. § 3509(c) presumes children competent.
  • Competency asks whether this witness can be tested by cross; hearsay asks whether this statement can be (slides).

14. The Definition of Hearsay (FRE 801(a)–(c), 802)

rule

FRE 801(a): a "statement" is a person's oral assertion, written assertion, or nonverbal conduct, if the person intended it as an assertion. 801(c): hearsay is a statement that (1) the declarant does not make while testifying at the current trial or hearing, and (2) a party offers to prove the truth of the matter asserted. 802: hearsay is not admissible unless a federal statute, the FRE, or other Supreme Court rules provide otherwise.

  • The four testimonial capacities: perception, memory, narration, and sincerity. Cross-examination tests them in a live witness; hearsay leaves the declarant's untested (slides). Raleigh's trial on Cobham's confession is the founding example.
  • A witness repeating her own earlier words is still hearsay.
  • "You can never answer the question, 'Is this hearsay?' without knowing the proponent's purpose" (slides).
  • Not hearsay: words offered for their effect on the listener (notice, motive, reasonable fear: Shelley Winters's marriage demand in A Place in the Sun; a complaint to a supervisor offered to show notice); verbal acts (contract words, threats, defamation); nonassertive conduct (Santiago failing to pack; the sea captain sailing with his family; paramedics' silence); involuntary exclamations; words offered as circumstantial evidence of knowledge (a child's accurate description of the defendant's room; drug recipes showing know-how); machine output.
  • Conduct intended as an assertion is a statement (pointing at a fleeing car; Minister Gummer eating beef for the press). An implied or indirect assertion offered for the belief behind it, if the speaker intended to communicate that belief, is hearsay ("Planning my retirement home" offered to show the speaker was not suicidal).
  • 805: hearsay within hearsay is admissible only if each layer fits an exception.
  • Necessity and trustworthiness are the two justifications behind every exception (slides).
  • Implied assertions: under 801(a) a statement depends on the declarant's intent to assert. Words not intended to assert the fact they are offered to prove are not hearsay: callers placing bets on a bookmaker's phone did not assert that the premises were a betting operation (United States v. Zenni, E.D. Ky. 1980). The Rules reject the common-law view that treated such implied assertions as hearsay (Wright v. Tatham, 1837).

15. Opposing Party's Statements (FRE 801(d)(2))

rule

FRE 801(d)(2): not hearsay if offered against an opposing party and (A) made by the party in an individual or representative capacity; (B) adopted by the party or believed to be true; (C) made by a person the party authorized to speak on the subject; (D) made by the party's agent or employee on a matter within the scope of that relationship while it existed; or (E) made by the party's coconspirator during and in furtherance of the conspiracy. The statement must be considered but does not by itself establish authority under (C), the relationship under (D), or the conspiracy and participation under (E). If a party's claim, defense, or potential liability is directly derived from a declarant or the declarant's principal, a statement that would be admissible against the declarant or the principal under this rule is also admissible against the party (2024).

  • No personal knowledge or against-interest requirement (Mahlandt); opinions are fine. Justified by the adversary system: a party cannot complain she was unable to cross-examine herself.
  • Adoption by silence: the party
    (1) heard and understood,
    (2) was free to respond,
    (3) the circumstances naturally called for a denial, and
    (4) did not deny. Post-Miranda silence may not be used to impeach (Doyle) and likely not as an adoption; pre-arrest and pre-Miranda silence may impeach (Jenkins; Fletcher v. Weir), and non-custodial silence may be used substantively (Salinas, plurality).
  • Coconspirators: the judge finds the conspiracy and the "during and in furtherance" requirement by a preponderance under 104(a), may consider the statement itself, but needs some independent evidence (Bourjaily; the 1997 amendment). No conspiracy charge is needed; a joint venture suffices. Confessions to police almost never qualify.
  • Successors (2024 ACN): a party who stands in the shoes of a declarant or the declarant's principal gets no better position on hearsay than they had. An estate suing on the decedent's claim takes the decedent's statements; so do an assignee and a trustee pursuing the debtor's claims. The sentence does not reach a statement made after the rights or obligations were transferred, or one admissible against an agent but not against the principal (made after the employment ended).
  • Party-specific admissibility: a statement comes in only against the party it fits. An employee's statements came in against him under 801(d)(2)(A) and against his employer under 801(d)(2)(D), while the organization's minutes came in against the organization but not the employee; statements made within an organization count (Mahlandt, 8th Cir. 1978).

16. Prior Statements of a Testifying Witness (FRE 613, 801(d)(1), 612)

rule

FRE 801(d)(1): not hearsay if the declarant testifies and is subject to cross-examination about a prior statement, and the statement (A) is inconsistent with the declarant's testimony and was given under penalty of perjury at a trial, hearing, or other proceeding or in a deposition; (B) is consistent with the testimony and is offered (i) to rebut an express or implied charge of recent fabrication or recent improper influence or motive, or (ii) to rehabilitate the declarant's credibility when attacked on another ground; or (C) identifies a person as someone the declarant perceived earlier.

FRE 613(a): in examining a witness about a prior statement, a party need not show it or disclose its contents to the witness, but must on request show or disclose it to an adverse party's attorney.

FRE 613(b) (as amended 2024): unless the court orders otherwise, extrinsic evidence of a witness's prior inconsistent statement may not be admitted until after the witness is given an opportunity to explain or deny it and an adverse party may examine the witness about it. This does not apply to an opposing party's statement under 801(d)(2).

note

Rule 801(d)(1)(A) changes on Dec. 1, 2026, nine days before the Dec. 10 final. The Supreme Court adopted the amendment on Apr. 8, 2026. Unless Congress acts, it governs proceedings commenced after Dec. 1, 2026 and, as far as just and practicable, pending ones. New text of (A): "is inconsistent with the declarant's testimony;" The requirement that the statement was given under penalty of perjury at a trial, hearing, or other proceeding or in a deposition is gone.

Effect (Committee Note): a prior inconsistent statement of a witness who testifies and is subject to cross about it is substantive evidence even if unsworn, so no limiting instruction is needed, and inconsistent and consistent statements are treated alike. The Committee reasoned that delayed cross under oath allays the hearsay concerns, and that the old rule was unduly narrow, of use mainly to prosecutors with grand jury testimony. Unchanged: 613(b) still governs extrinsic proof; 403 handles difficulties of proof; and the rule governs admissibility, not sufficiency.

For the exam: Fisher (2022) and the Class 12–13 slides teach the old rule, and everything else in this section states it. Ask Prof. Hornzell which version the final uses. Until then, give both results for an unsworn statement.

  • 613 impeaches; 801(d)(1)(A) is substantive. An unsworn statement to police is impeachment only, so counsel may not argue it as proof (slides, Troisi). Extrinsic proof of an inconsistent statement is allowed under 613(b) on a non-collateral point. Under the amendment effective Dec. 1, 2026 (note above), that statement would come in substantively.
  • (B)(i) requires a pre-motive statement (Tome). (C) admits an identification even if the witness now cannot remember or misidentifies in court (Owens).
  • 612 vs. 803(5): anything may refresh memory; the witness reads silently and testifies from memory, and the adverse party may inspect the writing and introduce relevant parts. A recorded recollection comes in for its truth only with the 803(5) foundation (below). 612 gives those options for a writing used while testifying, and for one used before testifying if the court decides justice requires it. In a criminal case 18 U.S.C. § 3500 controls where it applies. The court cuts unrelated matter in camera. If a writing is withheld the court may issue any appropriate order; if the prosecution does not comply, it must strike the testimony or, if justice so requires, declare a mistrial.
  • Impeachment without substantive use: an inconsistent statement may impeach under 613 even when it cannot come in for its truth, and its vagueness goes to weight (United States v. Barrett, 1st Cir. 1976).
  • Memory loss: a witness who cannot remember is still subject to cross-examination for 801(d)(1)(C) and the Confrontation Clause, which guarantees "an opportunity for effective cross-examination, not cross-examination that is effective in whatever way, and to whatever extent, the defense might wish" (United States v. Owens, 1988).

17. Exceptions Requiring Unavailability (FRE 804)

rule

FRE 804(a): a declarant is unavailable if the declarant
(1) is exempted by a privilege ruling;
(2) refuses to testify despite a court order;
(3) testifies to not remembering the subject matter;
(4) cannot testify because of death or then-existing infirmity or illness; or
(5) is absent and the proponent could not procure attendance (or, for (b)(2)–(4), attendance or testimony) by process or other reasonable means. Not unavailable if the proponent procured or wrongfully caused the unavailability to prevent attendance or testimony.

804(b)(1) former testimony: given as a witness at a trial, hearing, or lawful deposition, and offered against a party who had, or in a civil case whose predecessor in interest had, an opportunity and similar motive to develop it by direct, cross, or redirect examination. (b)(2) dying declaration: in a homicide prosecution or a civil case, a statement made while the declarant believed death imminent, about its cause or circumstances. (b)(3) statement against interest: a statement a reasonable person in the declarant's position would have made only if believing it true because it was so contrary to the declarant's pecuniary or proprietary interest, or tended to invalidate a claim or expose the declarant to civil or criminal liability; in a criminal case, a penal-interest statement also needs corroborating circumstances that clearly indicate trustworthiness, considering the totality of circumstances and any evidence that supports or undermines it (2024). (b)(6) forfeiture: offered against a party that wrongfully caused, or acquiesced in wrongfully causing, the declarant's unavailability, intending that result.

  • Subjective vs. objective: dying declarations and excited utterances look to the declarant's actual state; statements against interest use a reasonable person (slides).
  • 804(b)(3)(B) corroboration (2024 ACN): the court must weigh both the circumstances of the statement (timing, spontaneity, the declarant's likely motives) and any evidence supporting or undermining it, such as evidence placing a third-party confessor near the crime. Independent evidence is relevant but not necessarily dispositive.
  • 806: once a hearsay statement (or a (d)(2)(C)–(E) statement) is admitted, the declarant's credibility may be attacked and supported as if the declarant had testified; the 613(b) foundation is not required.
  • Predecessor in interest (civil cases only): asks whether the earlier party had a similar stake and a similar motive, not formal privity. The State that prosecuted a man was the officers' predecessor in interest in his later § 1983 suit against them (Volland-Golden v. City of Chicago, N.D. Ill. 2015).
  • Similar motive: the requirement binds the government too, with no "adversarial fairness" shortcut (United States v. Salerno, 1992). It means a substantially similar interest at a similar stage, which prosecutors usually lack at the grand jury (United States v. DiNapoli, 2d Cir. 1993 (en banc)).
  • Collateral statements: 804(b)(3) admits only the individual remarks that are themselves self-inculpatory. Collateral statements within a broader confession, such as an accomplice's statements shifting blame, are not admissible (Williamson, 1994).
  • Dying-declaration foundation: the proponent must show the declarant had "a settled hopeless expectation" of imminent death. An accusation of a past act ("Dr. Shepard has poisoned me") is not a statement of state of mind either, because 803(3) excludes backward-looking memory (Shepard, 1933).
  • Forfeiture by wrongdoing (United States v. Gray, 4th Cir. 2005): having killed her husband a week before he was to testify against her on assault charges, the defendant forfeited her hearsay objection to his statements in her later insurance-fraud trial. The proponent proves the wrongdoing and the intent by a preponderance under 104(a) (ACN 1997).

18. Exceptions Regardless of Availability (FRE 803)

rule

803(1) present sense impression: a statement describing or explaining an event or condition, made while or immediately after the declarant perceived it. (2) excited utterance: a statement relating to a startling event, made while the declarant was under the stress of excitement it caused. (3) then-existing state of mind or condition: motive, intent, plan, emotion, sensation, pain, or bodily health, but not memory or belief offered to prove the fact remembered (except as to the declarant's will). (4) medical diagnosis or treatment: a statement made for, and reasonably pertinent to, diagnosis or treatment, describing medical history, symptoms, their inception, or their general cause. (5) recorded recollection: a record on a matter the witness once knew but cannot now recall well enough to testify fully and accurately, made or adopted when fresh, that accurately reflects the witness's knowledge; it may be read but received as an exhibit only if offered by an adverse party.

803(6) records of a regularly conducted activity all required: (A) made at or near the time by, or from information transmitted by, someone with knowledge; (B) kept in the course of a regularly conducted activity; (C) making the record was a regular practice; (D) shown by a custodian, another qualified witness, or a 902(11)/(12) certification; and (E) the opponent does not show a lack of trustworthiness (the opponent's burden since 2014). 803(7): absence of an entry. 803(8) public records: (A)(i) the office's activities; (ii) matters observed under a legal duty to report (but not, in a criminal case, matters observed by law-enforcement personnel); and, (iii) in civil cases or against the government in criminal cases, factual findings from a legally authorized investigation; (B) unless the opponent shows untrustworthiness. 803(10): absence of a public record (with notice-and-demand in criminal cases).

  • Personal knowledge applies to declarants (the ACN; The Graduate clip). Judge Posner (United States v. Boyce, 7th Cir. 2014, concurring) doubts both 803(1) and (2): most lies are spontaneous, and excitement impairs perception.
  • Hillmon doctrine: a statement of the declarant's own intent may prove the declarant later acted on it. Using it to prove another person's conduct is contested: Walters's letter could show he went, but showing Hillmon went depends on Walters's memory of Hillmon's plan (slides).
  • Identity in 803(4): fault and identity are ordinarily not pertinent; some courts admit the identity of a household child abuser; the proponent must show how identity affected treatment (State v. Smith, Iowa 2016).
  • 803(5) foundation (Johnson) all required:
    (1) firsthand knowledge;
    (2) made when fresh;
    (3) insufficient present memory; and
    (4) the witness vouches for the record's accuracy.
  • Business records must be made in the regular course by people with a business duty; litigation-driven reports are suspect (Palmer; 803(6)(E)). Police reports offered against a defendant cannot evade 803(8)'s limits.
  • Third-party Hillmon problem: the Ninth Circuit admits a declarant's statement of intent to show that the other person was there too (United States v. Pheaster, 1976), despite the House report's view that 803(3) proves only the declarant's own conduct. Some courts require independent evidence of the other person's involvement.
  • Outsiders in business records: an informant with no business duty to report (a bystander quoted in a police report) is a separate layer that needs its own exception under 805 (Johnson v. Lutz, N.Y. 1930).
  • Factual findings under 803(8): include opinions and conclusions based on a factual investigation, subject to the rule's trustworthiness check (Beech Aircraft v. Rainey, 1988).

19. The Residual Exception (FRE 807)

rule

FRE 807(a): a hearsay statement not admissible under 803 or 804 is not excluded if (1) it is supported by sufficient guarantees of trustworthiness, after considering the totality of circumstances under which it was made and any corroborating evidence; and (2) it is more probative on the point for which it is offered than any other evidence the proponent can obtain through reasonable efforts. 807(b): reasonable written notice before trial, including the statement's substance and the declarant's name (or during trial for good cause).

  • The 2019 amendment ended the "near-miss" debate: a statement that narrowly misses another exception may be considered (contrast the Laster dissent). Courts use 807 rarely (slides).
  • The model for 807: a 1901 newspaper report of a courthouse fire was admitted because it was necessary (no living witnesses) and trustworthy (no motive to lie; public and easily corrected) (Dallas County, 5th Cir. 1961). Today such an article might be an ancient document under 803(16) (prepared before Jan. 1, 1998).
tip

Hearsay steps:
(1) a statement (801(a)): a person's assertion, or conduct intended as one?
(2) Offered for its truth? Name the proponent's purpose; verbal acts, effect on the listener, and circumstantial proof of knowledge are not hearsay.
(3) Layers: split a multi-level statement, and give each layer its own route (805); an outsider with no business duty is a separate layer in a business record (Johnson v. Lutz).
(4) 801(d) exclusions: an opposing party's statement (offered against that party) or a testifying witness's prior statement.
(5) Exceptions: 803 (availability immaterial); 804 (prove 804(a) unavailability first); 807 last, with written notice.
(6) The proponent proves the foundation to the judge under 104(a).
(7) 403, a 105 instruction for limited use, and 806 attacks on the declarant.
(8) Against a criminal defendant, run the Confrontation Clause separately (Part VI).

Part VI — The Confrontation Clause

20. The Crawford Framework

  • The Sixth Amendment guarantees the accused the right "to be confronted with the witnesses against him." It protects only criminal defendants, and evidence must survive both the hearsay rules and the Clause (slides).
  • Roberts (1980), overruled: admissible if the declarant was unavailable and the statement bore "indicia of reliability" (a firmly rooted exception or particularized guarantees of trustworthiness).
  • Mattox (1895) and California v. Green (1970): prior testimony subject to cross may be used if the declarant is unavailable; a declarant who testifies and answers questions at trial removes the confrontation problem.
rule

Crawford v. Washington, 541 U.S. 36 (2004): testimonial statements of a witness absent from trial are admissible only if (1) the declarant is unavailable and (2) the defendant had a prior opportunity to cross-examine. The Clause does not bar statements the declarant is cross-examined about at trial, statements not offered for their truth, statements admissible under forfeiture by wrongdoing (Giles), and perhaps dying declarations. Nontestimonial statements are governed only by the hearsay rules (Whorton v. Bockting, 2007).

  • "Testimonial" includes ex parte in-court testimony and its equivalents (affidavits, custodial examinations), formalized materials, prior testimony, statements made for use at trial, and police interrogations. Nontestimonial: casual remarks to acquaintances, statements in furtherance of a conspiracy, most business records.
  • Davis/Hammon (2006): statements in police interrogation are nontestimonial when the objective primary purpose is to enable police to meet an ongoing emergency, and testimonial when there is no emergency and the primary purpose is to establish or prove past events for prosecution.
  • Bryant (2011): the emergency depends on the threat (a gun and a shooter at large threaten the public, unlike a domestic assault by fists). Courts consider
    (1) the location,
    (2) the declarant's and interrogators' purposes,
    (3) the declarant's medical condition, and
    (4) formality, judged objectively.
  • Clark (2015): testimonial means made with "the primary purpose of assisting in [a] prosecution." Statements to non-police are much less likely to be testimonial, and very young children's statements rarely are.
  • Forensic reports: sworn lab certificates are testimonial (Melendez-Diaz, 2009); a "surrogate" analyst who did not perform or observe the test cannot satisfy the Clause (Bullcoming, 2011). An expert who conveys an absent analyst's statements as the basis of an opinion offers them for their truth if the opinion depends on their truth (Smith v. Arizona, 2024); whether the statements are testimonial is a separate question.
  • Shifting purpose: a 911 call describing an ongoing emergency is nontestimonial but can become testimonial once the emergency ends (Davis); an account of past events given to police at the scene, with no emergency, is testimonial (Hammon).
  • Non-police audiences: neither a young child nor teachers addressing an emergency have the primary purpose of assisting a prosecution, and mandatory reporting duties do not make teachers police agents. A testimonial primary purpose is necessary but not always sufficient: dying declarations and forfeiture remain (Ohio v. Clark, 2015).
  • Forfeiture requires intent: forfeiture by wrongdoing applies only if the defendant intended to prevent the witness from testifying, matching 804(b)(6)'s "intending that result"; an abusive relationship meant to isolate the victim from police may show that intent (Giles v. California, 2008).
tip

Confrontation steps:
(1) criminal case, prosecution evidence?
(2) offered for its truth (including as an expert's basis)?
(3) does the declarant testify and submit to cross?
(4) is the statement testimonial (emergency, formality, audience, the declarant's age)?
(5) if so, unavailability plus a prior opportunity to cross, or forfeiture with intent;
(6) then the hearsay rules separately.

Part VII — Joint Trials And The Right To Present A Defense

21. Completeness and Bruton

rule

FRE 106 (as amended 2023): if a party introduces all or part of a statement, an adverse party may require the introduction, at that time, of any other part, or any other statement, that in fairness ought to be considered at the same time, and may do so over a hearsay objection. (Now covers oral statements.)

  • 2023 amendment (ACN): it resolved a circuit split over hearsay objections. A party who creates a misimpression cannot exclude the correction as hearsay. If a murder defendant said he owned the gun but had sold it months earlier, the prosecution cannot offer only the ownership half. The completing statement comes in for context when that is enough, and for its truth when it corrects the misimpression only if true (the sale). Fisher (2022) prints the old text, limited to writings and recorded statements.
  • The rule stays narrow: the statement must actually correct a misimpression. A prior denial of guilt does not come in merely because it contradicts a later admission (United States v. Williams, 2d Cir. 2019, cited in the ACN). An unrecorded statement needs admissible proof that it was made, and 403 still applies. The amendment displaces the common-law rule, and the court may still allow completion later than "at that time."
rule

Bruton v. United States, 391 U.S. 123 (1968): at a joint trial, admitting a nontestifying codefendant's confession that facially incriminates the defendant violates the Confrontation Clause even with a clear limiting instruction. The statement must be inadmissible against the defendant (fn. 3) and, after Crawford, testimonial.

  • Prosecutors' options (slides) any one suffices:
    (1) sever;
    (2) use separate juries;
    (3) have the confessing codefendant testify;
    (4) use prior cross-examined testimony;
    (5) redact;
    (6) try the case to the bench; or
    (7) use a statement admissible against both (a nontestimonial coconspirator statement raises no Bruton problem: the Dog O' War letter, Problem 8.8).
  • Redaction: removing the defendant's name and any reference to his existence is permitted (Richardson v. Marsh, 1987). Replacing the name with an obvious blank, "deleted," or a symbol is not (Gray). A natural neutral phrase such as "the other person" is permitted (Samia v. United States, 2023). Harmless-error review applies (Harrington).
  • Testing a redaction: ask whether the redacted statement would point at the defendant "even were the confession the very first item introduced at trial." Obvious redactions fall within Bruton as a class (Gray, 1998), but only those that are "directly accusatory" (Samia, 2023).

22. Compulsory Process and Due Process

  • The Sixth Amendment guarantees compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in the defendant's favor. The Constitution guarantees "a meaningful opportunity to present a complete defense," whether through due process, compulsory process, or confrontation (Crane v. Kentucky, 1986). A defendant must still comply with established rules of evidence.
  • Two tests: Chambers's critical and trustworthy test for valid rules applied mechanistically to unusual facts; and the arbitrariness test of Washington v. Texas (1967), Rock v. Arkansas (1987), and Holmes v. South Carolina (2006) for rules (or constructions) that are arbitrary or disproportionate to their purposes.
  • Chambers factors weighed together: the excluded third-party confessions were
    (1) made spontaneously to close friends shortly after the crime;
    (2) corroborated by other evidence;
    (3) against the declarant's interest; and
    (4) backed by the declarant's availability to the State for cross-examination. On those facts two evidentiary rulings together denied due process: "the hearsay rule may not be applied mechanistically to defeat the ends of justice" (Chambers v. Mississippi, 1973). Green v. Georgia (1979) extended the principle to a single hearsay ruling.
  • Arbitrary rules: a rule that judges defense evidence of third-party guilt only by the strength of the prosecution's case is arbitrary and denies "a meaningful opportunity to present a complete defense." Ordinary rules excluding third-party-guilt evidence that raises only a bare suspicion remain valid (Holmes v. South Carolina, 2006). Contrast Scheffer (a per se polygraph ban upheld).

Part VIII — Lay And Expert Opinion (Week 10 Reading)

23. Lay Opinion (FRE 701)

rule

FRE 701: a lay witness's opinion is limited to one that is
(a) rationally based on the witness's perception;
(b) helpful to clearly understanding the testimony or determining a fact in issue; and
(c) not based on scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge within the scope of 702.

  • Ordinary inferences are lay opinion: appearance, age, emotional state, speed, intoxication with a foundation, identity, and a familiar substance's identity (the 2000 ACN). (c) stops "an expert in lay witness clothing" from evading 702 and disclosure rules. A business owner may give opinions based on particularized knowledge of the business, but not specialized expertise.

24. Expert Testimony: Qualification and Topics (FRE 702, 704)

rule

FRE 702 (as amended 2023): a witness qualified by knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education may testify in the form of an opinion or otherwise if the proponent demonstrates to the court that it is more likely than not that
(a) the expert's specialized knowledge will help the trier of fact;
(b) the testimony is based on sufficient facts or data;
(c) it is the product of reliable principles and methods; and
(d) the opinion reflects a reliable application of the principles and methods to the facts. 704(a): an opinion is not objectionable just because it embraces an ultimate issue. 704(b): in a criminal case, an expert may not state an opinion about whether the defendant had a mental state or condition that is an element of the crime or a defense.

  • Qualification is topic-specific, and experience can qualify. Helpfulness fails on matters of common knowledge (whether "Match" and "Macho" sound alike) but not on technical ones (the slip resistance of wet tile; how all-white ads communicate to Black viewers).
  • Experts (1) may not instruct on the law or tell the jury what result to reach (Hygh); (2) may not opine on a criminal defendant's own mens rea (704(b); Diaz v. United States, 2024, allows opinions about most people in the defendant's position); and may not vouch for a witness's credibility, though they may explain behavior that seems inconsistent with abuse (Batangan; Nimely).
tip

Opinion steps:
(1) lay or expert (is it specialized?);
(2) lay: perception, helpfulness, not specialized;
(3) expert: qualified for this opinion, helpful, no legal conclusions, no 704(b) mental-state opinion, no vouching;
(4) basis and reliability (Rules 702(b)–(d) and 703; Week 11);
(5) 403.

Part IX — Expert Bases And Reliability (Week 11 Reading)

25. Eyewitness-Identification Experts (FRE 702)

  • Courts now accept findings many jurors would not expect: confidence and accuracy are at best weakly correlated; weapon focus and high stress impair perception and memory; cross-racial identifications are less accurate; memory fades within hours; procedures that are not double-blind and sequential are less reliable; postevent information inflates confidence; and witnesses can unconsciously transfer a face from another setting. Cross-examination exposes lies better than sincere mistakes, and a cautionary instruction comes too late and says too little.
  • Admissibility: expert testimony on the fallibility of eyewitness identification is admissible when it would help the jury, subject to the usual qualification, relevance, and reliability screening. The expert may describe general factors bearing on the identification but may not opine on whether a particular eyewitness is accurate (State v. Guilbert, Conn. 2012). This is a near-national consensus (44 states, D.C., and at least ten circuits).

26. Bases of Expert Opinion (FRE 702(b), 703, 705)

rule

FRE 703: an expert may base an opinion on facts or data the expert has been made aware of or personally observed. If experts in the field would reasonably rely on those kinds of facts or data, they need not be admissible for the opinion to be admitted. But the proponent may disclose otherwise-inadmissible facts or data to the jury only if their probative value in helping the jury evaluate the opinion substantially outweighs their prejudicial effect. FRE 705: unless the court orders otherwise, an expert may state an opinion and the reasons without first testifying to the underlying facts, but may be required to disclose them on cross-examination.

  • Three permissible bases (ACN to 703) any one suffices:
    (1) facts the expert personally observed;
    (2) facts made known at the hearing (watching the trial, or a hypothetical question whose assumed facts the evidence could support); and
    (3) facts made known before the hearing, including inadmissible hearsay, if experts in the field reasonably rely on that kind of data. Rule 602's personal-knowledge requirement does not apply to expert testimony under 703.
  • Reliance does not make the underlying facts admissible: only the opinion comes in. The 2000 amendment's reverse-403 test creates "a presumption against disclosure" by the proponent, with a limiting instruction if the facts are disclosed; the opponent may bring them out on cross (705). The expert must apply the discipline's methods, not act as a conduit for others' statements. Reliance makes hearsay substantive only through its own exception, such as 803(18) (learned treatises, read in but not received as exhibits) or 803(4).
  • In a criminal case, basis evidence the expert relies on for its truth is offered for its truth, so the Confrontation Clause applies to an absent analyst's statements (Smith v. Arizona, 2024, rejecting the Williams v. Illinois plurality's "not for its truth" theory).
  • Reasonable reliance: the question is what experts in the discipline reasonably rely on, not what the judge would; psychiatrists reasonably rely on reports from family members. Disclosure is harder, because the jury cannot use a basis to evaluate the opinion without believing it ("Labels cannot perform juridical alchemy") (In re Melton, D.C. 1991).

27. Reliability: Frye, Daubert, Joiner, and Kumho Tire (FRE 702(b)–(d))

  • Rule 702(b)–(d), added in 2000, codify Daubert and Kumho: sufficient facts or data, reliable principles and methods, and a reliable application to the facts. Since the 2023 amendment the proponent must show each element to the court by a preponderance (104(a)), and each opinion must stay within what a reliable application supports.
  • 2023 ACN: decisions treating the sufficiency of an expert's basis or the application of the method as questions of weight are "an incorrect application of Rules 702 and 104(a)." Once the court finds the requirements met, remaining attacks go to weight (an expert who has not read every study), and the proponent need not prove the expert correct, only reliable. Experts on contested facts may both testify, because the jury resolves the facts. The test is "help," not "appreciably help." Forensic experts should avoid claims of absolute certainty for a subjective method, and the judge should, where possible, receive an error-rate estimate. Fisher (2022) predates the amendment.
  • Frye general acceptance: the thing from which the deduction is made "must be sufficiently established to have gained general acceptance in the particular field in which it belongs" (Frye, D.C. Cir. 1923). It still governs in a handful of states, and its rejection of lie detection survives almost everywhere.
  • Daubert gatekeeping: Frye did not survive the Federal Rules, but the trial judge must ensure that scientific testimony is relevant and reliable, as a 104(a) question (Daubert, 1993). Factors weighed together:
    (1) whether the theory can be and has been tested;
    (2) peer review and publication;
    (3) the known or potential error rate;
    (4) standards controlling the technique;
    (5) general acceptance. The focus is on principles and methodology, not conclusions, and the testimony must "fit" the issue. Shaky but admissible evidence is met by cross-examination, contrary evidence, and instructions.
  • Analytical gap: "conclusions and methodology are not entirely distinct from one another," so a court need not admit an opinion "connected to existing data only by the ipse dixit of the expert" when the gap between the data and the opinion is too great. Rulings are reviewed for abuse of discretion (General Electric Co. v. Joiner, 1997).
  • All expert testimony: the gatekeeping duty covers "scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge." The judge may use the Daubert factors where they reasonably measure reliability, and has the same latitude in deciding how to test reliability as in deciding whether it exists (abuse-of-discretion review). The question is specific: whether this method can reliably answer this question in this case (Kumho Tire, 1999). Weak fields can no longer escape Daubert as non-scientific skills.
  • Forensic sciences: under Frye, handwriting, fingerprint, fiber, and ballistics evidence passed easily because the "relevant community" was the practitioners. The 2009 National Academy of Sciences report found that, apart from nuclear DNA, most fields lack standardized protocols, error rates, and criteria for a match. The 2016 PCAST report required empirical error rates for "foundational validity" (702(c)) and examiner proficiency for "validity as applied" (702(d)); it found bitemarks not valid and firearms short of validity. Courts usually admit forensic testimony with limits (no claims of certainty or a zero error rate; class associations such as "consistent with" for hair and fiber) rather than exclude it, though bitemark evidence is being pushed out. Polygraph evidence is still excluded almost everywhere (United States v. Scheffer upheld a per se ban).
tip

Expert steps:
(1) qualified for this opinion;
(2) helpful, not a legal conclusion (Hygh), not vouching (Batangan, Guilbert), not a 704(b) mental-state opinion;
(3) sufficient facts or data, of a kind experts reasonably rely on (703), not a conduit, and the reverse-403 test before the proponent discloses inadmissible bases;
(4) reliable methods reliably applied (Daubert factors as they fit, Kumho, Joiner's analytical gap), each shown by a preponderance;
(5) 403;
(6) in a criminal case, confrontation for basis evidence (Smith v. Arizona).

Part X — Authentication And The Best Evidence Rule (Week 12 Reading)

28. Authentication and Identification (FRE 901, 902)

rule

FRE 901(a): to authenticate or identify an item of evidence, the proponent must produce evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims it is.

  • The standard is conditional relevance (104(b) and Huddleston): the judge asks only whether a reasonable jury could find authenticity, the jury decides, and the opponent may still contest it before the jury. Authentication is only the first gate; the exhibit may still fail on hearsay, the best evidence rule, or 403.
  • 901(b) examples ("not a complete list"):
    (1) testimony of a witness with knowledge;
    (2) a nonexpert's opinion on handwriting based on familiarity not acquired for the current litigation;
    (3) comparison with an authenticated specimen by an expert or the trier of fact;
    (4) distinctive characteristics taken together with all the circumstances;
    (5) voice identification by anyone who has heard the voice at any time;
    (6) phone calls to an assigned number plus circumstances such as self-identification;
    (7) public records;
    (8) ancient documents (no suspicious condition, found where it would likely be, at least 20 years old); and
    (9) a process or system shown to produce an accurate result.
  • Rule 902 self-authenticating items need no extrinsic evidence (the opponent may still dispute them): sealed or certified public documents, certified copies of public records, official publications, newspapers and periodicals, trade inscriptions and labels, notarized documents, commercial paper, and certified business records and electronic records under 902(11)–(14) with advance notice.
  • Chain of custody: a unique item can be identified by a witness who recognizes it; a fungible item (white powder) needs a chain from seizure to court. The chain need not be perfect, and gaps usually go to weight. Texts, posts, and emails: prove the author, not just the device, through content, timing, and context. Photos and videos: a witness testifies that the image fairly and accurately shows what the witness saw ("pictorial testimony"), or, with no eyewitness, the proponent shows the recording process is accurate ("silent witness," 901(b)(9)).
  • Ancient documents: documents with no suspicious condition, found where such records would likely be, and more than 20 years old are authenticated under 901(b)(8), with no strict chain of custody required (United States v. Stelmokas, 3d Cir. 1996). Authentication and hearsay are separate hurdles: since 2017 the 803(16) hearsay exception covers only documents prepared before Jan. 1, 1998, but 901(b)(8)'s 20-year rule is unchanged.
  • Texts and posts: ownership of the device alone does not authenticate a message; content, timing, and context can show the author under 901(b)(4) (State v. Benton, S.C. Ct. App. 2021).
  • Silent witness: when no one saw what a camera recorded, a regular user can usually lay the 901(b)(9) foundation (installation and service history, how he could tell a camera was down, an exact copy), and his lack of technical knowledge goes to weight (State v. Luke, Haw. Ct. App. 2020).
  • How much authentication: the kind and amount required depends on what the proponent claims the exhibit is. Images offered only to show what a commercially available item looks like need no testimony from the maker or the websites, and internet evidence needs no heightened standard (United States v. Oreckinto, D. Conn. 2017).

29. The Best Evidence Rule (FRE 1001–1008)

rule

FRE 1002: an original writing, recording, or photograph is required in order to prove its content unless these rules or a federal statute provides otherwise. FRE 1004: other evidence of content is admissible if
(a) all the originals are lost or destroyed, and not by the proponent acting in bad faith;
(b) an original cannot be obtained by any available judicial process;
(c) the party against whom the original would be offered had control of it, was put on notice that it would be a subject of proof, and fails to produce it; or
(d) it is not closely related to a controlling issue.

  • The rule applies only to writings, recordings, and photographs, and only when a party proves their content: when the writing itself is at issue (libel, copyright, the terms of a contract or will) or when the event is proved through the recording (the bank tape after the eyewitnesses have died). A teller may testify from memory about a robbery even though a tape exists. Objects (the knife in the robber's sock) are not covered.
  • "Original" is broad: any counterpart intended to have the same effect, any accurate printout of electronically stored information, and a photo's negative or a print from it. A duplicate made by an accurate mechanical or electronic process (not by hand) is admissible like the original unless there is a genuine question about the original's authenticity or admitting the duplicate would be unfair (1003). Other routes: certified copies of public records (1005); summaries (1006, below); the opponent's own testimony or written statement about the content (1007). Under 1008 the court decides the 1004 conditions, but the jury decides whether a writing ever existed, which is the original, and whether other evidence of content is accurate.
  • Rule 1006 (as amended 2024): the court may admit as evidence a summary, chart, or calculation offered to prove the content of voluminous admissible writings, recordings, or photographs that cannot be conveniently examined in court, whether or not they have been introduced. It is substantive evidence, and the court may not tell the jury otherwise (ACN). The proponent must make the underlying originals or duplicates available at a reasonable time and place, and the summary must pass 403. Rule 107 (2024): a chart that only helps the jury follow other evidence is an illustrative aid: allowed if its utility is not substantially outweighed by the 403 dangers, not evidence, and kept out of the jury room unless all parties consent or the court for good cause orders otherwise.
  • Bad-faith loss: drawings are "writings" under 1001, so when their content is at issue (a copyright claim) the originals are required, and if they were lost in bad faith, 1004(a) does not excuse their absence; reconstructions are excluded (Seiler v. Lucasfilm, 9th Cir. 1986). The rule guards against fraud and faulty memory where a work's precise content decides the case.
  • Testimony about an event that was also recorded is not proof of content: a witness may testify from memory to what was said under oath without the transcript (Meyers). But testimony about what a GPS device's stored data showed is proof of a recording's content and requires the original or a 1004 excuse (United States v. Bennett).
tip

Authentication and best evidence steps:
(1) what does the proponent claim the exhibit is?
(2) which 901(b) route, or is it self-authenticating under 902?
(3) is the party proving the content of a writing, recording, or photograph? If so, an original or duplicate, or a 1004 excuse, or 1005–1007;
(4) 1008 allocates disputes;
(5) then hearsay and 403.

Part XI — Privileges And The Lawyer-Client Privilege (Weeks 13–14 Reading)

30. Rule 501 and Recognizing Privileges

rule

FRE 501: the common law, as interpreted by United States courts in the light of reason and experience, governs a claim of privilege unless the Constitution, a federal statute, or Supreme Court rules provide otherwise. But in a civil case, state law governs privilege regarding a claim or defense for which state law supplies the rule of decision.

  • Congress replaced the 13 proposed privilege rules (nine specific privileges) with 501. The proposed rules (503 lawyer-client, 504 psychotherapist, 505 spousal, 506 clergy, 511 waiver) were never enacted but remain respected statements of the common law. Privileges exclude reliable evidence to serve outside goals, so they are strictly construed. There is no federal physician-patient privilege.
  • Proposed FRE 506 and 511: 506 would let a person refuse to disclose, and prevent a clergy member from disclosing, a confidential communication made to the clergy member as a spiritual adviser. 511 provides that a holder waives a privilege by voluntarily disclosing, or consenting to disclosure of, any significant part of the matter, unless that disclosure is itself privileged.
  • Psychotherapist-patient privilege all required:
    (1) a confidential communication;
    (2) between a licensed psychotherapist, including a licensed clinical social worker, and a patient; and
    (3) made in the course of diagnosis or treatment (Jaffee v. Redmond, 1996). Recognized under 501's "reason" (effective therapy depends on confidence, mental health is a public good, and little evidence is lost because the statements would otherwise not be made) and "experience" (all 50 states and D.C.). No case-by-case balancing: "An uncertain privilege ... is little better than no privilege at all." This is the template for recognizing a new privilege.
  • A defendant's constitutional need can collide with a witness's privilege. Because privileges exclude reliable evidence on purpose, reliability alone cannot defeat them; courts weigh the defendant's need against the privilege. The lawyer-client privilege usually holds; some courts make the psychotherapist privilege yield; a witness's Fifth Amendment privilege almost always prevails.

31. The Lawyer-Client Privilege

  • Common elements (Proposed Rule 503):
    (1) the privilege is the client's, and only the client may waive it;
    (2) it protects communications made to facilitate professional legal services, not a lawyer acting as a friend, lobbyist, or business agent;
    (3) only confidential communications (the client intends confidentiality, and precautions bear on reasonableness);
    (4) only communications, not the underlying facts. A client cannot refuse to state a fact because he told it to his lawyer. Prospective clients are covered even if the lawyer declines the case.
  • Professional capacity: discussing legal matters with a lawyer is not enough; the client must consult the lawyer in a professional capacity, and statements made after the lawyer clearly refuses to act are not privileged. Whether a consultation is professional is a question of fact, and the party claiming the privilege bears the burden (People v. Gionis, Cal. 1995).
rule

FRE 502(b): a disclosure in a federal proceeding does not waive the privilege if
(1) it is inadvertent;
(2) the holder took reasonable steps to prevent disclosure; and
(3) the holder promptly took reasonable steps to rectify the error. 502(a): an intentional waiver extends to undisclosed material only if it concerns the same subject matter and in fairness ought to be considered together. 502(d): a federal court order that disclosure is not a waiver binds all other federal and state proceedings. 502(c): a disclosure in a state proceeding is no waiver in a federal proceeding if it would not be one under 502 or under the law of the state where it occurred. 502(e): an agreement on the effect of disclosure binds only the parties unless it is put in a court order. 502(f): the rule applies in state proceedings and even if state law provides the rule of decision. 502(g): it covers the attorney-client privilege and work-product protection.

  • Proving 502(b): the holder bears the burden on each element. Unsworn assertions that a paralegal reviewed the documents did not show reasonable steps to prevent disclosure, and one letter followed by years of silence was not prompt, reasonable rectification (Williams v. District of Columbia, D.D.C. 2011).
  • Fees and identity: neither the fee arrangement nor the client's identity is ordinarily privileged; they are facts, not confidential communications. The narrow exception applies only when revealing them would itself disclose a confidential communication (the Baird case, where naming clients would tie them to admissions about unpaid taxes).

32. Duration, Crime-Fraud, Government Lawyers, and Self-Incrimination

  • Duration: the privilege survives the client's death and applies the same way in criminal and civil cases. Clients care about reputation, civil liability, and harm to family after death; the testamentary exception carries out the client's wishes rather than showing that the privilege ends; and posthumous balancing would add the uncertainty Jaffee rejected (Swidler & Berlin v. United States, 1998).
  • Crime-fraud exception: the privilege does not protect communications seeking advice for future wrongdoing; past crimes stay protected. A court may review allegedly privileged communications in camera to decide whether the exception applies, but first requires "a showing of a factual basis adequate to support a good faith belief by a reasonable person" that review may reveal evidence establishing it, a lower showing than is needed to defeat the privilege. The showing may rest on any lawfully obtained evidence not adjudicated privileged (United States v. Zolin, 1989).
  • Government lawyers (syllabus topic; Rowland itself is a syllabus skip): In re Grand Jury Investigation [Rowland], 399 F.3d 527 (2d Cir. 2005), upheld the Connecticut governor's office's privilege for communications with its official counsel against a federal grand jury subpoena, refusing to abrogate it because the investigation was criminal. The court acknowledged conflict with Ryan (7th Cir.) and tension with the Eighth and D.C. Circuits (Lindsey). Problem 12.9 asks who controls the privilege if a later governor waives over the ex-governor's objection. Rowland reserved the question; compare Kitzhaber (9th Cir. 2016).
  • Self-incrimination (syllabus topic; pp. 1041–59 are a syllabus skip): Fisher v. United States, 425 U.S. 391 (1976), and United States v. Doe, 465 U.S. 605 (1984).
    (1) The lawyer-client privilege protects communications, not underlying facts or preexisting documents just handed to a lawyer.
    (2) The client's Fifth Amendment privilege does not protect documents in the lawyer's hands, because the client is not the one compelled.
    (3) If the documents would have been protected in the client's hands and were given to the lawyer in confidence to get legal advice, the lawyer-client privilege protects them in the lawyer's hands.
    (4) The Fifth Amendment protects only against compelled, testimonial self-incrimination.
tip

Privilege steps:
(1) which law governs under 501 (state law for state-law claims in civil cases);
(2) which privilege, and who holds it;
(3) a communication, not a fact, confidential, made to obtain professional services;
(4) waiver: voluntary disclosure by the holder, 502(b) for inadvertence, 502(a) for scope, 502(d) orders;
(5) exceptions: crime-fraud (Zolin) and testamentary; death does not end it (Swidler);
(6) identity and fees;
(7) no ad hoc balancing (Jaffee).

Part XII — Familial Privileges (Week 15 Reading)

33. The Spousal Testimonial Privilege

  • The witness-spouse holds it: only the witness-spouse may refuse to testify adversely, and that spouse may be neither compelled to testify nor foreclosed from testifying, so the defendant cannot block a willing spouse. Testifying after immunity and promises of leniency is not involuntary (Trammel v. United States, 1980, modifying Hawkins). When one spouse is willing to testify, little marital harmony is left to preserve.
  • Scope all required:
    (1) a valid marriage when the privilege is claimed;
    (2) in federal court only in criminal cases (including the grand jury) in which a spouse is the defendant; and
    (3) only adverse testimony, including acts observed. Most courts deny it when one spouse is charged with a crime against the other or a child of either. States vary: most follow Trammel, a few let the defendant bar the testimony (Washington, which let Michael Crawford keep his wife off the stand).
  • Proposed FRE 505 (syllabus) would have codified Hawkins by giving the privilege to the defendant-spouse, which Trammel rejected. It excepted crimes against the spouse or a child of either (505(c)(1)), limited the privilege to events during the marriage (505(c)(2)), and recognized no privilege for marital confidences, which survives anyway at federal common law.

34. The Marital Confidences Privilege

  • Elements:
    (1) a communication (not observations of the other spouse's conduct);
    (2) made in confidence (presumed, but third parties or monitored systems defeat it);
    (3) during a valid marriage. Either spouse may refuse to disclose and may prevent the other from disclosing. It survives divorce, applies in civil and criminal cases, and applies even if neither spouse is a party. Exceptions: communications about joint ongoing or future crimes, and crimes against the spouse or a child of either.
  • Communications, not facts: confidential conversations during the marriage stay privileged despite a later divorce or the presence of infant children, and there is no general exception for financial matters. The joint-crime exception requires the holder's wrongful complicity (an extortion victim is not a participant), and telling a third party the underlying facts does not waive the privilege for the conversations (United States v. Rakes, 1st Cir. 1998).

35. A Parent-Child Privilege?

  • No federal parent-child privilege: almost every court rejects it, only four states protect such communications, it did not exist at common law, a testimonial privilege is not essential to the relationship, and children are unlikely to know the law; any such privilege should come from Congress (In re Grand Jury Proceedings, 3d Cir. 1997). Without a consensus in "experience," there is no new privilege (the counterexample to Jaffee).
tip

Familial privilege steps:
(1) a valid marriage (when the privilege is claimed for the testimonial privilege; when the communication was made for confidences);
(2) testimonial privilege: a criminal case, a spouse is the defendant, adverse testimony, and the witness-spouse decides;
(3) confidences: a confidential communication during the marriage, held by both spouses, surviving divorce; exceptions for joint crimes (complicity required: Rakes) and crimes against family members;
(4) no federal parent-child privilege.
